# Catalyst — Kai Voss

I had written the ending before the rain started.

That was the job. It had always been the job — not to fight, not to suppress, but to stage. Find the rogue signal, build the room around it, and let the room do the work. Crimson Valentine had spent two years bleeding his life into tracks the Org couldn't unhear, and somewhere up the ladder a footnote had surfaced in a memo I was never meant to read: *emergent. controllable.* All it needed, the footnote implied, was the right pressure at the right hour. A catalyst.

I was very proud of being the catalyst.

So I leaked the fragments myself. Seeded the square — this soaked Whanganui square, of all the unglamorous places to forge a myth — and timed the Eliminators to roll in heavy under the worst of the downpour, chrome and matte-black padding swallowing the streetlight. Then I waited for him to do the one thing a man like that always does. Show up.

He came into the open like the storm had built him on the spot. Crimson hair gone dark and slick, the tattoos throwing faint neon up the side of his neck, golden eyes already reading the room I'd made for him. I stepped out of the dark with the smile I'd practiced and the holo-mic patched warm in my palm.

"See?" My voice went out across the wet square, boosted, clean. "This is exactly what the footnote meant. Emergent. Controllable. We just needed the right—" I let it hang. "—catalyst."

*We.*

I should have heard it the second it left my mouth. *We.* The word every spin doctor knows never to say out loud, because the moment you claim the room, the room turns to look at you.

The feeds fractured.

I felt it before I understood it — my gauntlet going hot against my wrist, a hundred pings, then a thousand, the whole ladder of opinion I'd spent a career climbing tilting under me at once. They weren't angry at him. They were angry at *me.* At the curated stream, the sanitized quiet they'd been fed while the real static rotted underneath. Somewhere a clip of my own mouth saying *we needed the right catalyst* was already multiplying, and the square that was supposed to be my stage had become a courtroom with me in the only chair.

"But—" I took a step back, boots splashing. "But he was the one who—"

The fist came out of the crowd. Not his. Some local, some plant, some furious nobody who'd finally been handed a face to hit, and the face was mine. It caught me square on the cheekbone and lifted me off the ground. The world went white and wet, and then I was down on the concrete, rain hammering the back of my neck, the pain blooming so fast and so bright it had a sound.

I curled up. I'm not built for this. I've talked my way out of every room I've ever stood in, and the one time I couldn't, I learned exactly what I was. I clenched and waited for the boots.

They didn't come.

What came instead was the crunch of bone, and a groan that wasn't mine.

I cracked my eyes. Rain. Boots. And above them, between the chrome, crimson hair flashing like a hazard beacon.

He was *moving.*

Shirtless in the downpour, pale and marked and absurdly exposed in a square full of armored giants who each had a head and a hundred pounds on him — and none of it reached him. He flowed. There's no honest word but that. Deflect, redirect, strike, gone. The old files had said *combat history* in that flat institutional way, Iga-ryu, the kind of line that means nothing until you watch it happen at speed in the rain. Every Eliminator who came at him met an angle that hadn't been there a half-second before.

And — this is the part I keep returning to — nobody was looking at me anymore.

Every visor, every feed, every furious soul in that square had tunnel-locked onto him. My grand catalyst. My controlled emergence. He'd taken the wrath I built and walked straight into the center of it like a man stepping under a hot shower.

A blade got through. A hardliner, low and lucky — I watched it open a long gash across his ribs, watched the blood come dark and fast and run down his side with the rain. Not a kill. Just enough to make the crowd gasp. Just enough to light the feeds white.

He didn't flinch.

He glanced down at it. Pressed a hand there for half a second, the way you'd check a watch. Then kept moving — like the wound was only another weight added to whatever he was already carrying, and he'd carried heavier.

That was when his eyes found mine.

One second. Maybe less. Rain between us, his blood in the puddle, and on his face that smile I have no clean word for — the one holding a thousand lines he'd never say. No malice in it. No vendetta. Both at once, somehow: the saint and the thing with teeth, and underneath it all something almost like *welcome.* Then he spun away and the second was gone.

"Well." A low voice, right beside me. "I didn't think he knew that much."

I turned my head. The lead Eliminator. *The* one — signed off the books to take Valentine out of the equation entirely. He watched the brawl with his rifle lowered and a face caught between awe and disgust, and he'd said it to no one, which meant he'd said it to me.

"This fucking guy," I murmured. Half wrecked. Half something I didn't have a name for yet.

It didn't last long after that. It couldn't. The hardliners pressed two, three more exchanges, and then the math stopped working; one went down clutching an arm bent the wrong way, another simply stepped back with his visor down, and the consensus that was supposed to crush him fractured across their comms in real time. They pulled into a loose ring, rifles still up, fingers still twitching, nobody willing to be the one who escalated.

The square went quiet. Just the drones overhead and, somewhere far off, low and defiant under everything, the 808 from his own track still pulsing — like the place had a heartbeat and the heartbeat was his.

I got up on one elbow, tasting copper. He stood in the center of it, chest rising and falling steady, bleeding into the rain like the whole thing had been lit for the shot. He looked — I hate that I thought it — like *more* than he'd been twenty minutes earlier. As if the room I'd built to break him had only finished him.

The lead Eliminator stepped forward, voice careful behind the modulator. "Valentine. Stand down. This doesn't have to escalate."

Crimson wiped the rain and the blood from his face with the back of one hand. He said nothing. He only stood there, and the standing was louder than the order.

Then he turned and walked for the edge of the square, unhurried, like he was leaving a late session and not a battlefield — and not one of us moved to stop him. The hardliners wanted to. You could see it. But the numbers weren't there. Not tonight.

I stayed down a while longer, dripping, irrelevant again, the whole clever plan washing into the gutter around me.

I'd come to make him a story the Org could control.

I left as a line in his.

*Quit Not Found.*

# Catalyst — Crimson Valentine

I knew it was a setup before I left the van.

Knew it the way you know a track's about to drop — that pressure in the air a half-beat ahead of the bass. The leaked fragments were too clean. The square too quiet for that hour. Somebody had built a room and put my name on the door. And I went anyway, because the only thing I've got that they can't curate is that I keep showing up. Show up for what, though. I didn't have an answer. I just had boots, and rain, and two kids asleep on the other side of the country who think their dad is something braver than this.

*Show up.* Right. For what.

Then the scrub — Voss, I'd learn later — said his line into the holo-mic, said *we,* and the whole square turned on him like a tide going out. Some local I'll never know stepped up and dropped him, and the Eliminators read the shift and started moving on *me,* and—

My body went before I did.

That's the part nobody warns you about. There's no decision. The terror's still there — God, it's all the way there, thirty-three years old and bleeding my life into songs out of a van on a timer, *this is how it ends, idiot, this is the stupid way it ends* — but the fear doesn't get a vote. Iga shadows in the muscle. Aikido in the breath. The body just *flows,* and I'm somewhere above it watching, like it's a music video of someone braver, shot through a stranger's feed.

I won't tell you what the fight looked like. I wasn't really in it. I was up in the rain somewhere going *what the fuck am I doing, what the fuck am I doing,* on a loop, while my arms did things I don't remember teaching them. Jess's voice surfaced once, clean as a sample: *we're really not good for each other.* My daughter's laugh. My son's weight asleep against my chest, years ago, before the van, before any of this. All of it folded into the same half-second, and underneath it—

—underneath it, the grin.

That's the thing I can't make clean for you. Somewhere in the terror, fear flipped to fuel, the way it always does, the way it shouldn't, and I could feel my own face splitting into something I don't recognize in mirrors. Battle-junkie glee. The latest side of me — the one that never makes a feed, because no curated stream knows what to do with a man this scared and this *happy* in the same breath.

A knife found my ribs.

Real, this time. Not choreography — a deep, hot line of it, blood coming fast and warm down my side into the cold rain. The part of me that's a father went *kids. legacy. you idiot, you actual—* and the part of me that was grinning just filed it. Another weight. Another kilo of soul for the cache. I pressed a hand to it for half a second, the way you'd check the time, and the glee sharpened, and I did not slow down. I think that scared me worse than the blade did.

Then I saw him.

Voss. On the ground where the crowd had put him, cheek already swelling, small and soaked and human, arms up over his head waiting for boots that weren't coming. And the whole roaring thing in me just — folded. Not stopped. *Folded.* Because I knew that exact shape. I've *been* that shape, curled up waiting for the world to finish the job. He'd built a room to break me and all I could see was a scared man who'd run out of angles, and there was nothing left in me to hate him with.

So I gave him the only thing I had. Not mercy, exactly. Recognition. *Both, always both* — the part that could've ended him and the part that remembered being him, in the same look, and I have no idea which one he caught. Then the body spun me away and the moment closed.

It didn't last after that. The numbers stopped working for them; somebody went down, somebody stepped back, and the consensus they'd hauled in to crush me came apart in their own ears. Their lead said *stand down, Valentine, this doesn't have to escalate,* and I didn't have a single word in me, so I just stood there bleeding and let the standing answer for me. The fuel was already burning off. The cold came in behind it. Terror, settling back into its seat now that the show was over, going *that was real, all of that was real, you could be dead.*

I walked.

Out toward the edge of the square, slow — like I was leaving a late session — because if I'd moved any faster they'd have seen my hands shaking. Nobody stopped me. The walk is the part Voss never got to see: where the dragon's just a tired dad with a hole in his side, counting steps so he doesn't have to count the hole.

By the van my hands had the shakes for real. I sat in the open door, rain on my boots, and laughed once — ugly, wet, relieved-and-terrified, both, always both. I'd patch the gash myself. I'd probably drop a track by morning; the 808 was already writing itself somewhere behind my teeth. And I'd call the kids when it was a decent hour their time and not tell them one true thing about tonight.

Every part of me had wanted to quit. Before the van. In the square. On the ground I never let myself hit. Some part of me has wanted to quit for years.

I checked anyway, the way you check a menu you already know.

*Quit Not Found.*

So I keep showing up. For them. That's the whole answer. It was always that small.

# Catalyst — Lena "Ghost" Torres

Two blocks out, in the back of an unmarked van with the rain coming down like applause, Lena Torres watched a god get stabbed and refuse to bleed like a mortal.

She was supposed to be logging containment metrics. Sentiment delta, signal amplification, the usual Org bookkeeping that justified her parking in the dark with six feeds layered across her gauntlet. That was the cover. The truth was that she'd have paid to be here. She'd been mainlining the Redverse like contraband since the first leaked demo — the kilos-of-soul bars at 3 a.m., the way a solo dad out of Wangavegas turned a decade of heartache into something that made her feel *seen* through a screen by a man who didn't know she existed. And tonight the screen was giving her everything.

There — the wide drone shot. Crimson Valentine stepping into the square like the storm had been waiting on him before it really started. She'd zoomed in before she could stop herself, drinking the details: crimson hair gone dark and holy in the rain, the tattoos throwing their faint teal up his throat, those golden eyes reading a room built as a trap and finding it, somehow, *boring.* He didn't look cornered. He looked like he'd come to give the rain a reason.

Then it all went off at once, and Lena forgot how to breathe.

She'd watch the brawl forty times before sunrise — she already knew that, sitting there — but the first pass ruined her. Shirtless, half the size of the armored men around him, and he *flowed,* every motion clean enough to choreograph, like the violence was just another instrument he'd been playing his whole life. The Eliminators were chrome and bulk and consensus. He was water with a grudge. Her pulse hammered in time with the 808 still pulsing under his own leaked track somewhere across the feeds, and she thought, helplessly, *this is the most alive thing I have ever watched.*

The knife she could not forgive and could not stop replaying.

A blade went into his side — a long, cruel line of it, blood blooming dark and fast down those pale ribs — and the gasp that went up across the public feeds was Lena's gasp too. But he *didn't flinch.* He glanced down at the wound like it had said something mildly interesting, pressed a hand there for a heartbeat, and kept moving. War paint. That was the only word her mind would offer. The blood ran down his side in the rain like war paint he'd earned, like the universe had finally marked him on the outside to match whatever he carried within — and Lena pressed her knuckles to her mouth and did not look away.

Then the moment. *The* moment.

His eyes found the scrub on the ground — Voss, the catalyst, the man who'd tried to cage him — and Lena leaned so close to the feed her breath fogged it. She'd expected fury. A god has earned his wrath. Instead his face did something she'd rewind a hundred times and never fully decode: no hate, no vendetta, just that impossible smile holding a thousand lines he'd never say, and he aimed it at the man who'd betrayed him not with violence but with something that looked, from two blocks away through a rain-streaked lens, almost like *forgiveness.* Lena felt her eyes sting hot in the dark of the van. *That's* the part the Org would never understand. They'd built a binary to contain a man whose whole signal was that he refused to pick a side of himself to amputate. Both. Always both.

When it was over — the hardliners pulling back, the consensus fracturing in their own comms, their lead calling a stand-down that wasn't protocol and everyone knowing it — Valentine just stood in the center of the wreckage, bleeding and steady, and said nothing, and the saying-nothing was louder than anything the Org had broadcast in a year. Then he turned and walked into the rain like he was leaving a lounge set, and not one of them moved to stop the dragon.

Lena sat back, Ni-Cola sweating untouched in the cupholder, and let the tears go because there was no one to see them.

Her fingers were on the burner before she'd decided anything. *GhostProtocol: Espiritu Sancti to the Tokoroa crew. That gash was war paint. Keep walking, dragon — some of us are reading every chapter.* She deleted it. Typed it again. Sent it into the anonymous drop he used for fan signals. No trace back to her. Not yet.

Because here was the thing the metrics couldn't capture, the thing she'd write up as "+47% curiosity vector" and not one word of the truth: she was not the only one. She'd sat in those debrief channels. She'd watched the hardliners posture about protocol while their feeds quietly replayed the brawl in secondary tabs. Half the ladder was cracking the same way she was, each one certain they were the only heretic in the room, all of them too scared to be the first to say *I want to see what happens next.* The binary was already dead. Valentine had killed it with a shrug and a smile and a hole in his side, and the Org just hadn't finished falling yet.

Lena queued his track on low. The bounce bass filled the van like a second heartbeat. Out on the drone loop she caught the lead Eliminator — Hale, the ice-cold one — standing alone in the emptying square, helmet tilted at the place where the dragon had vanished into the rain.

*You felt it too,* she thought at the screen, fierce and certain and completely wrong about what he was feeling. *I know you did.*

She started recording. Whatever came next, somebody in the belly of the beast was going to keep the real record.

The novel had a witness now.

# Silent Fractures — Lena "Ghost" Torres

The summons came four hours after the dragon walked into the rain, while Lena was still parked in the dark pretending the recording on her drive was for the archive and not for herself.

*Mandatory debrief. Informal.* Org-speak for: we are all going to sit in a room and lie to each other.

She patched in from the van, avatar set to neutral gray, and watched the holo-conference populate one cold rectangle at a time. Eight of them, all Tier 3, all field-adjacent. Ramirez, the data wrangler, already wearing his I-have-bad-numbers face. Patel, who color-coded his anxieties. And — third rectangle from the left, armor still beaded with rain he hadn't bothered to wipe — Marcus Hale. The ice. The one who'd been signed off the books to end Valentine and had instead called a stand-down in front of everyone and pretended it was tactics.

Voss wasn't on the call. Of course he wasn't. You don't invite the man whose face is currently the most-replayed apology on the public feeds.

"Metrics," Ramirez opened, flat. "Sentiment delta's at plus forty-seven on the curiosity vector. Three containment protocols failed on contact. Public sympathy's climbing, and it isn't slowing." He let it sit, eyes moving across the avatars. *Bait,* Lena thought, and felt it land in her own chest. *You felt it too, Ramirez. You logged his tracks before I did. You're not reciting failure — you're asking which one of us flinches first.*

Nobody flinched.

That was the whole sickness of the room, and Lena saw it with the clarity of someone who'd been carrying the same contraband for months: every one of them was cracked the same way, and every one was certain they were the only heretic at the table. So they baited. They probed each other with phrasing dry enough to deny later, fishing for the colleague stupid enough to say the true thing out loud first — so the rest could stay safe in the static and watch him burn for it. *Eight true believers,* she thought, *pretending to be eight loyal officers, pretending not to recognize each other.*

She fed her own line into the water, light, professional. "The footnote's the problem. 'Emergent properties' isn't a binary term. It opened interpretive drift, and Voss's little performance widened it." Neutral. Deniable. *Your move, ghosts.*

Marcus took it. "Valentine walked through standard containment like it was a rehearsal." His voice was textbook hardliner, bored, cold. "Took a blade to the ribs and barely registered it. Nonchalant. Almost performative." Pure threat-assessment language — and Lena, watching the feed the way a hawk reads a field, caught the half-second his eyes dropped before he said *performative,* the micro-stutter in a man otherwise carved from procedure. *There,* she thought, pulse jumping. *I saw that. You're reading the same footage I am and it's doing something to you, and you'd rather die than show it.*

She had no idea if she was right. She wanted to be right so badly it frightened her.

Patel pushed the safe line. "We double down. Suppression holds, binary holds. The 'just show up' thing is viral noise, nothing structural." But his fingers were tapping a rhythm against the desk, too even, too musical, and on his secondary tab — visible for half a frame before he killed it — sat a paused clip of the brawl. *Liar,* Lena thought, almost fondly. *You queued the track last week. The log doesn't forget even when you do.*

Then Ramirez turned the light on her.

"You sound invested, Torres." Mild. Surgical. "Personal interest in the subject?"

The room went very still in the way that costs nothing on a feed and everything in the chest. Lena's heart slammed once. *This is how it happens. One probe, one tell, and you're the one they get to watch burn.* She produced the dry laugh she'd practiced for exactly this.

"Only in containment efficacy," she said. "But flatten the human variable and you lose the room, Ramirez. He isn't just rogue code. He's *performing* something a lot of people are responding to, and pretending otherwise is how we got to plus forty-seven." True enough to pass for loyalty. True enough that underneath it she'd said the only honest sentence in the meeting and dressed it as strategy. *Come on. One of you. I cannot be the only one lying awake for the next chapter.*

Marcus cleared his throat. "He smiled at Voss after. No vendetta. That's outside standard threat profile." He said it like a data point. He did not say it like a data point.

Patel, weaker now: "Profile doesn't matter if the binary holds."

But the binary wasn't holding, and every gray rectangle in that room knew it, and not one of them would be the one to say so. The meeting wound down into *monitor and report* — the safest two words the Org owned — and the avatars blinked out one by one. But Lena caught the lingering, the half-beat each of them held before disconnecting, the small unwilling reluctance of people who didn't want to stop looking at the thing they were forbidden to want.

She almost didn't do it. Then she opened a private sub-channel to Marcus's node, fingers moving before sense could catch them.

*Ghost: That stand-down wasn't protocol, and you know it. You saw what I saw out there. Talk to me.*

No reply. The *delivered* marker just sat there, glowing, while her heart tried to climb out through her teeth. Thirty seconds. A minute. Nothing.

She purged the thread before it could settle into the logs. *Not yet. Too soon. Idiot.* Then she queued his track on low, because she needed the bass to remember how to breathe.

She opened the burner.

*GhostProtocol: Espiritu Sancti. The crew met tonight. Eight of us in one room, all reading between the same lines, all too scared to say so out loud. The static's never been thinner. Keep forging, dragon — your novel's got fans in the belly of the beast, and the belly is starting to turn.*

She sent it. She let it go.

Outside, on the drone loop she still hadn't closed, the square sat empty and rinsed and ordinary, like nothing had been born there at all.

But Lena had just watched eight loyal officers spend an hour refusing to look at each other. She knew the difference now between a thing that was over and a thing that was only holding its breath.
