# Sho-Zen Juan: The Sight and the Dragon

### I. The Boy Who Saw the Weather Beneath the Skin

In the grey-rot suburbs, where the sky sat low and the gardens were kept too carefully for what they concealed, there was a boy named Sho-Zen Juan whose left eye did not see as other eyes saw.

His right eye took in the world the way any child's did — the cracked pavement, the overcast noon, the neighbor's dog straining its chain. But his left eye read something underneath all of that. It read the *weather beneath the skin* — the true shape of a person moving just beneath the shape they showed.

He did not choose this. It was simply how he had been made, the way some children are born left-handed or perfect-pitched, and for the first five years of his life he assumed everyone carried this second sight and simply didn't speak of it, the way no one spoke of breathing.

What the left eye showed him had its own grammar, and he learned it the way other children learned their letters.

When a grown-up's smile did not match the thing moving underneath it, the aura around them would go *thin* — translucent, stretched, the way ice goes glassy right before it gives. When someone meant him harm, or feared him, or wished he would simply stop being strange in their presence, their edges would spike and jag, sudden and sharp as broken glass catching light. And when someone was simply, quietly, without agenda, *glad he existed* — their aura moved slow and warm, like a held hand that did not need to grip to prove it was there.

It was not mind-reading. He never once heard a thought. It was something older and simpler than that — an early-warning system built into the architecture of him, letting him know the true shape of a room before a single word confirmed or denied it.

For a while, it kept him safe in ways he would not understand until long after it was gone.

---

### II. The Intercessors

Word travels strangely in small, watchful towns, and word of the boy with the strange left eye reached the church before he was old enough to understand what a church even was for.

They did not send the ordinary clergy. They sent the Intercessors — quiet, tired people, the ones the institution called on when a case did not fit the usual shape of things. Field operatives of a stranger kind of faith. Many of them had already seen enough real darkness in their working lives that they no longer needed to perform authority to feel it was theirs.

They arrived at his door expecting one of two children: the truly afflicted, or the merely convenient — a small stage upon which some adult's private terror could act itself out. They carried their scripts and their exhaustion in equal measure, the way any professional carries the tools of a trade they no longer quite believe fixes anything.

What they found instead was a boy who treated them like people.

He asked them real questions. Are you tired. Do you like this work. What does it feel like, to be sent for. He listened to the answers the way other children listened to bedtime stories — completely, without agenda, without trying to catch them failing to be holy enough.

It disarmed them, every time.

For the length of an afternoon, in a boy's plain living room, several tired adults in service to an old institution got to set the weight of it down. He did not perform sanctity for their benefit, and he did not need them to be anything other than what they were. In exchange, he received something almost no one in his young life had offered him: adults who saw him clearly, understood exactly what he was, and chose gentleness anyway.

They taught him, without meaning to teach him anything: that clarity and softness were never opposites. That people could act from duty instead of understanding, and that this was a fact to be navigated rather than mourned.

He taught them, without trying: that being seen completely, and stayed with anyway, was rarer than any of them had let themselves hope for.

---

### III. The Asking

The trouble with the eye was never that it lied. It was that it never learned how to be quiet.

A child who can see the true shape of a room does not, at five or seven or nine, understand that most rooms are built on a polite agreement not to say so. He would name what he saw the way he named the weather — plainly, immediately, without calculating first what it would cost him to be right. The secret someone was carrying. The fear underneath the smile. The lie thinning out an aura like ice about to give. He said these things aloud, to the people they belonged to, because he did not yet know that being seen that clearly was something most people would rather die than be handed by a child.

It made him impossible to be near.

It made his family impossible to be near, by extension — the ones who loved a boy who could not stop announcing what everyone around him had spent real effort trying to keep hidden. The peers who avoided him. The elders who watched him the way you watch something that might, at any moment, say the one thing in the room nobody had agreed to say out loud. Whatever the eye had once been for — an early warning, a kind of grace — it had become, in the mouths and eyes of everyone around him, a wound he was inflicting on purpose.

By ten, he had learned what the room wanted, even if the eye itself never did.

He went to the church elders himself. Asked them, plainly, to pray it out of him. To take the seeing away.

They obliged him.

And it worked — immediately, completely, the way a switch works. One moment the world was full of its second layer, the weather beneath the skin, spikes and warmth and the slow honest pulse of people at peace with themselves. The next moment, nothing. Just the room, as everyone else already saw it. Flat. Quiet. Blessedly, finally, *ordinary*.

He was glad, that night. He had asked for the dark and the dark had come, fast and total as a light switched off, and for the first time in years no one flinched when he walked into a room.

It would be a long time before he understood what he'd actually traded away, and longer still before the gladness curdled into something closer to grief — the particular grief of having asked, correctly, for the one thing that would have kept you safest, and getting it.

---

### IV. The Question

At thirteen, in the second baptism — the one they called the baptism of fire — he stood before the weight of something vast, and it asked him a single question, plainly, with no further explanation offered:

*"Do you want to be the Dragon, and all that it entails?"*

No warning came with it. No accounting of cost. Just the question, bare as a blade laid flat on an altar.

Once, the left eye would have shown him the true shape of that offer before a single word was spoken — the spikes, the thinness, the warmth or the absence of it beneath the surface of the ask. But the eye had been gone three years by then, and he answered the way a boy answers when he has spent his whole short life being told that certainty was the same thing as faith.

He said yes without hesitation.

He did not know yet what he had agreed to see through blind.

---

### V. The House of Morbis

The dreams changed that same season, and did not change back.

Night after night he found himself inside a house he knew too well — old, familiar, wrong in every corner, the kind of house that remembers everything that has ever happened inside its walls and holds the memory close like a grudge. And in that house waited something he came, eventually, to call **Morbis** — not a devil in the way stories tell it, not a monster with teeth and appetite, but an *uncreation*. A force whose only nature was the undoing of things that had once been whole.

Every attempt to leave the house led him to someone he loved.

And every time, Morbis wore their face. Twisted the familiar mouth into something no mouth should make. Snapped the head around, backward, laughing in a register that had no place in a human throat. Used the people he trusted like puppets on a single cruel string, because the house had learned — the way the dimming had already taught him once — that the fastest way to break someone is to make the danger wear the shape of what they love.

Each time, it caught him. Each time, it opened his chest and took the thing inside it. Each time, it threw him down into a dark that had no bottom, and he clawed upward through it for what felt like whole lifetimes, only to wake back inside the same house, facing the same door, starting the same walk toward the same waiting horror.

This happened not once, not a dozen times, but across a span uncountable — a thousand deaths, a thousand falls, a thousand wakings into the same unbroken loop.

And somewhere inside that repetition — not on any single try he could point to afterward, but somewhere in the accumulated weight of all of them together — something in him stopped flinching at the door.

---

### VI. The Wall That Was Never Real

On the cycle that finally broke, he did not find a clever trick or a hidden key. He simply walked toward the wall that had stopped him every single time before, and did not stop.

And the wall, which had never once let him through in a thousand tries, gave way — because it had never been a wall at all. It had been the shape his own fear had built and rebuilt every night, brick by obedient brick, and on the thousand-and-first attempt he simply declined, for the length of one breath, to keep laying them.

He stepped out of the house into a world gone wrong in every direction. No blue remained in the sky — only a permanent burnt orange, the color of a dusk that had forgotten how to end. The air sat heavy in the lungs. Everywhere he looked, ruin: a wasteland stripped down to its bones, hopeless in the specific, patient way of a place that had given up waiting to be rebuilt.

He walked through it anyway, because a thousand deaths teaches a body strange kinds of courage, and eventually he came upon a temple, and inside the temple a great bloated shape — slow, grotesque, wearing the posture of some rotted chancellor — that he understood without being told he must never let touch him.

But he had survived a thousand deaths already. Godlike, in the specific and terrible way that only comes from having nothing left to lose, he simply sidestepped it, again and again, buying himself minutes at a time to explore a world that had already tried its worst on him and failed to keep him down.

---

### VII. The Sister in the Green Cloak

It was in dodging the slow, patient horror of the temple that he first saw her — a masked figure in a green cloak, sword drawn, standing where nothing should have been standing.

He did not know her yet. He only knew that when the bloated thing finally closed the distance and panic finally caught up with the courage a thousand deaths had built in him, she moved between them without hesitation and ended the threat in a single motion, clean as punctuation.

She told him, after, that she should not have been there at all. That there were rules older than either of them that forbade her staying. Her voice carried a frustration that wasn't really about him, and beneath the frustration, a grief he recognized instantly — the particular grief of loving someone and being made to leave anyway.

Before she vanished back into whatever governed her comings and goings, something passed between them that left its mark on him permanently — a pattern, sword-shaped, that would sit on his skin for the rest of his life as proof that she had been real, whatever "real" meant inside a dream that repeated a thousand times before it let him through.

He would carry her mark, and never quite stop missing her, though he would not understand for years exactly who it was he'd been given a glimpse of.

---

### VIII. The Hollow Town and the Coin of Blood

Past the temple, he found what remained of a town, and the people in it still wore human shapes, though something essential had been scraped out from underneath. They were not cruel. They were not evil. They had simply been hollowed down to the single, animal instinct that remains when everything else has been stripped away: survive, at any cost, by any means offered.

He had companions with him by then — three presences who urged him, with real urgency, not to approach. Not to give the hollow ones anything. To keep walking and let the wasteland keep its ruined town.

He did not listen. He had never been good at listening when his own arrogance told him he could fix what nothing had been able to fix yet — least of all a boy who had just walked through a wall that wasn't supposed to open.

The currency the hollow ones wanted, it turned out, was blood, and it came from him, coin after coin after coin, and the more he gave, the hungrier they became, until *hungrier* turned to *desperate* and *desperate* turned to a crowd closing in from every side at once, the way a debt always eventually closes in on the one foolish enough to think it could be paid in full.

---

### IX. The Blue Dragon

He was going under — really under, past the point where courage or stubbornness could pull him back out — when the sky above the hollow town split open with a light too sharp and too blue for a world that had forgotten what blue looked like.

Out of that light came the dragon.

It was enormous, and it did not hesitate. It dove straight through the crowd, closed a single massive claw around him, and pulled him up out of the horde and onto its back before the hollow ones could finish closing their circle.

As they climbed — through the burnt orange, through the heavy air, up past the last thin edge of the ruined sky — the dragon spoke, and its voice was not a stranger's voice. It carried something achingly, impossibly familiar, though he would not understand why until much later.

*"I never left you."*

They broke through the last of the clouds together, and on the other side of that ruined, dying sky was a place of such overwhelming, unbroken beauty that after a thousand deaths and a wasteland and a house that never stopped waiting to kill him again, the sight of it undid something in his chest that grief and terror combined had never quite managed to reach.

He understood, though no one told him outright, exactly what — or rather *who* — had been carrying him the entire time without his knowing it.

---

### X. What the Eye Remembers

The left eye never returned to him the way it once had been. What came back, in fragments and flickers across the years that followed, was something changed — filtered now through the choice at thirteen, through the thousand cycles, through everything the house had tried and failed to make permanent.

He had asked for the dark himself, once, and been grateful for it the night it came. That was the part that took the longest to sit with honestly: that no one had taken the sight from him against his will. He had wanted the room to stop flinching, and the room had stopped flinching, exactly as promised, exactly as fast as he'd hoped. It was only later — years later, long after the elders who'd granted it had stopped thinking of him at all — that gratitude turned itself slowly inside out into grief. Not because the asking had been wrong. Because no one, least of all a boy of ten, could have understood what he was actually trading for the quiet.

The memory of the sight stayed whole, even after the sight itself did not: the memory of what it had once felt like to walk through the world with one foot planted in what people showed you, and the other planted in the truer, softer, more dangerous ground of what they actually carried. He mourned that ground the way you mourn a country you left willingly, certain at the time it was the only sane thing to do.

And when, deep inside the House of Morbis, one of the old Intercessors' faces was finally turned against him — twisted the way every other beloved face had been twisted before it — it did not land as merely one more death among the thousand. It landed as something far more precise: the dream reaching back to corrupt the last relationship he had ever had with someone in power who had chosen to see him clearly and stay anyway. It was Morbis reaching for the one wound that had never fully closed, because it was the only wound old enough, and honest enough, to still bleed.

The blue dragon's words did not erase that wound. They simply answered it, finally, from somewhere far larger than a tired field operative's afternoon kindness had ever been able to reach.

*I never left you.*

Sho-Zen Juan survived the thousand.

The Dragon survived the thousand.

And the words remain true.
