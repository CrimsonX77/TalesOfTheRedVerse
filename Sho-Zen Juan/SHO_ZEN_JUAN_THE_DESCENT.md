# SHO-ZEN JUAN: THE DESCENT
## A Testament in Cycles

---

## PART ONE: THE GREY-ROT WORLD

### Five Years Old, Somewhere in New Zealand

You are happy.

This is the most important thing to understand: you are *happy*. Genuinely, completely, unselfconsciously happy — the way five-year-olds are when the world hasn't taught them sadness yet. You don't think about being happy. You just *are* it, the way plants are green without debating the decision.

The world around you is grey-rot. Suburban. Dull. The kind of place where nothing *happens* — streets look like they were designed by someone who thought about efficiency before they thought about color. Houses sit in neat rows. Gardens are maintained but not loved. The sky is often overcast, the kind of white-grey that doesn't rain so much as *persist*.

But you don't notice the dullness. You notice *everything else*.

Your left eye sees things.

Not in the way you'll later learn to call them. Not with words like "aura" or "energy." To you, at five, it's just... *how things are*. Your left eye shows you the world with extra information — pulse and rhythm and color that moves in time with breathing. Every person is surrounded by something that shifts and breathes and *tells you things*.

Martha, the neighbor, has an aura that spikes sharp and jagged like broken glass when you talk to her. You think this is normal. You assume everyone's aura reacts like that.

Stan, the reverend's nephew, has a copper-red pulse that beats in time with his jaw clench. When he's pretending to be nice, the color gets thin and flat. You notice. You don't have words for it yet.

Your mother's aura is warm — softer colors, less violent. But it spikes too, sometimes. You learn to recognize the pattern that means she's worried about you.

At five years old, you think all of this is *normal*.

You mention it sometimes, the way five-year-olds mention anything — casual, assuming everyone knows, genuinely confused when they react weird.

"Martha's aura got all spiky when I said hi."

"My left eye sees the colors around people."

"Stan's doing the thin-red thing again."

And the adults around you *freeze*.

It's subtle at first. A pause. A look exchanged. The kind of adult-communication that happens over your head. But you notice it — it's a different pattern in their auras than the normal ones. This pattern means *concern*. Or *confusion*. Or something that makes them uncomfortable in ways you don't have the framework to understand yet.

But you're happy, so you don't worry about it.

---

### The Church (Age 5-7)

"The boy sees things," they tell your mother. Not mean. Just... factual. Concerned. The way people talk about a problem that needs solving.

Your mother is worried. You can see it in her aura — that sharp spike that means fear mixed with love. She loves you. She's also scared of what you can do.

The church becomes a regular thing. Not as a family. As a *case*.

You don't understand this at first. Church is fine. Church is where people sing and sit still and the reverend talks about God in ways that feel important even if the words don't make complete sense. You like church. You like the singing especially.

But then you start noticing: the reverend pulls your mother aside. There are *conversations* that happen when you're not supposed to be listening. Martha — spiky-aura Martha — crosses herself when you walk past. Stan looks indignant, like you're personally offending him by existing.

And Reverend John...

Reverend John is *performing*. You can see it clearly in his aura — all pomp and authority-flex, the colors doing that theatrical thing where they get bright and showy because there's an *audience*. He's not actually concerned about you. He's concerned about looking concerned about you. In front of other people. Where they can see how authoritative and helpful he is.

You're seven years old and you already understand performative spirituality.

But you're still happy. You're still unbothered. The mocking doesn't register as cruelty — it just registers as *information*. Background noise. You assume this is how people are.

The pearl-clutching intensifies. "The boy needs help." "This is dangerous." "We must intervene."

And then the intercessors arrive.

---

### The Intercessors (Age 7-10)

They are *different*.

Dark. Broody. The kind of people who look like they've seen things that made them quiet. Not quiet-afraid. Quiet-*knowing*. They move like people who've done hard work and don't need anyone to applaud them for it.

When they're first assigned to you — "the German kid with the aura problem" — they look exhausted. Like this is another spiritual emergency in a long line of spiritual emergencies. Just another check-the-box exorcism.

They're expecting a problem child. A demon case. Something dangerous that needs removing.

What they find instead is a seven-year-old who just wants them to be happy.

And that breaks them in a completely different way.

Because you ask them questions. Real questions. You listen to their answers. You don't have an agenda. You're not trying to perform spirituality at them or test them or catch them in some theological contradiction. You just... want to understand them.

For the first time in a long time, they get to exist around someone who doesn't have an institutional weight pressing down on the interaction. Someone who's not side-eying them or questioning their methods or deciding whether they're *real* enough to be doing this work.

You are genuinely interested in *them*.

They pretend to be annoyed. "Another case. Another spiritual dysfunction." But they're not annoyed. They're *relieved*. Finally, someone to talk to without the performance. Someone who actually listens.

And in that space — that small, protected space between a seven-year-old and the people the church sent to exorcise him — something real happens.

They teach you things. Not about theology. About *clarity*. About seeing past the performance. About understanding that sometimes people do things because they've been asked to do them, not because they understand why.

You teach them — without meaning to — that gentleness is not weakness. That someone can see you completely and still choose to stay.

The ten lost a lot when they lost you.

Around age ten, something shifts.

It's gradual at first. Your left eye — the one that sees the colors and pulses and tells you things about people — starts to fade. The colors get dimmer. The rhythms get harder to read. You're losing resolution, like someone slowly turning down the brightness on a screen.

You don't panic. You're still happy. Still unbothered. But you do mention it.

"My eye's getting fuzzy," you tell the adults.

And you watch their auras *relax*.

The church's concern starts to dissipate. The intercessors' protective urgency softens. Martha stops crossing herself. Stan stops looking indignant. Reverend John's performative aura gets less bright because he doesn't need to perform anymore — the problem is solving itself.

By the time you're fully ten years old, the ability is gone.

The early warning system — the thing that let you *know* people before you had to deal with them, the thing that let you see patterns in their behavior before they acted, the thing that kept you safe in ways you didn't even understand yet — is *gone*.

You lost an entire dimension of perception and didn't understand what you'd lost until years later, when you'd need it and it wouldn't be there.

But right now, at ten, you're still happy. You're still unbothered. You're just a kid who used to see colors and doesn't anymore. No big deal.

(This will cost you everything.)

---

### The Second Baptism (Age 13)

It's a regular Sunday.

Nothing special. Nothing *unusual*. Just another service in a long line of services. You sit with your mother. You stand when you're supposed to stand. You sing the hymns — you still love the singing. The words wash over you without much weight. You're thirteen. Spiritual stuff is fine but not exactly *exciting*.

You close your eyes to pray. Standard protocol. Head bowed. Eyes closed. Just you and whatever God is, in whatever way God is.

And then the world *stops*.

Not gradually. Not like a fade or a dissolve. Like someone *cut* reality. Like a hard edit in a film. One moment you're in church. The next moment you're *not*.

The world goes blurry — not your vision, but *reality itself*. The sounds fade into a low roar. The physical sensation of sitting in a pew just... ceases. You don't feel the bench anymore. You don't feel anything.

And when the blur clears, you're *somewhere else*.

It's not a place with geography. It's not a location you can describe in spatial terms. It's more like an *idea* of a place. The Architect's study from The Matrix, but also the presence of Luffy from One Piece — that impossible weight of someone who exists so *fully* that reality bends around them just to accommodate their being.

There is a presence here.

It doesn't have a face. It doesn't have a form. It's just... *there*, the way gravity is there, the way time is there. It's older than you. Much older. Older than everything.

And it speaks.

The words don't come through your ears. They come *through* you. You don't hear them so much as *become* them, the way you become aware of your own heartbeat when someone points it out.

"Do you want to be the Dragon, and all that it entails?"

That's it. That's the entire question. No explanation. No softening. No description of what the Dragon is or what "all that it entails" means. No warnings. No details. Just the question, hanging in the immensity of this presence, waiting for an answer.

You're thirteen years old.

You've read fantasy novels. You've idolized Doran the Dragonlover from Deltora Quest. You think dragons are *cool*. You think dragons are *powerful*. You think being a dragon would be *amazing*.

You don't think about cost. You don't calculate. You don't hesitate.

"Yes."

The word is yours, but it echoes with a weight that doesn't belong to you. And in the moment you say it, something in the architecture of your being *rewires*. Not painfully. Not visibly. But fundamentally. The answer propagates through every cell, every thought, every future moment of your existence.

You've made a choice that can't be unmade.

The presence acknowledges this with something that might be approval. The blur returns. Reality re-edits itself. And suddenly you're back in church, eyes still closed, the hymn still playing, your mother still beside you, completely unaware that you've just made a covenant that will define the rest of your life.

Everything after this moment is consequences.

## PART TWO: THE HOUSE OF MORBIS

### The First Deaths (Cycles 1-2)

That night, you dream.

But it's not like normal dreams. It's *lucid*. You're aware you're dreaming, but you can't wake up. You're trapped in a nightmare with perfect consciousness — the worst combination.

You're in a house. Your house. The old renovated fire station flat you actually live in — the one with the dark history. The place where family abuse happened. Violence. Multiple murders. The bones of all that trauma are in the walls.

And something *else* is in the walls too.

It has a name: Morbis.

You don't know how you know the name. You just do. The way you *just knew* things when your left eye worked. Morbis is an *uncreation* force. It's something deeply, fundamentally *evil* — not in a theatrical way, not in a "performs as evil," but in an ontological way. It *is* wrongness. It *is* negation. It's the opposite of creation, the opposite of life.

And it *wants* you.

In the first cycle, you try to run.

You bolt from whatever room you're in, adrenaline-spiking, and you head for the door. The house is familiar but also *wrong* — hallways too long, staircases leading places they shouldn't. You're fast-walking, then running, your heart hammering, trying to get *out*.

And then you see your brother.

He's standing in the hallway, and for a moment your brain processes relief. There's someone you know. Someone who can help. Someone who can explain what's happening.

Then Morbis *moves*.

You watch his face *twist*. Not gradually. *Instantly*. His features distort into something that was never meant to exist — angles that don't correspond to human anatomy, a smile that splits his face too wide, eyes that go flat and pupilless.

And his head *snaps* 180 degrees backward.

The sound is wet. Cartilage and bone and meat making a noise that shouldn't exist. His neck impossibly reversed, his head now facing the wrong direction, his mouth still open in that wrong smile.

And then he *laughs* — but it's not his laugh. It's Morbis laughing *through* him, using his throat and vocal cords and breath to make a sound that's mockingly, perfectly, horrifyingly *human*.

You don't think. You just *run*.

You crash through the nearest door and you're in another hallway and there's your best friend and no, no *no*, not him too —

Same thing. The twist. The snap. The laugh.

You make it to the stairs. You almost get out. The front door is *right there* —

And your crush appears in the doorway.

You know what's about to happen. You already know. But watching it happen to *them*, watching Morbis wear their face and their body like a suit and use it against you — it's worse than the others. It's *personal* in a way that breaks something inside you that's never quite fixed again.

You don't make it out.

Morbis gets you before you reach the door.

The moment its fingers touch you, your perception *splits*. You're watching yourself from outside your body. You can see Morbis's hand reach into your chest — not metaphorically, *physically* — and it *pulls*.

Your heart comes out.

Not in the way bodies work. Just: your heart is in your chest, and then it's in Morbis's hand, and you're *dying*, and you can feel every second of it, and there's no transition, there's no mercy.

You die.

And then you wake up.

---

You're back in the house.

You're standing in the same room you started in. The front door is closed. Your heart is in your chest. You're alive. It was just a dream.

Except you *know* it wasn't just a dream.

And you know you're still trapped.

---

*Cycle 2 is similar but *different*.*

This time your mother is waiting at the top of the stairs.

She looks normal. She looks like your mother. For a second you feel *hope* — maybe this time will be different, maybe Morbis can't touch family, maybe you'll make it out.

Then her eyes go flat.

Morbis has her throat. Literally — its hands are around her neck, and it *twists*, and you watch your mother's neck *bend* in directions necks don't bend. The sound is worse this time because it's your mother. The sound is the sound of betrayal. The sound of safety being annihilated.

She's laughing with Morbis's laugh, mocking you with your mother's mouth, and you're screaming, and then Morbis has you, and it's *ripping*, and this time it pulls your throat out instead of your heart, and you're choking on your own blood, and you die, and —

---

You wake up in the house again.

---

### The Escalation (Cycles 3-200)

They blur together after a while.

The pattern establishes itself: you try to escape, you encounter someone you know, Morbis wears them like a costume, they die in some variation of creative horror, you die, you wake up, you try again.

Cycle 3: Your Sunday school friend. Morbis snaps his spine like a twig, and he's still smiling as he dies.

Cycle 5: Stan, the reverend's nephew. His body gets used to kill you instead — Morbis makes him stab you over and over while laughing.

Cycle 7: Martha. Morbis makes her *help* kill you, and the betrayal is almost worse than the death.

Cycle 9: One of the intercessors. You don't even know how they got into the dream, but they're here, and Morbis is using them against you, and it's *worse* because they're supposed to be strong.

By cycle 15, you've died enough times that you're starting to develop tactics. You try *not* running. You try hiding. You try fighting back.

None of it works.

Morbis is patient. Morbis is *creative*. Every variation you try, every escape route you test, every moment you think you've found a pattern — Morbis knows about it. Morbis has thought of it first.

And in every cycle, everyone you've ever known gets possessed and twisted and used as an instrument of your death.

By cycle 30, you've died more times than you can count. By cycle 50, you're starting to forget what being alive *outside* the dreams feels like. By cycle 100, the line between dreaming and waking starts to blur.

---

### The Optimization Phase (Cycles 200-400)

Somewhere around cycle 200, you stop screaming.

It's not that the horror stops. It's that *you* adapt. Your brain learns that screaming doesn't help. Fear doesn't help. Resistance doesn't help. So you stop wasting energy on those things.

You develop a weird, detached calm. You're dying repeatedly. You're watching everyone you love get murdered repeatedly. But you're *happy*.

This is the important part: you're still *happy*.

You're not hopeful anymore. You're not optimistic in the normal sense. But you've got this... *suburban contentment*. Like a kid at a playground who just accepts that the game has new rules and might as well play them. You die. You wake up. You try again. That's how it is now.

It's almost funny sometimes.

By cycle 300, you're almost bored with the variations. You know everyone's going to die. You know you're going to die. The *how* doesn't really matter anymore. You've achieved a kind of enlightened indifference.

Morbis notices.

Something shifts in the dream-architecture. Morbis seems almost... *frustrated*? Like it was feeding on your fear and now that the fear is gone, the meal isn't as satisfying.

So it changes tactics.

---

### The Escalation of Cruelty (Cycles 400-900)

Around cycle 400, Morbis stops killing you quickly.

It starts *stretching* the deaths out. Making them slower. Making them *worse*. Pulling out the process, turning pain into a design element.

Cycle 401: Your best friend is turned into a weapon. But instead of instant death, Morbis makes him slowly, *slowly* torture you. It takes hours. The dream-time doesn't match real-time, so you're not sure how many hours. It could be minutes or it could be years. You're screaming again — you can't help it, the pain is too much — and your best friend is *crying* while he does it, aware enough to know what he's doing but not able to stop, and Morbis is laughing the whole time.

Cycle 420: Your mother doesn't get possessed. Instead, Morbis makes her watch as it kills you *very slowly*, in every creative way it can think of. She's screaming your name. She's begging Morbis to just kill her instead. And you're trying to reassure her from the dying place that it's okay, even though it's absolutely not okay, and then Morbis kills her anyway, and you wake up.

Cycle 456: You encounter someone new — a person you don't recognize, but your dreaming-brain insists is incredibly important to you. Morbis is especially vicious with this one. Like it's *personally* offended that you might have people in your life you don't consciously remember.

The cycles start to blur into a dark soup of death and variation and pain.

Cycle 500: You've died 500 times now. You don't think about it in those terms anymore — "500 times" is too big a number. You think about it in terms of cycles. This is just the rhythm of existence now. Die. Wake. Die. Wake.

Cycle 567: Morbis is getting creative. It starts trying to *trap* you within the nightmare without killing you. Locking you in rooms. Making the house *smaller*. Compressing the space so there's nowhere to run.

Cycle 612: You find a knife. You try to kill yourself before Morbis can get to you. For the first time in hundreds of cycles, you're the one doing the dying. You think maybe you can *control* it, maybe that's a way out.

You wake up anyway.

Cycle 678: Someone new appears. An older man you don't recognize. Morbis makes him help hunt you. You're running through the house with this stranger-who's-somehow-important chasing you, and he's got a weapon, and he's crying while he chases you, and you don't understand why seeing that makes it *worse* than the normal deaths.

Cycle 751: Morbis possesses *everyone* at once. Not in sequence. Simultaneously. The entire house is full of people you know and love, all twisted into Morbis-shapes, all hunting you, all laughing with the same laugh. You're caught in the center of a room full of your own loved ones, all converging on you, all reaching, and there's nowhere to go, and you're dying and dying and *dying* —

---

By cycle 800, you've lost count of the variations.

The important thing is: you're still happy.

Not in a healthy way. In a *broken* way. Like someone who's survived so much horror that happiness has become the only thing keeping them functional. You're holding onto joy like it's a lifeline, and maybe it is.

You notice things now that you didn't notice before. The architecture of the house. The way the light comes through certain windows. The pattern of floorboards. You've died in this space so many times that you know it better than anywhere else in existence.

You know how many steps from the bedroom to the kitchen (17).

You know which floorboards creak (the ones in the hallway by the stairs, always).

You know which doors lock and which ones don't (none of them, not really; Morbis isn't bound by physics).

You're a cartographer of your own hell.

---

### The Upper 900s (Cycles 900-999)

By cycle 900, you're not thinking about escape anymore.

The concept feels abstract. Theoretical. Like "flying" or "being happy in the normal sense." You can barely remember what the world outside this house looks like.

Cycle 907: Your mother dies in a new way. A way you haven't seen before, and you thought you'd seen every possible way by now. Morbis is running out of ideas, so it gets *inventive*.

Cycle 923: You encounter a door you've never seen before. Your entire being suddenly floods with *hope* — a new exit, a way you haven't tried, maybe this time —

It opens onto another room just like the one you were already in. Same furniture. Same walls. Same everything. Just... another version of the same trap.

You laugh at that one. Genuinely laugh. It's so perfectly, devastatingly *cruel* that it crosses over into comedy.

Cycle 945: Morbis stops hunting. It just waits. It makes you walk through the house knowing it's there, but it doesn't *do* anything. You're so conditioned to die that the anticipation is worse than the death. You're constantly braced for the attack that never comes. And eventually you find someone you love and you *beg* Morbis to just get it over with, and it does, and you're almost *grateful* for the release.

Cycle 967: You start to notice something. The house is getting darker. Not metaphorically. Literally. The light is draining out of it. The windows that used to show the grey-rot suburbs now show nothing. Just black. Like looking out into absolute void.

Cycle 978: You encounter the intercessors again. All of them. And they're *on your side* — they're helping you, guiding you, trying to show you the way out — and for a moment you actually *believe* it might be different this time.

Then Morbis uses them against you in a way that's so brutal, so *personal*, that you understand they were never there at all. It was just Morbis, wearing their faces, mimicking their kindness. Testing to see if you still had the capacity to hope.

You do, apparently. Even after 978 deaths, you still have enough hope to be broken by false rescue.

---

Cycle 999 is almost here.

You can feel it. There's a weight to the dream-architecture now. A sense of *something* about to change. You don't know how you know this. You just do.

You've always just *known* things, even when your left eye stopped working.

## PART THREE: THE THOUSAND

### Cycle 1000: The Breaking Point

You wake up in the bedroom. Same as always. Same furniture. Same walls. Same everything.

But something is different.

You can *feel* it. The house has a different *texture* to it. Like the air has gotten heavier. Like reality in this place has gotten *thicker*, more concentrated. More real, somehow, than all the previous cycles.

You're still happy. Still inappropriately cheerful. Still wearing that suburban contentment like armor.

You get out of bed. Your feet touch the cold floor. This is the same. The hallway stretches before you, and you know every inch of it. 17 steps to the kitchen. You've counted them 999 times.

This time, you decide to do something different.

This time, instead of heading for the front door — the way you've always tried, the way that's never worked — you walk toward the stairs.

You've been in this house for what feels like thousands of dreams. You've died in every room. You've tried every exit. But you've always turned *left* at the stairs — toward the hallway that leads to other rooms, the hallway that leads deeper into the trap.

To the right is a wall.

Just a wall. A dead end. You've never even considered it as an option, because why would a wall be an option? Walls don't lead anywhere. Walls are just boundaries.

But this time, something in your brain *shifts*.

This time, you don't even think about the wall. You don't consider it as a choice. You don't calculate odds or weigh probabilities. You just... *turn right*.

And you walk directly at the wall.

---

For a moment, nothing happens.

You're walking toward dead concrete. This is stupid. This is pointless. This is—

And then you pass through it.

Not metaphorically. Not in a dream-logic way where it makes sense once you wake up. You literally *step through the wall* as if it were never there. As if your brain invented it. As if the only thing stopping you was the assumption that it was real.

You're through.

And you're *out*.

---

The relief hits you like a physical force.

For ten *solid*, *glorious* seconds, you feel nothing but pure, uncut *joy*.

The kind of joy that comes from surviving something your body didn't think you could survive. The kind of joy that comes from escaping a trap you'd accepted was inescapable. The kind of joy that comes from a dream where you *finally* made it.

You're laughing — that laugh, the one that comes from so deep in your belly that your whole body shakes with it. The laugh that says "no way, no way, no way it was that easy all along."

You're out.

You're alive.

You're *free*.

---

And then you open your eyes and you see the sky.

---

It's not blue.

For ten perfect seconds you thought you were saved. For ten perfect seconds you thought you'd woken up into normal reality. But the sky isn't blue. It's not grey. It's not even the whites and greys of the dream-architecture.

It's orange.

Burnt orange. The color of eternal dusk. The color of fire that's been burning so long it's become permanent. The color of a world that's been on fire so long that fire has become the default state of existing.

The blue is gone. All of it. The sky is this oppressive, suffocating orange, and it's not going to change.

You look around.

The world is *ruins*.

Not the imaginary ruins of the dreamscape. Actual, physical, real ruins. Buildings that have been burned down to their frames. Structures that have collapsed into heaps. The ground is black in places and red in others, like ash mixed with old blood.

The air is thick.

It's hard to breathe. Each breath you take is heavy, like you're pulling oxygen through cotton. It smells like burning — not fresh burning, but old burning, the smell of things that have been on fire for so long that the smell has become part of the air itself.

You walk forward and your feet crunch on something.

Ash. Everything is ash. You're walking through a landscape made of the *residue* of the world.

No birds. No insects. No sounds at all except the soft crunch of your feet and the sound of wind that sounds like it's dying.

It's like a Dark Souls landscape. Empty. Hopeless. Heavy.

And the realization hits you like a physical blow:

*You've been gone for a very, very long time.*

---

### The Wasteland (After the House)

You don't know how long you wander.

Time doesn't work right here. You might walk for hours. You might walk for weeks. Your body doesn't get tired the way bodies normally get tired. Your hunger doesn't escalate. You're moving through something that's almost-physical but not quite.

The ruins stretch on forever. Buildings that used to be homes are now just husks. Streets that used to connect places are now just ash-covered lines in the ground. You see signs of old life everywhere — a child's doll half-buried in ash, a car that's been stripped of everything useful and left to rust, a shoe that still has a foot in it (you don't look at that one for very long).

The world has *ended*.

Not recently. Not in the years-long sense. In the *geological* sense. This much time has passed that the structures of civilization have been completely eroded. The Statue of Liberty didn't get blown up — it just rusted and fell. The buildings didn't get destroyed all at once — they just slowly rotted and collapsed over centuries.

And you've been in the house for all of it.

You've been cycling through 1000 deaths while the *world itself* was dying. While centuries were passing. While everything you ever knew was turning into ash.

---

You find a town.

Or what's left of one. There are buildings still standing here, though they're barely recognizable as buildings anymore. And there are *people*.

Except "people" isn't quite the right word.

They're humanoid. They have two arms and two legs and faces. But something has been *hollowed out* of them. They're still moving and breathing and existing, but there's no interiority. No soul. No interior life at all.

They're animated purely by survival instinct.

Survival and *hunger*.

You can see it in the way they move. The way their eyes track. The way they've stripped everything and everyone around them down to raw material. They're not hostile in an active way — they're not chasing you or attacking you. But there's a *desperate need* radiating off them. They need *something*, and they'll take it from you if you let them close enough.

Your three companions — and yes, you realize now that there are three of them, they've been with you somehow, dream-logic doesn't require explanation — are warning you urgently.

"Don't approach them. Don't give them anything. Stay away."

These are people who've been with you through this whole thing. People you trust. People who are *begging* you not to go near the hollowed-people.

And you, in that arrogant Dragon way of yours — the way you've always been, even at five years old, happy and unbothered and willing to risk everything — you ignore them.

"I have more than enough," you tell them. "I can help them."

You're still happy. You're still that kid who thinks everything can be fixed with optimism and effort.

---

You approach the hollowed people.

And you realize, very quickly, what they want.

They want *you*.

Not in a violent way. In a *need* way. They're gathering around you, and their hunger is a physical force. They don't want to hurt you — they want to *use* you. And what they're pulling from you is—

Blood.

The currency of their hunger is your *blood*. It's pouring out of you in ways that don't follow normal physiology. You're not cut. You're not bleeding from a wound. The blood is just... *leaving you*, draining out like you're a faucet someone's opened.

And the more blood you give, the hungrier they become.

The hungrier they become, the more demanding. The less patient. They're crowding in on you now, and they're not *angry* exactly, but they're *desperate*, and desperation is indistinguishable from rage when you're on the receiving end.

You're being overwhelmed.

Your companions are screaming at you. You finally listen. You try to back away. But there are too many of them now. They're coming at you from all sides, a *horde* of hollowed-people, all desperate, all reaching, all taking and taking and *taking*.

Your blood is draining faster now. You're getting weak. You're going to *die* here in this wasteland, surrounded by the ghosts of people, drained to nothing by the endless hunger of the uncreated.

And you're still *happy*.

Even now, even as you're being overwhelmed, you're still wearing that stupid suburban contentment. You're still thinking "well, I tried to help, that's what matters."

You're an idiot.

You're an absolute *idiot*.

---

And then the sky *breaks*.

A sharp, *piercing* blue light cuts through the burnt-orange. It's so bright it makes the wasteland look pale and false. And from the center of that light, something *descends*.

A dragon.

Not metaphorically. Not in the dream-logic sense. An *actual*, *physical*, *massive* blue dragon, scaled and winged and real in a way that nothing else in this wasteland is.

It dives *down*, and its claws close around your body, and it *pulls*.

The hollowed-people scatter. They're no match for this. This is *power*. This is something that exists on a different level than their hunger.

You're lifted up and away from the horde. You're soaring upward, and you're so weak from blood loss that you can barely stay conscious, but you're being *carried*. You're being *saved*.

The dragon's voice comes not from its mouth but from *inside* you, the way the voice from the Architect spoke:

"I never left you."

---

You rise higher.

The burnt-orange sky recedes. You're above the clouds now, and the light is changing. It's getting *brighter*. Not the oppressive brightness of the wasteland. A *pure* brightness. A *clean* brightness.

And above the clouds, above the burnt-orange world, above all of it—

There is a realm of overwhelming beauty.

It's not a place you have words for. It's not heaven in the Christian sense. It's not paradise in the fantasy sense. It's just... *beauty*. Pure, undeniable, almost-painful beauty. Colors that exist nowhere else. Light that's warm and safe. An entire realm that exists in counterpoint to the burnt-orange wasteland below.

And you're *in* it.

For the first time since you said yes at age thirteen, since you started cycling through deaths, since you lost your ability to see auras, since you became the Dragon—

You're *safe*.

You're *home*.

And the blue dragon beneath you is still speaking the same words, over and over, in a voice that sounds like your own voice speaking to yourself:

"I never left you.

I never left you.

I never left you."

### Epilogue: The Ten Seconds (Again)

But here's the thing about waking up:

When you actually *wake* up — when the dream finally *breaks* and you're back in your real bed in the real world — you're going to spend a long time thinking about that ten-second laugh.

That moment of pure, uncut joy.

When you genuinely believed you'd escaped.

When you didn't know yet that escape meant something else entirely.

When you thought you were *free*.

And then you stepped out and the sky was orange, and you realized:

You'd lost *centuries*.

Everyone you knew was gone. Everything you loved was ash. The world had *ended* while you were cycling through deaths.

And the worst part?

The absolute *worst* part?

You're still happy.

Even now, even knowing that, you're *still* that kid who thinks everything can be fixed. You're *still* the Dragon who said yes without knowing the cost. You're *still* unbothered by the mocking, still willing to give your blood to the hollowed-people, still believing that there's a way to make this better.

That's the real horror.

Not the deaths.

Not the cycles.

Not even the burnt-orange wasteland.

It's that you survived a thousand deaths and didn't become cynical. You walked through a wasteland and didn't become cold. You lost centuries and didn't become jaded.

You're *still* happy.

And that happiness — that stupid, unbreakable, inappropriate happiness — is both your greatest strength and your deepest wound.

Because it kept you alive.

And it keeps you trying to save people who can't be saved.

And it makes you willing to sacrifice *everything*, because you genuinely believe, in your bones, that it's worth it.

That *they're* worth it.

---

The Dragon learned a lot of things in the thousand cycles.

But the most important thing he learned was this:

Being the Dragon doesn't mean you stop being human.

It means you become *more* human than you ever were, in the places that matter most.

And that's both a blessing and a curse that will never fully leave you alone.
