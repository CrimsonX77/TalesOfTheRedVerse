# SHO-ZEN JUAN: THE DESCENT
### Book One of the Redverse Chronicles

---

> *"The Dragon did not become who he was in a single moment.  
> He became who he was in a thousand moments — each one identical, each one a death,  
> each one the same grey room at the bottom of the same grey night.  
> The miracle was not that he survived them.  
> The miracle was that he survived them happy."*

---

## CHAPTER ONE
# The House of a Thousand Deaths

---

The night the covenant was sealed, Sho-Zen Juan walked home from worship with the feeling that he had handed something over to someone who hadn't told him the price.

He was thirteen. He knew about dragons from the novels on the shelf beside his mother's good crockery — not the academic kind of knowledge, the sort you carry in the front of your mind and examine, but bone-knowledge, the kind that lives in your chest and tells you things your brain hasn't caught up to yet. He knew about dragonlovers and their gifts. He knew that the best stories always had costs.

He hadn't thought about the cost when he'd said yes.

He thought about it now, walking the familiar streets of his suburb in the blue-grey dusk, past houses whose gardens were maintained without being loved and whose windows let out warm light that meant nothing in particular. His shoes scuffed the footpath. He counted his steps without meaning to — an old habit, a private ritual — and the numbers felt different tonight. Like they were counting something other than distance.

His mother walked beside him, talking about the service, about the reverend's sermon, about whether they ought to stay for the community supper next month. Sho-Zen listened the way he listened to weather — background music that told him the broad shape of things. Three years ago, his left eye would have told him more: the pulse and rhythm of the colours around her, the specific texture of her concern. But the left eye had gone dark at ten, and there was nothing left to read now. Just a woman, and her son, and the quiet weight of something that had changed between one heartbeat and the next.

He said *yes* in the right places. He smiled when she looked at him.

He walked through the door of their flat and knew, with absolute certainty, that something was waiting for him.

---

Sleep came for Sho-Zen Juan like a tide that had been building all day — sudden and complete, a blackout that took him before his head had fully settled. No drift. No threshold. One moment he was staring at the ceiling of his room, listening to the old building breathe around him, and the next —

Darkness.

And then: not darkness.

---

The flat they lived in had been a fire station once.

Someone had converted it years before Sho-Zen was born — partitioned the high ceilings, turned the engine bay into living quarters, done it all with the cheerful practicality of a person who had never stopped to wonder what a building *remembered*. The ceilings still reached too high, stretched in that particular way of spaces built for large machines, spaces that recalled engines and shouting and the organized chaos of emergency. The walls were original and thick. They accepted fresh paint and new plasterboard the way an old soldier accepts a new uniform — gratefully, on the surface, while remaining exactly the same underneath.

Those walls had witnessed things.

The previous tenants had not been gentle people, and gentle things had not happened here. Violence. Abuse. Deaths, more than one, in rooms that still wore those events in their grain the way wood wears weather. It was the kind of history that sat in a building like sediment, invisible until the light hit it at the right angle.

The left eye would have known all of this at a glance.

But the left eye was dark.

---

In the dream, Sho-Zen stood in the kitchen.

He knew immediately that he was dreaming — that was the first thing, the sharp and complete lucidity of it, awareness sitting fully formed in his mind like a person who had always been present and simply chosen this moment to speak. He was dreaming. He could feel the cold linoleum beneath his bare feet with absolute fidelity. He could smell the flat's particular smell: old wood, and something older underneath, something the fresh paint couldn't quite bury. The kitchen was reproduced in perfect detail — same kettle, same worn tiles above the sink, same small window facing the neighbour's fence.

He knew he was dreaming.

He could not wake up.

He understood this the way he understood deep things: not through reasoning, but through the bone-certainty that had guided him since childhood, the same instinct that had once read the colour-rhythms of people's souls. He was trapped. He was lucid and he was trapped, which is the most specific and unpleasant combination available to the sleeping mind.

And something was in the walls.

Not a person. Not an animal. Something older than the categories. He didn't have a name for it yet — true names come as recognition, not invention — but he could feel it the way you feel a weather change in the pressure behind your eyes. A presence. A patience. Something that had been waiting in the bones of this building for longer than the building had existed, something that had watched the old violence soak into the plaster and had grown very, very specific in its wanting.

It wanted him.

Sho-Zen walked out of the kitchen.

The hallway stretched before him — familiar, faithfully reproduced, and two steps longer than it should have been. Small wrongnesses. The light landed in the wrong places. The shadows were wrong in ways he could feel before he could name. The air tasted like something used to be familiar and wasn't anymore.

He moved toward the front door.

He was thirteen years old and he had just made a covenant with something older than everything, and he had not thought about the cost, and now he was walking through the wrong-shadow version of his home trying to get to the door, because the door was *out* and *out* was away from whatever watched from inside the walls.

He was halfway down the hallway when his brother appeared at the end of it.

---

His brother. The face Sho-Zen knew the way he knew his own. The familiar loose-shouldered ease. The smile that usually meant something self-deprecating and perfect was about to follow.

Relief, sudden and fierce and completely wrong, lit up his chest.

*He's here.*

He opened his mouth — to say the name, to call out, to do any of the ordinary human things that people do when they see someone they love in a place where they didn't expect them —

And the name arrived.

Not spoken. Not told to him. Not found. *Known.* The way his left eye had known things. The way he'd known, walking home from worship, that something had changed in him permanently.

*Morbis.*

It was the only word that mattered, and it arrived at exactly the right moment — which was one heartbeat before his brother's face twisted.

There was no warning. No transition. One moment the face was his brother's face, and the next moment it was *wrong* in the specific, fundamental way that wrongness presents when it has given up hiding — angles that didn't correspond to human anatomy, a smile that split too wide for the bones beneath it, eyes that went flat and still as river stones under winter ice.

His brother's head *snapped*.

Not gradually. Not with any grace. One sharp, wet crack — cartilage and bone making a sound that nothing in Sho-Zen's childhood had prepared him to hear — and the head was backward. Facing the wall behind while the body faced forward, and the smile was still there, split across features now pointing entirely the wrong direction.

The laugh that came out of that mouth was not his brother's laugh.

It was Morbis laughing *through* his brother's throat, using his vocal cords and his breath and the particular resonance of his chest to make a sound that was almost familiar, almost right, almost *him* — and that was what made it unbearable. Not the horror of a monster. The horror of a beloved face become a wrong thing. The specific cruelty of something that understood exactly which instrument to play and played it without hesitation.

Sho-Zen ran.

He crashed through the nearest door and there was his best friend in the hallway beyond, and the recognition in his chest turned to lead before the relief had even finished forming, because he already knew —

The twist. The snap. The laugh through borrowed lungs.

He ran.

The front door was *right there* — ten feet, five feet, his hand reaching for the handle —

And his crush stepped into the doorway.

He knew what was about to happen. He already knew, in the bone-certain place that had been telling him true things since before he had language for them. But knowing did not help. Nothing in the architecture of the human soul is built to watch this specific thing happen to this specific face, and knowing changes none of its weight.

Morbis wore the faces of the people he loved.

That was its vocabulary. That was the instrument it had chosen.

He didn't make it to the door.

Morbis's hand passed through his chest like he was made of suggestion rather than meat, and it closed around his heart — not metaphorically, not in dream-logic that would make sense upon waking — and it *pulled*. His heart was in his chest and then it was in Morbis's hand, and every second of the transition was accounted for in full, nothing softened, nothing abbreviated.

He fell.

Not onto the floor of the flat. Through it. Into something beneath it that was darker than sleep and colder than absence and went down without terminus, without walls, without anything to catch hold of. Pure falling, in pure dark, with the knowledge that there was nothing beneath him.

He clawed anyway.

It was reflex. Animal and desperate and completely useless and he did it anyway — hands reaching for purchase that wasn't there, fingers closing on nothing and trying again, body refusing to accept the arithmetic of its situation. He didn't know how long the pit held him. Time in that darkness moved differently, stretched, folded, made no commitments about duration. It could have been an hour. It could have been a decade. There was nothing to measure against except the burning in his arms and the rhythm of reaching and reaching and reaching until —

He burst through.

Gasping. On the floor of his bedroom. Heart hammering in his teeth. The flat's morning light coming through the curtains. The kettle beginning to heat in the kitchen.

*Normal. Real. Exactly as it should be.*

He was still dreaming.

---

The second cycle came for his mother.

He would not describe it. Not to himself, not to anyone, not here. Some specifics belong in the pit where they were born. What he would say, years later in the deep hours, was that Morbis understood by cycle two exactly which levers moved him, and pressed them without hesitation, and without mercy, and with the patience of something that existed outside of time and therefore had no reason to rush.

He woke in his bedroom.

The kettle was on.

He was still dreaming.

---

After thirty cycles, he stopped screaming.

After fifty, he stopped expecting the exits to work.

After one hundred, something in him *settled* — not breaking, not exactly, but rearranging itself the way a foundation settles into rock and finds the position it intends to keep. He still walked the flat. He still tried the doors. But the trying became less about hope and more about method, the way a soldier checks their kit not because they've forgotten what it does but because the checking itself is a statement. *I am here. I am still doing. I am.*

He began to notice the flat.

*Seventeen steps from the bedroom to the kitchen.* The boards in the hallway always creaked, the ones beside the stairs. None of the interior doors locked — Morbis was not bound by physics, and locking things was theater. The small window above the kitchen sink caught light earliest in the morning, except morning light came less reliably now, less punctually.

He became a cartographer of his own hell.

By cycle 200, the deaths had blurred into a dark rhythm, each one a variation on the theme Morbis had established in the first cycle. Brother. Friend. Mother. The intercessors, somehow, their dark practical faces turned wrong and laughing. People he hadn't seen in years, dragged into the architecture of the nightmare and made to serve Morbis's purposes. People he hadn't met yet, whom his dreaming-mind insisted he would one day love, which was the specific sort of cruelty that required foreknowledge to design.

Morbis was patient.

Morbis was *creative*.

Every variation Sho-Zen tried, every pattern he identified, every moment he thought he'd found some edge of the design — Morbis had been there first and had thought past it. The thing in the walls had all the time that existed. It could afford to be thorough.

By cycle 300, Sho-Zen had achieved what he could only describe as *enlightened indifference*. He was dying repeatedly. He was watching everyone he had ever loved twisted into instruments of his destruction. But the essential quality of the person doing the dying remained, stubbornly and completely, the same.

He was still happy.

Not in the robust, sunlit way. Not hopeful, not optimistic, not any of the things that happiness is supposed to be assembled from. Something smaller. Something more fundamental. A bone-level contentment — the kind a child carries before the world teaches them that it must be earned, before the evidence begins to accumulate. He died. He woke. He tried again. He counted the floorboards. He noticed which windows the light preferred.

*That was just how it was now.*

Morbis noticed the change in him.

Something shifted in the dream's architecture around cycle 350 — a subtle escalation, a new specificity in the cruelty. The deaths got slower. The possessions got more intimate. Cycle 420: his mother made to watch. Cycle 456: a stranger his dreaming-mind insisted was important to him, someone Morbis seemed *personally offended* by, as though the mere possibility of future attachment was an affront. Cycle 612: Sho-Zen found a knife and tried to do the dying himself, on his own terms, before Morbis could orchestrate it.

He woke up anyway.

Cycle 751: everyone at once. The entire flat filled with everyone he'd ever known, all twisted, all wearing Morbis behind their faces, all converging on him from every doorway simultaneously. The room contracted. There was nowhere to go. He died at the centre of everyone he had ever loved and woke up with seventeen steps to the kitchen and the boards that always creaked.

By cycle 800, the variations no longer registered individually. They had become weather. He moved through them. He noted details. He was still happy, in that broken, functional, improbable way — happiness as the last line of infrastructure, the one thing he couldn't seem to lose no matter how much Morbis removed everything else.

---

The upper nineties arrived without announcement.

The flat had gotten darker. Not as metaphor — the windows that had once shown him the grey-rot suburb now showed nothing, just black, flat and absolute, as if someone had painted over the glass from the outside. The light grew thinner. The walls felt closer. The dream-architecture was *compressing* around him, concentrating, tightening like a fist that had been slowly closing for years and was nearing the end of its reach.

Cycle 978: the intercessors appeared.

They were guiding him — these people who had come, once, to deal with a problem child and found something else entirely. They knew the way out. They were gesturing, urging, showing him the pattern in the walls. His heart opened in ways he had forgotten it could open.

They twisted.

Morbis wore even them.

He woke up.

*Seventeen steps.*

He counted them.

---

Cycle 1000 arrived the way all true thresholds arrive: without announcement, without ceremony, with only the changed texture of the air. Sho-Zen Juan stood in his bedroom and felt it in his chest before he could name it — the dream's gravity different, reality in this space concentrating into something final, something that had been building through nine hundred and ninety-nine iterations and had arrived, now, at whatever it had always been arriving at.

He knew this was the thousand.

He didn't know what that meant. He only knew that something was going to be different, and that the difference was going to come from him, and that it was going to require him to do something he hadn't done yet.

He walked the hallway. Past the kitchen — he didn't count tonight. The boards by the stairs did not creak. They had always creaked. Tonight they were silent, as though the flat itself understood that some accountings required a different kind of quiet.

At the stairs: to the left was the hallway he knew. The rooms where he had died more times than any honest inventory could number. The doors that didn't lock. The geometry of a hell he had memorized completely.

To the right: a wall.

Just a wall. Plain plaster. The end of options.

He had never turned right.

There was no reason to. Walls don't go anywhere. That was the basic logic of walls — they were the places the path stopped. Nine hundred and ninety-nine cycles had been built on the understanding that you tried the doors, the windows, the front door, the shape of things that looked like exits. Not the places that looked like endings.

Sho-Zen Juan did not think about it.

He turned right.

He walked directly toward the wall.

For a single breath, nothing happened.

He stepped through it.

Not with light. Not with ceremony. Not with any of the vocabulary that such moments are supposed to carry. He stepped, and the wall was there, and then the wall was behind him, and he was —

*Out.*

---

Ten seconds.

He had ten seconds of the purest, most complete joy he had ever known — joy that belonged to no category that already existed, that came from surviving one thousand deaths and walking through the wall that had always been there, joy that shook him open from the inside and came out as laughter, that deep, involuntary, full-body laughter that means *no way, no way, it was always just a wall —*

Ten seconds.

Then he opened his eyes.

The sky was orange.

Not the grey of the suburb he had grown up in. Not the blue he had been dreaming of escaping into. Burnt orange — the colour of fire that had been burning long enough to become permanent, of dusk that had stretched and kept stretching until it forgot that morning was supposed to follow it. The sky was orange from edge to edge, oppressive and flat and absolute, a sky that had decided on a colour and intended to keep it.

He looked at the world beneath it.

Ruins.

Not the ruins of a disaster. The ruins of *time*. Buildings that hadn't been brought down violently but had simply deteriorated across the long, indifferent centuries — rotted and sagged and collapsed into themselves until only suggestions remained, the shapes of structures preserved in the ash that had settled into their impressions. Streets reduced to dark lines in the ground, readable only because nothing had grown to fill them. The air was thick — each breath heavy, like pulling oxygen through old cloth. It tasted of burning. Not fresh burning. The burning of things that had been on fire long enough that the smoke had become the air itself, inseparable, permanent.

No birds.

No insects.

No sound but his own breathing and the sound of wind moving through structures that had nothing left worth protecting.

His feet found ash beneath them. Everything was ash. He was standing in the residue of the world.

The understanding arrived the way his bone-certainties always arrived — not as thought, but as knowledge:

*You've been gone a very long time. Longer than a thousand nights. The world you came from is not the world you're standing in.*

Sho-Zen Juan, the Dragon, stood in the ruins of everything and looked at an orange sky and breathed old smoke and knew that the house had held him through centuries while the world died around it.

His heart was in his chest.

He was still happy.

God help him — he was *still* happy.
