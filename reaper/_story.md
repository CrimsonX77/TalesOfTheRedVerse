The Dragon and the Reaper
Book One: The Place I Call Home

The coffee machine had not, to Sable's knowledge, done anything wrong.
She cleaned it anyway. This was the fifth time since opening, which had been at seven, which meant it was not yet nine, which meant the morning had approximately four more hours of this particular texture before the lunch crowd came in and gave her something to actually do.
The café was called The Anchor. She hadn't named it. Her ex-husband Dren had, in the early optimistic years when he'd believed a café on Tolver Street could be a destination rather than a convenience. He'd been wrong about that, and eventually wrong about several other things, and then one morning three years ago he'd left a set of keys on the counter beside a folded document that turned out to be the deed, and a note that said I think this suits you better than it suits me. Sorry it took me so long to notice. Which was, she had decided, the most Dren thing he had ever done. Quiet exit. No fuss. Reasonable terms.
She had kept the name.
The machine gleamed. Sable looked at her own reflection in the steel — silver hair pulled back, ears slightly annoyed at the early hour the way cat ears communicate annoyance, which is mostly through the angle — and decided it was clean enough to dirty again. She reached for the coffee.
The door opened.
Not a customer. The bell had a different sound for her daughter, something she couldn't have explained to anyone who asked — maybe the force of it, maybe the specific speed. Mira came in the way eight-year-olds come through doors they consider their own, which is completely.
"Mum," she said, "there's a man in the alley on Cotter Street and he's got blood everywhere and I think he might be dead but he's probably not dead because his hand moved but there was a lot of blood, do you want to come see?"
Sable set down the coffee.
"How much blood?"
Mira held her arms out to approximately shoulder width.
"A lot-a lot or a little-a lot?"
Mira considered this with the seriousness it deserved. "A lot-a lot," she said. "But not scary. More like..." she searched for the word with her whole face. "...impressive."
Sable untied her apron.
There was a protocol for this, technically — you called someone, you waited, you did not go investigate bloody strangers in alleys two blocks from your place of business before nine in the morning. She was aware of the protocol.
She hung the apron on the hook behind the counter.
"Show me," she said.

The alley on Cotter Street was exactly what Mira had promised.

Sable stood at the entrance to it with her daughter at her elbow and took stock the way she took stock of most things — quietly, thoroughly, without immediately deciding what to do about it.

He was face-down. That was the first thing. Face-down in the particular way of someone who had not chosen the position but had arrived at it through a process of elimination — every other position having become unavailable somewhere along the way. He was not large, which surprised her. The quantity of blood suggested something larger. He was compact, she decided. Built close to the ground. Dark coat, or what had been a dark coat before it became a record of recent events.

The hand moved.

Just the fingers. A slow curl against the cobblestones like something testing whether the ground was still there.

"See," said Mira, with quiet satisfaction.

Sable walked into the alley.

"Mum," Mira said, in the tone of someone who has decided to register an observation without expecting it to change anything, "you're not supposed to just—"

"Go back to the café," Sable said. "Turn the sign to closed and wait inside. Don't open the door for anyone."

"Can I have a pastry?"

"Yes."

The sound of small feet retreating. The bell above the café door two blocks away, inaudible from here but present in Sable's mind the way the sounds of your own place always are. She crouched beside him.

Up close the damage was specific and extensive and made no immediate sense. Not the pattern of an accident. Not quite the pattern of a fight, or not any fight conducted by the usual means. There was something almost ceremonial about the distribution of it — as though whatever had happened had been thorough on purpose, had wanted to reach every part of him.

Still. The fingers had moved.

"Hey," she said.

Nothing.

"You're in the alley behind Cotter Street. My daughter found you. I'm going to turn you over now."

She did. He was heavier than he looked, which she filed away without comment.

His face was young. That was the first thing and the wrong thing — the face of someone who should not have accumulated this quantity of damage, should not have the particular quality of stillness that lived around the eyes even now, the stillness of something very old that had learned not to show what it was carrying. Young face. Old stillness. The combination sat wrong in a way she couldn't immediately name.

His eyes opened.

Green. Extraordinarily green, the green of something that had been many other colours first and arrived at this one through a long process she was not going to get the full account of today.

He looked at her without apparent surprise. Without the usual confusion of someone regaining consciousness in an unfamiliar alley. He looked at her the way someone looks at a thing they have been waiting for — not with recognition exactly, but with the specific quality of attention that belongs to arriving somewhere after a long journey.

Then the corner of his mouth moved. Not quite a smile. The ghost of a smile. The memory of one.

"...hi," he said. His voice was wrecked, low, carried the texture of someone who had been somewhere loud for a very long time.

Sable looked at him for a moment.

"Can you walk?"

He considered this with more genuine uncertainty than she expected. "...probably not well."

"Okay." She stood, assessed the geometry of the problem, made a decision. "I have a café two blocks from here with a back room and a first aid kit and nobody in it. You look like you need all three."

He blinked at her. Something moved behind the green eyes that she didn't have a name for yet.

"You're not going to ask what happened?"

"You'll tell me or you won't," she said, and got an arm under his shoulder. "Either way you're bleeding on my city's cobblestones and that's a problem we can solve right now. The other problems can wait."
