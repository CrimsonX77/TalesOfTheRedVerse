Book One: The Place I Call Home

The coffee machine had not, to Sable's knowledge, done anything wrong.
She cleaned it anyway. This was the fifth time since opening, which had been at seven, which meant it was not yet nine, which meant the morning had approximately four more hours of this particular texture before the lunch crowd came in and gave her something to actually do.
The café was called The Anchor. She hadn't named it. Her ex-husband Dren had, in the early optimistic years when he'd believed a café on Tolver Street could be a destination rather than a convenience. He'd been wrong about that, and eventually wrong about several other things, and then one morning three years ago he'd left a set of keys on the counter beside a folded document that turned out to be the deed, and a note that said I think this suits you better than it suits me. Sorry it took me so long to notice. Which was, she had decided, the most Dren thing he had ever done. Quiet exit. No fuss. Reasonable terms.
She had kept the name.
The machine gleamed. Sable looked at her own reflection in the steel — silver hair pulled back, ears slightly annoyed at the early hour the way cat ears communicate annoyance, which is mostly through the angle — and decided it was clean enough to dirty again. She reached for the coffee.
The door opened.
Not a customer. The bell had a different sound for her daughter, something she couldn't have explained to anyone who asked — maybe the force of it, maybe the specific speed. Mira came in the way eight-year-olds come through doors they consider their own, which is completely.
"Mum," she said, "there's a man in the alley on Cotter Street and he's got blood everywhere and I think he might be dead but he's probably not dead because his hand moved but there was a lot of blood, do you want to come see?"
Sable set down the coffee.
"How much blood?"
Mira held her arms out to approximately shoulder width.
"A lot-a lot or a little-a lot?"
Mira considered this with the seriousness it deserved. "A lot-a lot," she said. "But not scary. More like..." she searched for the word with her whole face. "...impressive."
Sable untied her apron.
There was a protocol for this, technically — you called someone, you waited, you did not go investigate bloody strangers in alleys two blocks from your place of business before nine in the morning. She was aware of the protocol.
She hung the apron on the hook behind the counter.
"Show me," she said.

Dark coat, or what had been a dark coat before it became a record of recent events.

Up close the damage was specific and extensive and made no immediate sense. Not the pattern of an accident. Not quite the pattern of a fight, or not any fight conducted by the usual means. There was something almost ceremonial about the distribution of it — as though whatever had happened had been thorough on purpose, had wanted to reach every part of him.

"Hey," she said.

"You're in the alley behind Cotter Street. My daughter found you. I'm going to turn you over now."

She did. He was heavier than he looked, which she filed away without comment.

His face was young. That was the first thing and the wrong thing — the face of someone who should not have accumulated this quantity of damage, should not have the particular quality of stillness that lived around the eyes even now, the stillness of something very old that had learned not to show what it was carrying. Young face. Old stillness. The combination sat wrong in a way she couldn't immediately name.

His eyes opened.

Green. Extraordinarily green, the green of something that had been many other colours first and arrived at this one through a long process she was not going to get the full account of today.

He looked at her without apparent surprise. Without the usual confusion of someone regaining consciousness in an unfamiliar alley. He looked at her the way someone looks at a thing they have been waiting for — not with recognition exactly, but with the specific quality of attention that belongs to arriving somewhere after a long journey.

Then the corner of his mouth moved. Not quite a smile. The ghost of a smile. The memory of one.

"...hi," he said. His voice was wrecked, low, carried the texture of someone who had been somewhere loud for a very long time.

Sable looked at him for a moment.

"Can you walk?"

He considered this with more genuine uncertainty than she expected. "...probably not well."

"Okay." She stood, assessed the geometry of the problem, made a decision. "I have a café two blocks from here with a back room and a first aid kit and nobody in it. You look like you need all three."

Chapter Two: Forty Seven Seconds
The control room of the Tolver City Watch never fully slept.
Aldric Vane had been captain for eleven years and in that time he had learned to read the room the way sailors read water — by texture, by sound, by the particular quality of attention on the faces of his operators. At three in the morning it had a certain hum. At shift change, a different one. On a slow Tuesday it produced the specific flatness of people doing necessary work with the enthusiasm of people doing necessary work.
He knew all the textures.
He did not know this one.
It had started at eight forty-seven. Operator Thess had said sir in a specific register — not the register of a routine anomaly, not the register of equipment malfunction — and Aldric had crossed the room in four steps and looked at her screen and felt something move in his chest that he would not, in his report, call dread.
The energy reading made no sense.
Not elevated. Not spiking in any category his instruments had names for. The needle — metaphorically, the display — had simply gone to a place the display had not been designed to include. Past the calibrated range. Past the emergency range. Into the white space beyond both where no number lived because no number had been considered necessary there.
"Source?" he said.
"Cotter Street adjacent," Thess said. "The alley network behind the market district."
"Duration?"
"Forty seven seconds. Then—" she paused. "Then it stopped. All at once. Like a door closing."
Aldric straightened. Looked at the board. Everything nominal now — insultingly, impossibly nominal, the readings of a city going about its morning as though nothing had occurred in the alley network behind the market district forty seven seconds ago.
"I want a squad on site," he said. "Full assessment, report back in ten."
"Already dispatched," said Thess. "Squad Seven. They checked in at the alley entrance at eight fifty-two."
Aldric nodded. Watched the board. Picked up his coffee and found it had gone cold without his noticing.
Eight fifty-three. Nothing.
Eight fifty-four. Nothing.
"Thess," he said, without moving, "when did Squad Seven last check in?"
The silence before her answer was three seconds long and contained a great deal of information.
"Eight fifty-two, sir."
Aldric set down the cold coffee.
On the board, seven signal indicators — the personal beacons every Watch officer wore on duty, standard issue, tested weekly, powered by a charge that lasted thirty days — sat completely dark. Not malfunctioning. Not obscured. Dark in the way a candle is dark after it has been put out. The way something is dark when it is no longer producing the thing that makes it visible.
The room had gone very quiet.
Aldric was aware of his operators looking at him. This was the moment, he understood, that separated the captains who had actually prepared for the impossible from the captains who had prepared for the difficult and assumed that covered it.
He breathed in through his nose. Out through his mouth.
"Get me the duty commander," he said, level. "Get me a second squad standing by — do not dispatch, standing by. Get me every reading from the last ten minutes archived and locked. And someone find me a map of every structure within two blocks of Cotter Street."
Movement. Good. People with tasks don't panic — they just do the tasks.
Thess spoke quietly beside him. "Sir. What do you think it was?"
Aldric looked at the dark signal indicators on the board. Seven of them. Seven good officers who had checked in at eight fifty-two and had not checked in since and whose beacons were producing the specific darkness of something that was no longer there to produce light.
He thought about the reading that had gone past the white space. Past the place where numbers lived.
"I think," he said carefully, "that something came down in the market district this morning. And I think it's very important that we find out what it is before it finds out we're looking."
He picked up the cold coffee. Drank it anyway.
On the board, the seven dark signals did not flicker.

The back room of The Anchor was not designed for medical emergencies.

It was designed for storing dry goods, doing paperwork nobody wanted to do at the front counter, and occasionally eating lunch standing up over the sink. Sable had cleared the small table of invoices and pulled the first aid kit from the shelf above the mop bucket and was now working through it with the methodical focus of someone who had patched up enough scraped knees to not be dramatic about blood.

He sat on the table because there was nowhere else to put him. He hadn't complained about the journey from the alley. He hadn't complained about the stairs. He sat very still now while she worked, which she appreciated.

Mira sat on the flour sack in the corner eating her pastry and watching with open academic interest.

"You have a lot of cuts," she observed.

"I noticed," he said.

"Did it hurt?"

"Mira," Sable said.

"It's fine," he said, and glanced at the girl with something that softened the lines around his eyes. "Yes. A bit."

Sable cut away what remained of his shirt without asking permission because it was beyond saving and she needed to see what she was working with. She kept her expression neutral. He was lean and built and covered in damage that was already, she noticed with a small private alarm she didn't show, looking older than it should. The deepest cuts at the edges already sitting somewhere between fresh and healed. The bruising yellowing faster than bruising did.

She was not going to comment on that.

She cleaned what needed cleaning, closed what she could close, and worked her way across his shoulders and down his arms with the same focus she brought to the coffee machine. At some point she registered that his arms were quite good and felt heat move into her face and was grateful she was standing behind him.
