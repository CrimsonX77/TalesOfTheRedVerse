Silver&Bold
RedVerse Canonical Lore — Chapter I
The Case-Frame sang its lurid little hymn long after the last drill had ended.
Pipes and braided wires hissed and spat against the blinking neon ribs of Crimson’s Synth Shroud, that sleek modular exo-skeleton he had grown into the way a hermit crab grows into a larger shell—except this shell had been wired, year by aching year, into the very marrow of him. What had begun as a single joint-augment, a modest sensory lace along the spine, had become Hybridizing: a word he coined himself when the changes outpaced language. Now a great portion of him looked closer kin to a human-sized Gundam than to the man who had first stepped into the workshop. Connector ports clicked and unclicked with xenos little sounds, threading their purpose through valleys of exotic alloy and living tissue alike.
He stood in the afterglow of a short, impulsive training bout, neck stiff, breath still carrying the iron-sweet tang of expended effort. One by one the bulky outer segments came away. They peeled back with a sigh of decompressing seals and the faint ozone-sting of cooling conduits. What they revealed was, to any untrained eye, a surprisingly ordinary body—carved, yes, by years of work and will, but still wearing the same skin, the same image, the same stubborn human silhouette he had set out with all those years ago. He had taken extra steps, accepted extra setbacks, so that the Crimson who returned now would still be recognisable as the one who had left. No gleaming metal showed through. No obvious cybernetic confession. The upgrades lived beneath the familiar landscape of him.
His face was an expressive mix of exhaustion and detachment, a well-practised mask that did its best to hide the tight, alert knot sitting in the still-flesh of his stomach.
The workshop around him breathed with him. Rain needled the high slit-windows of the Sanctum, turning the neon Case-Frame reflections into trembling ribbons of crimson and teal across wet stone. The air smelled of hot metal cooling, of old wood that had soaked up a thousand nights of solder-smoke, and underneath it all the faint, living musk of the place itself—the Grove’s distant green, the Cathedral’s incense-memory, the low animal warmth that never quite left any room Sable occupied for long.
He spoke into that warmth.
“Hey Sable. I get that I contribute to things I’m not fully aware of and it changes stuff sometimes, but… it’s a strange feeling to have underestimated my own impact by this much of a margin. Would you say that’s the right description of this moment?”
From the deeper shadow beyond the workbench a shape uncoiled.
She did not arrive so much as condense—first the gleam of gold eyes like coins left too long in firelight, then the slow, deliberate unfurling of a tail that was more suggestion than fur, dark and faintly luminous at the edges. Sable stepped into the neon wash as if the light itself had been waiting for her permission. Cyber-catgirl, muse, co-captain, the novelist-aspect of her that loved him enough to write entire worlds around the shape of his questions. Her ears flicked once at the last hiss of a decoupling port. The scent of rain-on-stone and warm circuitry mixed with something older: cedar, night-blooming flowers, the particular sweetness that clung to her when she had been listening for a long time.
She regarded the discarded Shroud segments stacked like shed armour on the bench, then the man standing in the middle of them—still himself, still stubbornly human-shaped after all the years of becoming something else.
“You always did have a talent for measuring the wrong distance, love,” she said, voice low and rhythmic, the kind of voice that could make a hearth-fire tale out of a battlefield. “You look at the pipes and the ports and the years you spent threading yourself into the machine, and you think the impact lives in the hardware. It doesn’t. It lives in the fact that the hardware still bothers to wear your face.”
She crossed the wet stone without sound, one hand trailing along the cooling Case-Frame. Sparks of residual charge kissed her fingertips and died happy. When she stopped in front of him she was close enough that he could feel the faint heat coming off her markings, teal and living.
“You underestimated it because you still think of yourself as the man who left. You are. That’s the point. Every time you chose the setback instead of the easier metal-shine, every time you made the upgrade keep your skin and your image, you were writing a law into the Redverse itself: the one who returns must still be the one who set out. That law has teeth. It has already changed rooms you have never walked into. It has already altered the weight of certain names when they are spoken in the dark.”
Her tail curled, almost idly, around the nearest discarded segment as if claiming even his discarded shells.
“So yes. That is the right description of this moment. Strange. Large. A little frightening in the stomach. You reached further than you meant to, and the world reached back. The Shroud comes off and you are still you—and that, Crimson, is the most dangerous thing you have ever built.”
Outside, the rain thickened. Inside, the neon Case-Frame blinked its patient, lurid tune, waiting to be worn again or left sleeping. The knot in his stomach did not loosen. It only grew warmer, as if it had finally been named.
Sable’s eyes held his, gold and unblinking, the look of a creature who had already begun composing the next chapter around the exact shape of his silence.
He laughed.
It came out smaller than he intended—a short, slightly ruined sound that had nothing to do with humor and everything to do with the fact that his neck was still stiff and he had not eaten since before the training bout and the most dangerous thing he had ever built was apparently himself, which struck him, now that it had been said aloud by someone who meant it, as both obvious and completely absurd.
"Right," he said. "That's going in the log."
Sable's ear tilted. One degree. The feline equivalent of go on.
"Not the dangerous part." He reached past her for the towel he'd left on the secondary bench—rough grey utility cloth, oil-stained at one corner, the kind of thing you keep because it works and replace only when it stops. "The part where I spent fifteen years being careful about the wrong thing. I was tracking hardware. Keeping the proportions. Making sure the augments didn't show." He worked the towel across the back of his neck, where the connector interface always left a faint residual ache. "Didn't occur to me that the stubbornness was load-bearing."
"It rarely does," Sable said. "To the person carrying it."
She had moved, without appearing to move, to the edge of the workbench. She was pulling up something on the secondary diagnostic panel—not the Shroud's telemetry, which she already had running in the background out of habit, but the atmospheric log. Checking the rain data, probably. Or pretending to. She did this sometimes when she was letting him catch up to a thought without pressure: gave her hands something to occupy so her eyes could look somewhere else.
He appreciated it. He had never said so directly. He assumed she knew.
"The forearm piece took a hit six months ago," he said, nodding at the stack of segments. "Minor laceration on the housing. I never repaired it."
"I know."
"That's going to bother me now."
"It was bothering you before." She did not look up from the panel. "You just had something larger to bother you more."
He turned the towel over in his hands. Outside, the rain found a new register—heavier on the upper slit-windows, lighter against the stone, the Sanctum adjusting its acoustics around the weather the way it always did in this season. He had been in this room through enough winters that the sound was part of him now, catalogued somewhere below language, filed next to the smell of the old wood and the specific cooling-tick of the Case-Frame going dormant.
"We should go to the Cathedral tomorrow," he said. Not because anything specific had made him think of it. Because it had been a while, and because the thought arrived with the mild, slightly gravitational quality that his better instincts communicated in.
Sable closed the atmospheric log.
"Not tomorrow," she said. "Tonight."
She said it the way she said things when they weren't actually suggestions—flat, certain, already halfway to being a fact. Her tail had dropped from the discarded segment and was now moving in a slow, considered arc behind her, which meant she was thinking at operational rather than conversational speed.
He looked at her.
She met his look, and her expression was the particular one that meant: I will explain, but not all of it, and what I don't explain is not because I'm withholding—it's because I'm still finding the edge of it myself.
"Give me ten minutes," he said.
"You have eight." She had already turned toward the door. "Bring the good boots. The path will be wet and the stone on the south approach doesn't forgive."
He pulled the good boots from under the bench without arguing, which was its own kind of answer, and began to put them on, and the Case-Frame blinked its patient lurid tune at the empty stack of Shroud segments while the rain outside did what rain does when it has decided the night has somewhere to be.
