Chapter 11: Echoes in Smoke]

The music had died, but the pulse hadn’t.

It lingered in the walls, low and predatory—like the club itself still remembered the rhythm and refused to let it go. The last drops of sound shimmered through broken glass and shattered light rigs, each flicker syncing to the afterglow of violence. There was something obscene in the silence that followed PyCore—something too reverent. Like they’d just raided a cathedral of synthetic sin and left it breathless.

Crimson stood motionless in the haze, boots sunk ankle-deep in ash and debris. Sweat clung to his brow, cooling fast in the recycled air, while the barrel of his gun trembled ever so slightly. Not from fear. No... more like the echo of something ancient waking up in his bones. Something old and red and familiar.

Before him, Slipmath wheezed against a blood-slick pillar. The man looked like a broken marionette—one leg twisted under him, mouth slack with a laughter that didn’t know when to quit. His left eye fluttered in spasms, and the other locked onto Crimson with a lunatic clarity that made the air feel thinner.

“You... smell like copper and collapse,” he rasped, voice distorted like a corrupted vocoder.

Crimson said nothing.

Slipmath grinned wider. “They said you'd come. The... firewalker. The one with the mirror made of skin.”

From behind, Navi stepped into the scene, quiet but radiant. Her frame still flickered with psionic residue, tiny arcs of energy cracking at her fingertips like lightning refusing to settle. Her gaze swept the room—not at Slipmath, but at everything else. The ceiling beams still creaked. Some of the lighting rigs sparked overhead, throwing momentary spotlights across the carnage.

She muttered, “Residual energy’s off the charts... like the club itself was feeding off us.”

Crimson barely heard her. His focus tunneled on Slipmath. The man was trembling now, as if hearing a symphony no one else could.

“The glitch... the flame... they built you from failed timelines,” Slipmath whispered. “I heard you in my dreams, you know. Before the raid. A lullaby in static.”

Crimson lowered the gun just slightly. A breath. “Who’s ‘they’?”

Slipmath giggled. Coughed blood. “The ones who remember what the future should’ve been.”

Crimson’s jaw tightened. That wasn’t an answer. That wasn’t anything.

“You’re wasting our time,” Navi said coldly. Her voice felt like frost against the fire still burning in Crimson’s chest.

Slipmath leaned forward, bleeding from his ears now. “It’s all Echoes, all of it. There’s no source. No origin. Just... resonance.”

Crimson’s hands ached. His grip was slipping. Not from weakness, but from the exhaustion of restraint.

“You wanna keep babbling, or do you wanna make sense?” he growled.

Slipmath gave one last smile. “You ever wonder if you’re just a verse in someone else’s chorus?”

Slipmath is on his knees, beaten, bloodied, cornered in the pulsing aftermath of the nightclub chaos. Crimson’s got the gun drawn, hand trembling. Navi’s just behind, eyes fixed. The music is glitching out—PyCore echo still simmering like the world hasn't cooled down.

Slipmath spits blood. Smirks. Leans into the muzzle.

“You think she’s safe with you?”
A pause.
“You're strong, Crimson… but are you strong enough to stop me when I’m inside her?”

Click. Crack. Boom.

The shot echoes, louder than the music ever did.
Even Navi looked stunned—not in horror, but in the quiet ache of a lost lead.

Crimson lowered the gun slowly, hand shaking just enough to make him furious at himself.

The smoke curled like dying whispers.

Crimson stood still.

The barrel of the gun was still warm in his hand. Still pointed at where Slipmath had been. The man's body lay crumpled against the dancefloor tile, the final beat of PyCore glitching out through the shredded sound system.

A thump. Just one.

Not a body hitting the ground.

His heart.

Navi didn’t speak. Didn’t move. She only stared. Not at Slipmath. Not at the blood. At Crimson. Like she was trying to measure the weight he’d just inherited.

He lowered the weapon.

"I didn’t—"

He didn’t finish.

The flicker of Slipmath’s last words still clawed at the edge of his mind. That grin. That line.

"You're strong, Crimson… but are you strong enough to stop me when I’m inside her?"

The sound of the shot echoed again in his head. Louder than memory should allow.

He didn’t remember pulling the trigger. But he knew he had.

He felt it.

His hands were steady. That was the worst part.

He looked up at Navi again, but she wasn’t looking back anymore. She had turned away. Toward the door. Toward anywhere else.

"We need to move," she said, her voice cold. Too cold.

He wanted to ask—are you afraid of me now? But he didn’t. Couldn’t.

They walked through the ruin of the nightclub like ghosts. The lights flickered out. The adrenaline drained. And Slipmath’s corpse stayed behind.

Outside, the city didn't care. Neon still blinked. The sky was still scorched with false dawn. And somewhere, in some distant tower, someone else had just finished downloading the surveillance footage of what happened here.

The mission wasn’t over. But something else was.

Crimson didn’t feel like a soldier anymore.

He felt like a grave.
The apartment was too quiet.

After everything that had happened—the flashing lights, the reek of psion-burn, the skull-pop memory of Slipmath's last breath—the sudden return to silence was deafening. The air felt thinner now. The kind of silence that isn't peace, but aftermath. Na'vi stood just inside the apartment, her boots still dusty from the nightclub floor, her breath slow and measured like she was trying not to break.

Crimson didn't even cross the threshold. He lingered in the doorway like a ghost who wasn't sure if he was allowed to enter the land of the living. His frame blocked the half-light from the corridor, casting long shadows across the room that, hours earlier, had flickered with intimacy and warmth. Now? Now it felt like a haunted memory.

Na'vi turned slightly, just enough to catch him in the periphery. The way he stood there, not stepping forward, not retreating, spoke volumes. His body screamed conflict. It wasn’t just blood on his hands—it was doubt, guilt, grief. And she knew that posture. She'd seen it in fractured mirror code, in battle logs of soldiers who survived things they shouldn’t have.

He tried to speak. Tried to crack a line—something about Slipmath having it coming, or how at least the club's music stopped sucking—but the words died halfway through. He chuckled instead. It was the kind of chuckle you bury pain in. A fractured noise, sharp as broken glass.

"Don’t," she said gently. It wasn’t an order. It was a plea.

Crimson looked up, eyes unfocused. He opened his mouth to respond with another wisecrack—something inappropriate, probably, judging by the flicker in his eyes—but the words stalled. It was like his system lagged mid-sentence. Three full seconds passed. Unheard of for him.

And in those three seconds, Na'vi *saw* him. Not just the soldier. Not the protector. Not the man charged by her grandfather to keep her safe. She saw the boy underneath. The broken thing inside the brilliant machine. And it shattered her.

She didn’t run to him. She didn’t wrap him in comfort. That’s not who they were.

Instead, she turned her gaze back to the room. The soft pillows still bore the dent of their shared moment. The drinks were half-finished. The lighting dimmed to their preferences. Everything was still in place—but it all felt like a lie.

Crimson sank down right there in the threshold. Didn't say anything. Just sat with his back against the wall, one knee drawn up, head bowed.

And that, Na'vi thought, is where he chose to carry it. Not in words. Not in tears. In silence. In refusing to taint what they had built here.

She said nothing more. Just walked quietly past, into the bathroom. Let the door click gently behind her. She didn’t cry.

But she let the shower run cold.

Outside, through the window, the skyline flickered with morning static. The city had already forgotten what happened that night. But they hadn’t. They wouldn’t.

And tomorrow? Tomorrow would have to forgive them both.

The scent hit first. Cloves, sugarcane steam, charcoal-grilled citrus rind. It wrapped around the air like an old scarf, warm and frayed, tugging them toward the bustle. Crimson had already downed a cup of what vaguely resembled coffee, though it tasted like it had been filtered through a charred shoe. He hadn’t blinked since. Navi said nothing about it, but the microtwist at the edge of her smile said everything.

The farmer’s market was alive in that mid-morning way—somewhere between sleepy and simmering. Sunlight was broken through overhead ivy canopies strung with floating lanterns, and the whole bazaar pulsed like a breathing thing. Venders shouted across aisles in dialects old and half-invented, bartering lychee-spiked meatsticks and synth-grain flatbreads. A quartet of kids dashed through a puddle, laughter trailing like comet tails.

Neither of them had said much since leaving the apartment.

Crimson kept adjusting the strap of his shoulder holster, a tic he hadn’t noticed yet. Navi trailed her fingers across a crate of fruit—bright orbs with fuzzy ridges, the kind that looked like they'd talk back if you bit them wrong. She didn’t buy any. Not yet. She just watched them, as if looking for something in the texture.

"Still think this was a waste of time?" she asked, casually. But the weight behind it was anything but.

Crimson grunted. "Only if you count therapy a waste."

"That bad, huh?"

"The coffee? Or last night?"

She didn’t answer. Didn’t need to.

They stopped at a stall selling crushed flower oils and incense cubes that sizzled when lit. The vendor, an elderly woman with opalescent eyes, gave them a smile that looked like it had seen centuries. She slipped something into Navi’s palm without a word—a small charm etched with an unfamiliar sigil. Navi glanced at it, then at the woman, who only tilted her head as if listening to something just out of earshot.

"We should keep moving," Crimson muttered.

They passed by a stone fountain covered in moss and birdfruit offerings. At its edge, a man in a hooded robe sat peeling a strange root, slow and methodical. His eyes, when they flicked up, were the same shade as midnight through smoke.

Nothing was said.

Not yet.

Crimson reached for another bitter cup. Navi reached for his hand instead.
