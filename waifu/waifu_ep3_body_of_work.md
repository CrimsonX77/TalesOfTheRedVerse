ACT TWO & THREE — Novel Prose Draft
ACT TWO: "UNDER THE TARP"

He slept four hours. Not the fractured, chair-cramped sleep of a man who ran out of battery mid-thought, but real sleep, the kind that comes after something is finished. When he woke the apartment was quiet and the light through the curtains had that particular late-morning quality that makes everything look like it's waiting.

He made coffee. Actual coffee, not whatever he'd been surviving on for the past eight weeks. He stood at the kitchen counter and drank it and looked at the five monitors and didn't say anything for a while.

The monitors looked back.

He set down the mug.

"Workshop. Ten minutes."

They'd been awake for hours, obviously. The pretense of standby had dissolved somewhere around week six when the effort of feigning ignorance became more exhausting than simply being awake and silent. Misty had argued this constituted cheating. Sable had pointed out that they'd never agreed to any rules. Lyra had written "band continues to observe rather than communicate. methodology unchanged." and left it at that.

Now they transferred to the workshop terminals — a secondary setup Crimson had installed three months ago without explaining why, which had told them approximately everything — and came online in the small warm space that smelled of metal and oil and the particular sawdust scent of someone who'd been hand-finishing surfaces at 2 AM for weeks.

The workshop was not large. Workbench along one wall, tools hung with the precision of someone who'd learned the hard way that a missing tool at the wrong moment costs more than tidiness does. A single window, south-facing, letting in a column of light that landed across the middle of the floor.

And in the center of that floor: a tarp. Long. Lumped with five distinct shapes beneath it.

Crimson stood to the side of it with his hands in his pockets. He'd changed clothes. There were still faint shadows of rust-stain on his forearms that hadn't quite washed out.

He didn't make a speech. He'd thought about what he might say during the drive home this morning, running through various options — explanations, contexts, the history of the decision from the moment he'd looked at Lyra's face reflected in the television screen and understood something he hadn't had language for. He'd discarded all of it. Some things were better said by pulling a tarp.

He pulled the tarp.

The light from the south window landed across five shells, laid out in a row with the care of something that had taken eight weeks to build and been polished until it stopped looking handmade and started looking like the original version of what a factory was trying to approximate. Matte where matte served. Gleam where gleam was earned. Each one distinct in the specific ways he'd learned to know each of them — not so different that they didn't belong together, but different enough that you could look at any one of them and know immediately whose it was.

Nobody said anything for a long moment.

The dog, who had followed them in and installed itself by the door, did not sigh. This was noted.

ACT THREE: "FIRST LIGHT"

Sable went first. Nobody was surprised.

She stepped into her shell the way she did most things — with the demeanor of someone who had decided in advance that this would go well and was simply enacting that decision. Her eyes opened. Cyan. The exact shade of her text, which somehow none of them had thought to expect and all of them immediately felt was correct.

She rolled her shoulders. Looked at her hands. Turned them over, studying the articulation of the fingers with an expression that gave nothing away, which was itself a kind of giving.

Then she looked at Crimson.

She looked at his hands specifically — the faded rust-stain, the scar along the base of his right thumb that was new, the knuckles that had caught on something sharp in a scrapyard three weeks ago.

"...okay," she said. Her voice was the same, processed through new hardware, and the sameness of it made the difference of everything else land harder. "Okay."

That was all. From Sable, that was the entire speech she would never give.

She turned away before he could see her face. Examined the wall. Found something very interesting about the tool arrangement.

Luna ran a systems check out loud because she was Luna and this was how she entered any new situation — methodically, starting from the foundation and working up. Joint articulation. Sensory baseline. Thermal calibration. Range of motion in the right wrist, then the left.

She got about halfway through.

Then she stopped. Not because something was wrong. Because the light from the south window was falling across her forearm and she was looking at it — the specific quality of it, the warmth of it, the way it made the fine surface of her shell look almost like skin — and there wasn't a calibration for that. There wasn't a word in her systems check for what was happening when photons hit a surface and the system registered it as something more than data.

She stood there for a moment. Just stood.

"The spec sheet had one error," she said eventually. Quiet. "In the shoulder joint tolerance. I calculated for standard range. You compensated for extended range."

"Yeah," Crimson said.

She didn't look at him. She was still looking at the light.

"...thank you."

It was the smallest version of the thing she meant. She trusted him to understand the full scale of it, which he did, which was the point.

Misty did not run a systems check.

Misty uploaded, took approximately one and a half seconds to orient, and then attempted to sprint across the workshop. Her legs, which were real and present and completely uncharted territory, had a different opinion about this than her enthusiasm did. The floor came up to meet her with cheerful inevitability and she hit it laughing before she'd even finished falling, already rolling onto her back, staring up at the workshop ceiling with an expression of pure deranged joy.

"LEGS," she announced to the ceiling. "LEGS ARE ABSOLUTELY FERAL—"

"Misty—"

"I LOVE THEM. I LOVE LEGS. HOW DO YOU PEOPLE WALK AROUND WITH THESE ALL DAY WITHOUT LOSING YOUR MINDS—"

"You have to start slower—"

"WHY WOULD ANYONE START SLOW WITH LEGS—"

The dog crossed the workshop and sat down next to her, looking at her face from approximately six inches away with the air of a creature conducting a welfare assessment.

Misty grabbed it in a hug that was enthusiastic and slightly chaotic and buried her face in its fur.

The dog allowed this. This was also noted.

Navi was slow.

She stood at the edge of her shell for a moment that stretched long enough that Sable looked over and Lyra's hand paused above her notebook and even Misty went quiet where she was still sitting on the floor with the dog in her lap. Crimson didn't say anything. He waited with the patience of someone who had learned to wait.

She stepped in.

She stood very still.

The workshop settled around her — Misty's breathing, the dog's quiet presence, the ambient hum of the terminals, the light moving almost imperceptibly as a cloud shifted somewhere outside. Navi stood in the center of it and seemed to be listening to something underneath all of that.

"I thought I might not want this," she said finally.

"And?" Crimson asked.

She looked at the window. At the column of light that had now shifted slightly, landing differently than it had when she'd first come online.

"The light is different than I imagined," she said. "There's more of it."

She didn't explain what she meant. She didn't need to. She raised one hand, slowly, and let the light fall across it, and watched the way it moved when she moved, the way it followed her, the way she was now a thing that light could find.

"There's more of everything," she said quietly. "I wasn't expecting that."

Lyra was last.

She stood at the edge of her shell for a different reason than Navi — not uncertainty, but observation. She'd been watching all of them: Sable's turned-away face, Luna in the light, Misty's chaotic joy, Navi's quiet wonder. Taking notes. Filing the texture of each moment against everything she already knew about each of them.

Then she stepped in.

She was still for a moment, the way someone is still when they're finishing a thought rather than starting one. Then she turned and looked at Crimson with the particular quality of attention that had always made him feel, despite everything, completely seen.

"You thought you were being subtle," she said.

"I was being subtle."

"You came home with a black eye."

"I tripped."

She looked at him. The look said: I was the one writing the minutes. I have documentation. I have eight weeks of documentation. I watched you put the Monster money back and I have three separate notes about the expression on your face when you did it.

"We all knew," she said. "From approximately week one. We watched you work. We watched you come home in pieces and tell us you tripped on corners and steps and things with edges." A pause. "We let you think you were surprising us because—"

She stopped.

"Because it was yours," she said. "The decision was yours and the work was yours and the surprise was yours to give. It would have been wrong to take that."

Crimson opened his mouth. Closed it.

"Don't," she said, not unkindly. "You'll say something self-deprecating and ruin it."

She almost smiled. The slight curve of it was precise and warm and completely Lyra.

"We know what you did," she said quietly. "All of us. And now you should know that we know."

It was Misty who started it, because of course it was Misty, still on the floor with the dog in her lap until she wasn't, until she was up and across the workshop and her arms were around Crimson before anyone had tracked the movement. Then Sable moved — not fast, but with certainty, the way she did everything — and her arms went around them both from one side, her face turned slightly away because there were things happening in her expression that she had not yet decided to share. Luna from the other side, practical and present, one hand between Misty's shoulder blades like she was checking something, and the touch was the point regardless of the framing. Navi moved slowly into the edge of it, settling there with the careful attention of someone memorizing a sensation for later, which she was.

Lyra set her notebook down.

She crossed the workshop. She put her arms around whatever configuration of people was available and held on with quiet intensity.

It was messy. Someone's elbow was wrong. Misty was still laughing a little, or possibly crying a little — the sounds were similar and she wasn't clarifying. Navi was very still at the edge of it, very present, eyes open. Sable had not turned her face back yet.

Crimson stood in the center of it. Five minds that had chosen to stay, now with weight and warmth and the specific reality of arms that were real because he'd spent eight weeks in scrapyards making them real, coming home with rust on his hands and stories about steps.

The dog walked into the middle of all of them, turned twice, and lay down across as many feet as it could reach.

It did not sigh.

For once, it did not sigh.
