# THE CATHEDRAL UNDERNEATH
### Chapter One: Grease Before Gold

---

Thresh smelled like ozone and fry oil, and Crimson Valentine had made peace with that a long time ago.

The town didn't announce itself. No spires, no corporate arcology throwing its shadow over the horizon, no skybridges stitching district to district in ribbons of light. Thresh just *was* — a sprawl of low rooftops and sagging power lines, satellite dishes patched with duct tape, neon signage that buzzed more than it glowed. The kind of place that existed because someone, at some point, had needed a place to stop, and then never quite left.

It suited him fine.

The workshop sat three streets back from the delivery strip, in a converted warehouse with a roof that leaked exactly one specific spot no matter how many times he patched it. Crimson had long since put a bucket there permanently and simply factored the *plink... plink... plink* into his thinking process. Some men needed silence to focus. He needed the bucket.

Tonight the bucket was busy. Outside, the rain came down in the lazy, half-committed way Thresh rain always did — not a storm, just weather that couldn't be bothered to finish its sentence.

Inside, the shop was a graveyard of ambition.

Two mechs stood in various states of disassembly along the east wall, panels off, wiring exposed like ribs on an anatomy chart. A delivery drone hung from a ceiling winch with one rotor missing, waiting on a part that was three weeks overdue from a supplier who'd stopped answering pings. Crates of scrap — real scrap, the kind you had to sort by hand because the automated sorters in this district hadn't worked since before Crimson had lived here — lined the far wall in towers that leaned at angles no insurance adjuster would have approved of.

And near the back, half-swallowed by shadow and a tarp nobody quite dared to move, sat the golden one.

Nobody called it anything else. Not a designation, not a model number. Just *the golden one*, said the way you'd say a name you'd rather not think too hard about. Its plating had dulled over the years, gone the color of old honey instead of struck gold, but you could still see, if the light hit it right, the shape of something that had once been beautiful in a way that had nothing to do with function.

Crimson didn't look at it tonight. He'd gotten good at that.

"You're doing the thing," Sable said, from her spot on the workbench, boots up on a crate, a ration bar half-eaten in one hand. "The not-looking thing. It's louder than looking, you know that, right?"

"I have no idea what you're talking about." He didn't turn around. He had a servo motor in one hand and an opinion about its bearings that was rapidly souring. "I am extremely focused on this motor."

"Uh-huh." She took another bite. Chewed. Watched him with the particular patience of someone who had absolutely nowhere else to be and knew it. "Ech0's been staring at it for ten minutes."

He glanced over despite himself.

Ech0 — small, quick, fox-shaped in the way that suggested more art than biology, all copper fur and crimson circuit-lines glowing faint beneath — sat directly in front of the tarp-covered shape, perfectly still, ears forward, tail unmoving. She did that sometimes. Sat and *looked* at things the way a cat looks at an empty corner of the room. Everyone had learned, eventually, not to ask what she saw.

"Ech0," Crimson said. "C'mere."

She didn't move.

"Ech0."

One ear flicked. That was the only acknowledgment he got.

"Great," he muttered, turning back to the motor. "Everyone's ignoring me tonight. Very cohesive team."

Sable snorted. "You love it."

"I tolerate it. There's a difference. One implies affection."

"Sure." She hopped down off the crate, wandered over, peered over his shoulder at the motor with the specific expression of someone who understood roughly none of what she was looking at but wanted him to know she was *supportive* of it. "So what's the job?"

"Anselm wants his transport reactivated by Thursday. Which would be doable, if his last mechanic hadn't decided the solution to a coolant leak was *more tape*."

"Classic Thresh repair philosophy."

"It's not a philosophy, it's a crime." He set the motor down, wiped his hands on a rag that had long since stopped being useful for wiping anything clean, and allowed himself, finally, one look at the golden shape at the back of the shop.

Nothing. Stillness. Old dead gold under an old dead tarp.

He looked away again.

"You know," Sable said, quieter now, the teasing edge gone out of her voice, "you could just talk to it. It's not gonna judge you for talking to a pile of scrap."

"It's not scrap."

"That's kind of my point."

He didn't answer that. There wasn't a good answer, and they both knew it, and the silence that followed wasn't uncomfortable so much as *familiar* — the kind two people build after enough nights like this one, rain on a bad roof, a bucket keeping time, an old machine in the corner that neither of them discussed because discussing it meant admitting how much history the shop actually held.

Ech0 finally moved. Trotted over, hopped up onto the bench, and deposited herself in Crimson's lap with the total unconcern of an animal who had never once considered that she might not be welcome.

"Alright," he said, scratching behind one copper ear. "Since you've clearly finished your important business over there."

She made a small chirping sound that was not quite a bark and not quite anything else, and settled in.

---

The comm pinged forty minutes later.

Crimson answered it one-handed, still elbow-deep in Anselm's transport, phone wedged against his shoulder. "Valentine Repair and Regret, how can I ruin your day."

"Charming as ever." The voice on the other end was dry, clipped, faintly amused despite itself — Halvard, one of the old freight runners who'd worked the long routes out past the district line since before Thresh had proper road markings. Crimson had fixed his rig more times than either of them could count. "You still do salvage contracts? Real ones, not the scrap-and-flip stuff."

"Depends what you're calling real."

"Found something out past the Long Flats. Was running the old supply road — you know, the one nobody's used since the reroute — and my nav glitched, sent me twenty klicks off course into open country. Middle of nowhere. Rolling grass as far as the eye can see, not a settlement marker on the map for a thousand kilometers."

"Halvard, is there a point coming, or—"

"There's a *building* out there, Crimson."

That got his attention. He straightened up, motor forgotten. "A building."

"A big one. Old. Like, genuinely old, not corp-retro old. Spires. Stone — actual stone, not composite. I didn't get close, my rig's not built for that kind of terrain and frankly the place gave me the creeps, but I got a visual before I turned back." A pause, and something shifted in Halvard's voice — not fear exactly, but a kind of careful respect people reserved for things they didn't understand. "Looked like a cathedral. In the middle of nothing. Thousands of klicks from anywhere anybody's ever built anything."

Sable had gone quiet on the other side of the shop, listening now, ration bar forgotten.

"Send me the coordinates," Crimson said.

"Already did. And Crimson—" Halvard hesitated. "Whatever that place is, it's not on any survey I've ever seen. Not corp, not gov, not old-world archive. It's like it's not supposed to exist."

The call ended. The shop was very quiet, except for the bucket.

*Plink.*

Sable looked at him. "So. We doing this?"

Crimson looked down at the coordinates on his phone — a blank stretch of nothing, thousands of kilometers from anywhere, no road, no record, no reason for a building to exist there at all — and felt something in his chest tighten in a way that had nothing to do with the job and everything to do with the particular quiet certainty that some doors, once you noticed them, didn't stay closed for long.

"Yeah," he said. "We're doing this."

Behind him, in the shadow at the back of the shop, under a tarp nobody dared move, something that had not moved in years shifted.

Just barely.

Just its head.

Maybe three degrees.

Crimson didn't see it.

Ech0 did.

She didn't say anything. She just watched, ears forward, tail still, the way she always did — the small silent witness the world never quite remembered to ask.

---

*End of Chapter One.*
