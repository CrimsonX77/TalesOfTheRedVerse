REDVERSE OVA: THE CATHEDRAL UNDERNEATH
Working story bible / continuity brief
1. What this story actually is

A multi-chapter RedVerse archaeological adventure with an Indiana Jones flavour, set in the neon-arcane cyberfuture.

It begins in the mundane, greasy, lived-in world of Thresh, then expands into a long expedition across thousands of kilometres of largely untouched countryside, eventually reaching an ancient, overgrown cathedral standing alone amid vast rolling meadows.

Beneath that cathedral is an ancient vault.

The vault contains the remnants of a civilization that discovered ways to preserve consciousness and memory, but ultimately chose stasis rather than continued existence.

The surface adventure is archaeology.

The deeper story is about memory, preservation, identity, consent, mortality, and whether something should be awakened simply because you can.

And beneath that is a much larger RedVerse continuity thread that should not be explained in this story.

The story should work perfectly as an adventure for somebody encountering it cold.

A knowledgeable reader may discover that it connects to much older events.

An obsessive future reader / ARG hunter may discover that it connects to things we haven't even revealed yet.

That hidden continuity is intentional.

2. Where it comes from

This story grew out of the recent sequence:

Redverse OVA: Skyhold
→ Full Glow
→ Snack-is-faction

Those pieces established a broader emotional range for Crimson and Sable:

Skyhold = quiet companionship / staying.

Full Glow = vulnerability / emotional connection / holding someone's hand / trying to bridge distance.

Snack-is-faction = playful physicality / intimacy / rhythmic chaos.

The archaeological story doesn't need to continue that sequence directly, but it exists in the same increasingly mature RedVerse.

It also emerged from remembering older songs:

Navigating My Cup / Same Time Next Apocalypse
and
Still Smoking

Those older songs established a Crimson/Sable relationship through two perspectives, particularly the idea of intimacy occurring after violence and the fear of whether either person will stay afterward.

Then we rediscovered FRACTURE, from June 2025, which represents an older, much more aggressive articulation of Crimson's identity:

fracture, recursion, synthetic identity, machine-heart, self-authorship, rage, transformation, breaking cages and writing the page oneself.

These aren't discarded works.

They remain part of the private RedVerse archive.

3. The RedVerse itself

The RedVerse is not a linear franchise where old stories become obsolete when newer stories appear.

Nothing is discarded.

Stories may stop being actively told, but they remain perfectly preserved in Crimson's private world.

The world has accumulated eras.

Blue Skies Saga came first among the particularly relevant older histories.

Then came:

Blindblood → Mythopunk → Mechaedo → numerous other tales → present RedVerse.

There is therefore a huge amount of elapsed subjective history between Blue Skies and this story.

Even avid readers may not remember every callback.

That is intentional.

The audience is allowed to forget.

The world doesn't.

This is important to the storytelling philosophy.

A callback should not announce itself.

A historical object should be able to sit quietly in the background for years.

Then, someday, somebody notices.

4. Thresh

Thresh is home base.

It is a backwater town, far from the glamorous centres of the cyberfuture.

Crimson operates a delivery service / mechanical engineering scrap shed there.

The workshop is greasy, cluttered, practical and lived-in.

It contains:

half-built mecha,

machines in various states of ruin,

components,

scrap,

tools,

delivery equipment,

things Crimson insists are still repairable,

and things everyone else has correctly identified as probably dangerous.

The tone is:

“Don't touch that.”

followed shortly by:

“...aw hell.”

This is where many RedVerse stories begin.

It gives the universe a mundane anchor.

Before the mythic machinery, there is work.

Before the ancient vault, there is grease.

Before the cosmic mystery, there is Crimson fixing some stupid machine.

Do not rush through Thresh.

The workshop should feel like somewhere the characters genuinely live.

5. The golden mecha

This is critically important.

There is an old golden mecha sitting in Crimson's workshop.

It is a direct continuity object from Blue Skies Saga.

Its history is enormous.

Very compressed version:

Crimson served aboard a US battle-fleet Navi ship and had secretly been charged with protecting Navi without her knowledge.

The ship was attacked by raiders.

Crimson and Navi escaped aboard the prototype MechaNavi, but watched the ship explode. Navi's grandfather, the admiral, was aboard.

This led into a depression period, a badass cantina gunfight, and an investigation spanning the solar system.

They eventually discovered a resonance device capable of opening a portal into a realm where existence itself was based on certainty.

In that realm, uncertainty and entropy essentially dissolved beings.

Crimson survived because he was fundamentally incapable of becoming certain about anything.

Navi attempted to save inhabitants who were disappearing and created increasingly elaborate devices, eventually constructing the Hall of Mirrors, effectively a beautiful prison for souls.

Their arcs eventually collided.

Navi, the Queen of Ghosts, and Crimson, the Red Wanderer, fought beneath a dissolving world.

Navi used Crimson's affection for her against him, driving a blade through him.

His soul began slipping out.

They were eventually returned to the workshop.

Navi desperately attempted to save Crimson.

His physical body died during the transfer into a mech.

The mech awakened.

It was Crimson, but not quite.

Cleaner.

More efficient.

More mechanically capable.

More optimized.

Everyone loved the machine-form.

Navi was uneasy.

Later, rumours spread of a golden dragon flying beneath the blue skies.

The dragon was the spirit/flesh form of Crimson.

The machine was the machine-form of Crimson.

They ultimately fought over sole existence on that plane.

Navi eventually stepped between them.

The flesh/spirit Crimson used the opportunity to remove the machine's central gem.

The golden machine fell.

It landed with a DOOM that rewrote history.

And since then, the golden machine has remained in the workshop.

Much later, Navi is no longer there.

Sable is.

This is ancient history by the time of the present story.

The golden machine therefore should not be treated as a fresh plot device.

It is an old wound that became part of the furniture.

6. The golden machine's role in this story

This is one of the most important tonal constraints.

The golden machine does not become active.

It does not speak.

It does not reveal itself.

It does not become a boss.

It does not suddenly glow.

It does not explain Blue Skies.

It does not give Crimson a quest.

It does not become the key to the vault.

At most:

its head moves approximately three degrees.

Then it remains lifeless.

That's it.

For most readers, this might mean nothing.

For someone who remembers Blue Skies, it may produce:

“...wait.”

For someone who remembers the entire saga:

“Oh fuck.”

Crimson notices.

His response is:

“No.”

That is enough.

The meaning is deliberately left unexplained.

The objective truth is that the machine is connected to what is happening and will eventually come alive / participate in the larger continuity.

But this story does not say that.

The story leaves a trail.

This is an ARG seed.

The audience should be able to discover later that multiple seemingly unrelated things were connected.

The narrative itself should never wink at the camera.

7. The journey

The vault is not beneath Thresh.

This is important.

It lies thousands of kilometres away from civilization.

The destination is an ancient, overgrown cathedral standing alone in enormous rolling meadows.

The journey there should take multiple chapters.

Do not treat travel as filler.

The journey gives the audience time to live with Crimson, Sable and Ech0.

It allows:

conversation,

quiet,

humour,

small discoveries,

weather,

camping,

repairing equipment,

strange roadside ruins,

old settlements,

little mysteries,

and glimpses of the wider RedVerse.

The contrast is deliberate:

Thresh = greasy, mechanical, crowded, lived-in.

The journey = enormous, open, quiet.

The cathedral = ancient, abandoned, uncanny.

The vault = increasingly impossible.

The story should progressively widen its aperture.

8. The cathedral

The cathedral is ancient and overgrown.

It sits alone in rolling meadows, thousands of kilometres from civilization.

Nature has reclaimed it.

Trees grow through parts of the structure.

Roots penetrate the foundations.

Broken stained glass lies among grass.

Weathered statues remain.

The bell tower is damaged.

The place should initially feel like a genuinely abandoned religious ruin.

But slowly, inconsistencies appear.

Architecture that doesn't fit the apparent age.

Symbols that don't quite belong.

Evidence that later civilizations used or modified the site.

Perhaps signs that somebody has visited more recently.

The cathedral itself becomes an archaeological mystery before the actual vault is ever reached.

The audience should spend considerable time exploring it.

The cathedral is not merely the entrance hallway.

It is a location with its own history.

Eventually they discover that the cathedral was constructed above / around something much older.

It was effectively built to conceal or protect the structure beneath it.

The cathedral is the lid.

The vault is what is underneath.

9. The vault

The vault belongs to an ancient civilization.

The important thing is:

this was not originally a treasure vault.

It contains preserved consciousness, memories, personalities and fragments of a civilization.

The deeper characters go, the more the place stops feeling like an archaeological site and starts feeling like an enormous preserved life.

The story should reveal this gradually.

Small human details matter:

a cup,

a child's drawing,

a name,

a repaired object,

an unfinished task,

a personal argument,

a piece of music,

ordinary evidence that the people weren't simply “ancient beings.”

They were people.

The civilization eventually discovered that preserving consciousness indefinitely could become a form of imprisonment.

They chose stasis.

Not necessarily death.

Not necessarily annihilation.

A deliberate refusal to continue.

This distinction matters.

The vault is therefore not a tomb waiting for heroes to resurrect it.

It is a civilization that made a choice.

10. The ethical conflict

A modern corporate archaeological/extraction team eventually becomes involved.

Initially, they should not be cartoon villains.

They genuinely believe the technology represents an opportunity to preserve consciousness and perhaps save people.

Their reasoning should make sense.

Their mistake is assuming:

“If we can preserve them, we should.”

The deeper truth is:

They chose not to continue.

The conflict becomes:

Does discovering something give you the right to activate it?

The corporation gradually becomes increasingly unwilling to accept the vault's refusal.

The more warnings they encounter, the more desperate the corporate representative becomes.

Eventually the representative breaks.

Not because he is secretly evil from page one.

Because he becomes convinced that the ancient civilization is making an irrational decision and that he knows better.

Eventually:

he activates the machine despite the warnings.

That's the catastrophic turning point.

11. The ghosts

When the machine activates, consciousness begins manifesting.

Not necessarily as horror ghosts.

They are data made visible.

Human forms.

Fragments of personality.

Memories.

Faces.

Warmth.

Recognition.

This should initially be beautiful.

That makes the catastrophe hurt more.

Among the manifestations, Crimson sees a particular figure.

A face he should know.

He does not know her.

But something inside him reacts immediately.

His eyes fill with tears.

He reaches toward her.

She somehow recognizes him.

There should be no clean explanation here.

Is she somebody from his past?

Someone connected to the larger RedVerse?

A reconstructed memory?

A fragment?

Someone he knows but cannot consciously remember?

Do not answer this in the story.

The uncertainty is intentional.

She reaches the shutdown mechanism.

Crimson realizes what she is about to do.

He tries to reach her.

She activates the final shutdown.

A wall of solid light erupts.

Crimson's arm is caught in it.

He does not flinch.

He does not even register the injury.

He continues trying to reach the warm, smiling face.

Then she disappears.

The system shuts down.

Only afterward does Crimson realize his arm is gone.

12. The cybernetic arm

After this story, Crimson permanently has a cybernetic arm.

This is not cosmetic.

It is a continuity consequence.

The arm is a physical scar from the encounter.

Do not make the loss melodramatic in the moment.

The emotional point is that Crimson doesn't care about his own injury while someone he somehow recognizes is disappearing.

That tells us more about him than a speech could.

Later stories can reference the arm casually.

The story itself doesn't need to make a huge deal of it.

It becomes another piece of Crimson's accumulated history.

13. The deeper thematic structure

There are several overlapping themes.

Preservation vs. freedom

Is keeping someone alive always an act of love?

Can preservation become imprisonment?

Can immortality become another form of death?

Memory vs. identity

If a personality is reconstructed from memories, is it still the same person?

What happens when you recognize someone who doesn't exist in your conscious memory?

Uncertainty

Blue Skies established Crimson's peculiar relationship with uncertainty.

The certainty realm could not easily destroy him because he could never become completely certain.

This new story shouldn't directly revisit that mechanic.

But philosophically, it continues the question:

What do we do when we cannot know the truth?

Crimson's answer remains fundamentally Crimson:

He does not need certainty before he cares.

Consent

The ancient civilization chose stasis.

The corporation believes that technological capability overrides that choice.

That's the moral conflict.

The persistence of history

The RedVerse doesn't forget.

People do.

Readers do.

Characters change.

Stories disappear from the active surface.

But the world retains its history.

14. The hidden RedVerse / ARG layer

This is crucial.

Everything is connected.

But the story must never say so.

The RedVerse is intended to reward long-term observation.

Objects, lyrics, visual motifs, dialogue, locations, symbols and tiny physical movements can form trails across completely different stories.

A future ARG hunter should be able to discover:

“Wait, this symbol was in the cathedral.”

Then:

“It's in an old song.”

Then:

“It's visible in an unrelated OVA.”

Then:

“That character said something strange about it.”

Then:

“The golden machine moved.”

And eventually:

a new story emerges from the evidence.

The creator knows the complete history.

The audience reconstructs it.

This is one of the reasons the Outer Wilds comparison is interesting. Outer Wilds famously makes knowledge and inference central to progression, with small mysteries feeding into a larger “web of knowledge.”

But the RedVerse isn't trying to reproduce its game mechanics.

The relevant principle is:

Don't explain what the audience can discover.

15. Pacing philosophy

The story should breathe.

Do not rush from clue → dungeon → vault → apocalypse.

The progression should feel roughly like:

Thresh

↓

Preparation

↓

Long journey

↓

Quiet wilderness

↓

First ruins

↓

Remote settlement / environmental history

↓

Cathedral arrival

↓

Exploration of cathedral

↓

Cathedral mystery

↓

Evidence of another expedition

↓

Discovery of concealed structure

↓

Descent

↓

Ancient civilization

↓

Human details

↓

Ethical realization

↓

Corporate expedition arrives / conflict develops

↓

Warnings ignored

↓

Activation

↓

Beautiful ghost manifestations

↓

Crimson sees the unknown familiar face

↓

Shutdown

↓

Light

↓

Lost arm

↓

Escape

↓

Return to Thresh

And only afterward do we allow the golden machine to quietly become significant.

The emotional rhythm should alternate:

wonder → mundane humour → curiosity → unease → beauty → danger → intimacy → horror → quiet aftermath.

Don't make everything ominous.

The quiet parts are what make the catastrophic parts hurt.

16. The emotional texture

This should feel warm, immersive and lived-in.

That's one of the central things you specifically want preserved.

Crimson and Sable should feel like people travelling together rather than exposition delivery mechanisms.

Let them:

eat,

sleep,

repair things,

complain,

joke,

get distracted,

argue,

sit quietly,

look at scenery,

notice tiny things.

Ech0 should exist naturally in the world.

Not as a designated mascot.

She's simply Ech0.

Sometimes adorable.

Sometimes inconvenient.

Sometimes inexplicably useful.

Sometimes a fox-shaped liability.

🦊

The RedVerse should feel inhabited even when nothing important is happening.

17. What this story should NOT become

Do not make it:

“Ancient civilization secretly caused everything.”

Do not make the vault a generic superweapon.

Do not make the corporation stupid from the beginning.

Do not make Crimson a chosen one who was destined to find it.

Do not explain the ghost.

Do not explain the golden machine.

Do not explicitly connect this to Blue Skies.

Do not make the golden machine awaken dramatically.

Do not turn every callback into an Easter egg with a spotlight.

Do not make every mystery resolve.

Do not make the ARG clues required to understand the surface story.

And most importantly:

Don't make the world feel like it was constructed solely for Crimson.

The ancient civilization existed before him.

The cathedral existed before him.

The meadow existed before him.

The ghosts have histories that do not revolve around him.

Crimson is an explorer entering an already-existing world.

That's what makes the discovery feel real.

18. The core dramatic question

Underneath all the archaeology and cyberpunk machinery, the story is asking:

If you discovered someone had chosen to remain asleep, would you have the humility to leave them there?

The corporation answers:

“We can save them.”

The vault answers:

“We chose this.”

Crimson eventually understands:

“Then we don't get to choose for you.”

And that is why the shutdown matters.

Not because Crimson wins.

Because somebody else's choice is respected.

Even when it hurts.

Especially when it hurts.

19. The hidden second question

The story also quietly asks:

What if the person you are desperately reaching for is someone you somehow remember without remembering?

That question should remain unresolved.

It can become a future thread.

The audience may eventually discover who that person was.

Or they may discover that their assumption was wrong.

Either way, the story itself should leave the wound open.

20. The final image

I think the eventual return to Thresh should be deliberately quiet.

No triumphant music.

No “we solved the mystery.”

Crimson is back.

He's alive.

He's got one arm less than when he left.

Sable is there.

Ech0 is there.

The workshop is still greasy.

The half-built mecha are still half-built.

The world has continued.

The golden machine is still sitting exactly where it was.

Maybe Crimson pauses beside it.

Maybe its head has moved those three degrees.

Maybe not.

He doesn't touch it.

He just looks.

Then:

“Don't.”

And he walks past.

Cut.

The audience knows something happened.

They don't know everything.

The world continues.

And somewhere underneath an abandoned cathedral, an ancient civilization remains exactly where it chose to remain.

For now.

That, to me, is the North Star for writing the actual story.

The surface experience should be:

“Holy shit, what a strange beautiful archaeological adventure.”

The emotional experience should be:

“I cared about these people.”

The deeper RedVerse experience should be:

“Wait. I've seen this before.”

And the eventual ARG experience should be:

“...Jesus fucking Christ. It was all there.”

That's the architecture. 🐉⛪🌾⚙️

And importantly, we don't need to write toward the ARG. We write the story honestly. The clues exist because they're true in the world, not because we're planting puzzles. The puzzle is merely what happens when somebody eventually notices that the world has been telling the truth the whole time.
