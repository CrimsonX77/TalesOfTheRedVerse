# ANOMALY WARDENS

## Narrative Truth Ledger v1.0

---

# CORE STORY IDENTITY

This is NOT primarily a superhero story.

This is NOT primarily a conspiracy story.

This is NOT primarily a chosen-one story.

Those elements exist, but they are not the heart.

At its core, Anomaly Wardens is about:

* Connection versus Control
* Growth versus Trauma
* Restoration versus Exploitation
* Humanity versus Optimization
* The people who choose to remain themselves after suffering

The conspiracy, powers, battles, and mysteries all serve these themes.

---

# THE READER'S FIRST HOOK

The first major mystery is NOT:

* What are anomalies?
* Who are the Wardens?
* What caused the world to become this way?

The first hook is:

"Why did Crimson react to Dave?"

The reader should become curious about Dave because Crimson becomes curious about Dave.

The mystery enters through character rather than exposition.

---

# DAVE'S NARRATIVE FUNCTION

Dave is the primary POV protagonist.

This is important.

Crimson may appear more powerful, influential, knowledgeable, and mythic.

But Dave is the lens through which much of the world is experienced.

Dave allows the audience to discover the setting naturally.

Dave does NOT initially know:

* What he is
* Why he matters
* Why Crimson noticed him
* Why Oracle sent scouts

Dave simply lives his life.

The audience learns alongside him.

---

# DAVE'S THEMATIC FUNCTION

Dave represents restoration.

Not invention.

Not destruction.

Not domination.

Restoration.

Core motif:

"They stopped understanding the language."

Dave instinctively reconnects systems that have lost communication.

This applies to:

* Machines
* Technology
* Organizations
* People
* Relationships
* Potentially society itself

Dave's ability is likely an expression of healing rather than power.

---

# CRIMSON'S NARRATIVE FUNCTION

Crimson is NOT the main protagonist.

Crimson functions as:

* Catalyst
* Recruiter
* Bridge character
* Mythic supporting lead

Readers experience Crimson from the outside before understanding him.

This makes him larger-than-life while preserving mystery.

He should often feel:

* Brilliant
* Friendly
* Weirdly approachable
* Slightly impossible

---

# IMPORTANT CRIMSON TRAIT

Crimson is interested in everything.

Therefore:

If Crimson becomes dismissive about something:

Everyone notices.

Immediately.

Because it is profoundly out of character.

A simple:

"Fine."

from Crimson should feel like a seismic event to people who know him.

---

# THE WARDENS

The Wardens are:

* Unsanctioned
* Unfunded
* Semi-mythical
* Operate from shadows
* Recruit anomalies
* Function as a found family

The reader should fall in love with the group dynamic before understanding the organization.

Introduce relationships first.

Lore second.

---

# WARDEN THEMATIC DESIGN

All major Warden abilities involve perception.

Crimson:
Perceives systems, motion, patterns, combat vectors.

Ghostwire:
Perceives networks and data pathways.

Oracle:
Perceives future possibilities.

Riot:
Perceives structural weaknesses and friction points.

Hex:
Perceives trajectories and outcomes.

Dave:
Perceives broken communication and restoration pathways.

The team is unified through different ways of seeing reality.

---

# ORACLE

Oracle is NOT manipulative.

Oracle is NOT withholding information for amusement.

Oracle is ancient, calm, maternal, and trusted.

The team relies on her because:

If Oracle believes something is important, it is.

If Oracle says nothing, there is usually a reason.

Her wisdom is trusted.

---

# HEX

Hex should remain dangerous, seductive, chaotic, and slightly alarming.

However:

Her relationship with Crimson only works because there is mutual understanding beneath the madness.

The audience should gradually realize that what appears reckless often contains deep trust.

---

# NARRATIVE PACING RULE

The mythology should be discovered.

Not delivered.

Act 1 should raise questions.

Act 2 should reveal mechanisms.

Act 3 should reveal truth.

Do not reveal answers as soon as they are invented.

Protect mystery.

---

# NAVI

To the audience:

Navi is first introduced as:

Crimson's lost childhood friend.

Nothing more.

This innocence is crucial.

The audience must love Navi as a person before learning her significance.

---

# THE SECRET OF NAVI

Unknown to most characters:

Navi is a genetic hybrid experiment.

She possesses a unique marking phenomenon.

The mark creates potential.

The mark itself does not create monsters.

The mark amplifies what people become.

---

# CORPORATE MISUNDERSTANDING

The corporations misunderstand Navi's gift.

They believe:

Power comes from suffering.

They therefore manufacture suffering.

Their process:

1. Identify marked individuals.
2. Monitor them.
3. Destroy support systems.
4. Remove loved ones.
5. Create trauma.
6. Force awakening.
7. Weaponize outcome.
8. Use resulting anomalies as justification for control.

This is the central evil of the setting.

---

# THE GREAT LIE

The public believes:

Anomalies are dangerous.

The corporations provide safety.

Reality:

The corporations manufacture anomalies through suffering.

Then profit from public fear.

---

# THE PHILOSOPHICAL CONFLICT

Corporations believe:

Trauma creates power.

Navi's existence suggests:

Growth creates power.

The entire setting revolves around this ideological conflict.

---

# LYRA

Lyra represents retained humanity inside the system.

She is proof that not everyone involved became a monster.

She cared for Navi.

She approved of Crimson.

She recognized the positive influence they had on one another.

She serves as moral contrast to the corporation.

---

# WHY CRIMSON MATTERS

The corporations failed to understand something:

Navi's greatest growth occurred through connection.

Not suffering.

Crimson represents the path they could never control.

---

# THE ROOFTOP SCENE

The rooftop scene is NOT about winning a fight.

The rooftop scene is NOT about defeating a villain.

The rooftop scene is the thematic climax of an entire worldview.

The villain believes:

Pain inevitably creates monsters.

Crimson proves him wrong.

By refusing to kill him.

---

# "NAVI WOULD HAVE BEEN PROUD OF YOU"

This line is sacred.

Do not weaken it.

Do not overuse it.

The line works because:

Tank is not praising the action.

He is praising the person.

The meaning is:

"You remained yourself."

The entire story should quietly build toward making this moment earned.

---

# THE FINAL THEMATIC STATEMENT

The corporations attempt to prove:

Suffering defines people.

The story attempts to prove:

Choice defines people.

Everything should ultimately serve this conflict.

---

# STORY MOTTO

Nos Sumus Quod Restat.

We are what remains.

Not what was lost.

Not what was broken.

What remains.

Cyberpunk Anime Action / Found Family / Reality-Bending Thriller with romantic tension, dark humor, and deep tragedy.

Core Premise
In a near-future where reality itself has begun to fray at the edges, certain individuals manifest Anomalies — dangerous, uncontrolled reality-warping abilities that defy conventional physics. An underground blacksite crew known as the Wardens operates outside any official agency. Their mission is to locate, recruit, and protect these Anomalies before shadowy rival forces (a sterile corporate cult or rival blacksite unit known as the Cleansers) can hunt them down and erase them from existence.
Not every mission succeeds. Some Anomalies are lost to the enemy, some burn out from their own powers, some choose to run, and some are consumed by the very reality they warp. These failures are not side plots — they are the emotional core of the story. The Wardens must keep recruiting and fighting while carrying the ghosts of those they couldn’t save, turning pain into legacy (“signal not static”).
POV & Protagonist
Primary POV: Dave — a disgruntled, cynical mechanic freshly recruited after his latent powers manifest.
Dave’s gift appears mundane on the surface: with nothing but his trusty wrench, he can make any broken machine function against all odds. His words, however, cut deeper — a well-placed sentence can unmake machinery, unravel plans, or destabilize reality itself. He serves as the grounded everyman and audience surrogate: equal parts awed, sarcastic, and increasingly heartbroken as he witnesses the human cost of the Wardens’ work. His arc moves from reluctant outsider to someone who understands the true price of fighting for the broken.

# ANOMALY WARDENS
## Chapter One — Dave

---

The rain had been falling since afternoon, the kind that had given up pretending it would stop. It hammered the corrugated roof of the shop in steady, tired rhythms, dripping through the two leaks he'd stopped pretending to fix properly. He had buckets. That was his fix. Some problems were only worth managing.

The clock on his workbench read 3:14 AM.

The shop was small — tucked between a noodle stall that never seemed to close and a pawn shop that definitely dealt in more than watches — and it held the specific smell of old solder, machine oil, and whatever the noodle place was cooking tonight. The sign outside still read REPAIRS – HONEST WORK in flickering blue neon. The "HONEST" had been half-burned out for three months. Dave had never replaced it. The people who came here weren't looking for honesty. They were looking for cheap and fast. Mostly they were looking for someone who wouldn't ask questions about where the broken thing had been.

He'd heard enough not-questions in seven years of this to have developed a genuine immunity to the interesting backstory.

The drone that sat on his workbench now had been dropped off at eleven by a corporate suit who'd been too clean and too twitchy to pass for the kind of person who usually walked into this part of the undercity. Mid-level logistics, if Dave had to guess. The kind of company that ran a hundred courier drones and considered each one expendable. The kind that only came looking for a repair shop like this one when the math stopped working out in favor of a replacement.

"Three other shops," the suit had said, holding the drone out at arm's length like it had wronged him personally. "Said it was bricked beyond recovery."

Dave had taken it without comment. Standard firmware-over-hardware failure — someone had pushed an update through without verifying compatibility and fried the motor control architecture. The result was that every component was technically functional and the system as a whole was completely incoherent. They weren't talking to each other. They'd stopped understanding the language.

The suit had watched him turn it over in his hands, and for a second too long, he'd looked at Dave with the kind of expression that took a moment to place. Not suspicion. Not impatience. Something quieter than that. Like he was trying to figure out what he was looking at.

"Got it near the Harwick incident," the suit said finally, like he needed to say it to someone. "If that matters."

Dave had glanced up. "Does it affect the hardware?"

"...No."

"Then it doesn't matter."

He'd set the drone on the bench and started writing up the intake form, and after another few seconds of that look, the suit had left. Dave had already forgotten about him by the time the door closed.

He had the wall screen on — had it on most nights, for the noise — but he kept it muted with subtitles crawling at the bottom of the frame. Some local news segment was cycling through: traffic rerouting in the central district, a city councillor saying something that required three graphics behind him to look meaningful, and then the kind of segment that always made the rotation these days. ANOMALY ACTIVITY IN MID-CITY, read the subtitle, white letters on urgent red. WARDENS RESPONSE STILL UNCONFIRMED. There was shaky handheld footage of something that made the camera operator step backward fast, then a cut to a spokesperson arranging familiar words into a familiar order.

Dave picked up his remote and killed the screen.

He'd heard enough about Anomalies to have developed a genuine immunity to that too.

The drone sat there dead for most of the night. He picked it up around one, set it down, picked it up again at two. Something about the job felt like it needed the extra hour to get ready, which was the kind of thought that didn't make sense in any practical way, so he didn't examine it.

At 3:14 he finally sat down properly, pulled the lamp over, and got to work.

The wrench was the old one. Scuffed handle, electrical tape worn thin over the grip, the kind of tool that would have looked like junk to anyone who didn't know what they were looking at. He'd had it since his third year in the shop and had replaced everything else at least twice. Not that one. It wasn't sentimental. He just kept reaching for it when things were serious.

He opened the casing and looked at the interior for a long moment.

"Alright," he said, to no one in particular. To the drone, maybe. "Let's see what your problem actually is."

The problem, as best he could tell from the state of the circuits, was that every component was technically functional and the system as a whole had completely forgotten how to be a drone. The firmware update had gone through and taken the motor handshake architecture with it. Nothing wrong with any individual part. They'd just stopped talking to each other. They'd stopped understanding the language.

Dave moved the wrench to a specific connector, thought about it for a second, and said: "You're going to work now."

He didn't say it like a request.

Twenty minutes later, the status light climbed from dead red to soft, healthy blue. The rotors twitched once — a test pattern, startup cycle — then settled into a quiet, even hum.

Dave leaned back on his stool and looked at it.

He'd gotten used to this part a long time ago. The part where the machine that everyone else had written off just listened. He didn't think of it as a gift. He thought of it as knowing how to talk to things that had forgotten what they were supposed to be doing. His hands knew the shape of broken things. Some machines just responded better to him.

Some people were good with people. Dave was good with machines that wanted to stay broken.

He powered the drone down, wiped a smear of oil off the casing, and set it aside for pickup. His back was complaining. His eyes were doing that gritty, overused thing they did at this hour. Everything hurt in the low, constant way of a body that had been running too long on not enough.

He stood up, rolling his shoulders until something cracked in a way that was satisfying and probably concerning.

He was reaching for his jacket when the drone's status light flickered.

He turned around.

The drone was powered down. He'd killed the power himself. The status light should have been off — completely off, no standby draw, nothing — but for just a second it had pulsed. Not randomly. Not a dying flicker. It had pulsed in something that almost had a shape, a rhythm, the kind of signal that wanted to mean something to whoever was paying attention.

Dave stared at the thing.

The light stayed dark. Normal. Silent.

He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. Bad lighting and too many hours and the particular way the brain started manufacturing patterns when it was tired enough. That was all.

"Get it together," he said.

His voice came out a little flat. A little too deliberate, like he was talking himself down from something he hadn't quite named.

He grabbed his jacket — the old one, torn lining, oil stains that had outlasted three proper attempts to remove them — and killed the last lamp. The shop went dark except for the faint blue bleed of the neon sign outside and the rain-blurred city light through the windows. He was halfway to the door when he stopped again.

He didn't know why.

He turned back and looked at the bench. The drone sitting quiet in the dark.

Nothing.

He stood there longer than he meant to. Then shook his head, locked up, and stepped outside.

The rain hit immediately. Cold and indifferent, the way the city's weather always was. He turned up his collar and started walking — hands in pockets, eyes down, the same three blocks to the transit stop he'd done a thousand times. The elevated track ran overhead, mag-lev screaming past every twelve minutes like it had somewhere to be. A couple of food stall vendors were still running, the ones who did the real business at this hour: people coming off shift, people who'd been awake all night for reasons they'd prefer not to explain, people like Dave, the ones the city kept running on.

He was halfway up the stairs to the transit platform when he got the feeling.

It wasn't a sound. Wasn't anything he could have pointed at if someone asked him. Just a particular weight in the air at his back — the specific quality of being observed. He'd had it before. Everyone in this city learned to read it eventually, or they learned to stop having expensive things.

He turned around.

Street level, across from the repair shop, in the gap between a working light and a dead one. Someone was standing still in the rain. Not sheltering. Not moving through it. Just standing there, hands in the front pocket of an oversized hoodie, face tilted slightly upward.

Looking at the shop. Or at Dave. At this distance, in this light, there was no way to be sure which.

He waited for the figure to move.

They didn't.

The mag-lev announced itself from down the track — twelve minutes, right on schedule.

When he looked back at the gap between the lights, the figure was gone.

Dave stood there for another second, rain running off his jacket and down the back of his neck. Then he told himself the same thing he'd been telling himself all night, in one form or another.

*Get it together.*

He went through the turnstile.

Behind him, three blocks away, in the dark of the empty shop, the courier drone's status light pulsed once.

Twice.

Then went still.

*END CHAPTER ONE — DAVE*

# ANOMALY WARDENS
## Chapter Two — Drones

---

Dave opened the shop at noon.

He did this by walking down the stairs from his apartment above it, unlocking the door with the key that didn't fit cleanly anymore — stuck lock, two months, he'd gotten used to the jiggle — and going to put the kettle on in the back. The noodle stall next door was already running, which meant it had never stopped, which meant Tak was doing that thing again where he didn't sleep for three days and then crashed for twelve hours and called it even. Dave didn't say anything about it. Tak didn't ask about the buckets.

He'd slept four hours. Enough. That was what he always told himself.

The corporate suit had picked up the fixed drone at ten with minimal conversation and an expression Dave couldn't read. Fine. Not his job to read expressions. His job was machines.

He was midway through bench-cleaning, half a cup of tea in, when the door opened.

The guy who came in looked like he'd lost an argument with a colour scheme. Crimson hair — short, going in several directions at once that were probably intentional — and golden eyes that caught the lamplight in a way that suggested either very expensive cosmetic work or something stranger. In this part of the city Dave had learned not to ask which. Long-sleeve shirt under a beat-up jacket, dark jeans, the particular ease of someone who assumed rooms were generally glad to see him.

He looked about twenty-three and like he'd never been tired in his entire life.

"Hey," he said, to the shop in general. He glanced around — quick, easy, the scan of someone taking stock without making it obvious he was taking stock — and landed on Dave. "You the one who fixes the unfixable?"

Dave looked at him over his tea. "I fix things. Whether they're unfixable depends on who gave up first."

Something in the guy's face registered this as actually funny, which was better than the alternative. "Yeah. That's what I heard."

He set a case on the counter. Battered on the outside — the kind of case that was actively trying not to look like it was worth stealing. He opened it, and what was inside was not the kind of thing the outside deserved.

Four drones. Palm-sized, matte black with faint green trace lines running the antenna arrays. Custom-built to a degree Dave had never seen outside of military spec, and even military spec tended to look like it had been designed by a committee with competing opinions. These had been designed by one person. He could tell by the way every decision talked to every other decision — weight distribution referencing rotor geometry referencing housing design — the kind of coherence that only happened when one mind had made every single call.

Dave set his tea down.

"Where did you get these?"

"Made them." No elaboration. Not defensive — just factual. He leaned on the counter with the ease of someone who'd had this reaction before and found it acceptable.

"Right," Dave said. "What's wrong with them?"

He moved around the counter while the guy explained — two running hot at max output, one with a responsive lag so small most people wouldn't catch it. Dave only half-listened because he was already looking, already turning the first drone over in his hands. The balance point was exactly where he'd have put it if he'd built it himself, which was the kind of thought that didn't usually occur to him about someone else's work.

"These are neurolink-capable?"

"Yeah."

"You run them synced?"

"When I need to."

That was apparently all he was going to get on the subject. Dave accepted this the way he accepted most things — without comment.

He set the first one down and picked up the third. There was a port on the underside he almost missed. Tiny, purpose-built, added by hand — the kind of port that wouldn't appear on any spec sheet because it had never been submitted to anyone with a spec sheet.

He looked at it for a moment.

"The lag's not in the hardware," he said, mostly to himself. "It's in the sync calibration."

The guy, who had started moving around the shop in the unhurried way of someone with nowhere to be, didn't respond immediately. He was looking at a disassembled servo motor Dave had left on the side shelf, turning it over once, putting it back.

"Can you work around the link?" he said.

"Probably."

Dave set the drone aside and started on the two hot-running units first.

The guy talked while he worked. Not about anything useful — not about the drones, not about himself, not about where he'd come from or why he'd chosen this particular shop on this particular afternoon. He asked questions the way people did when they were making conversation, except the questions were slightly more specific than conversation usually required. How long had Dave been here. Whether he'd always been good with machines or whether that was something he'd learned. What the weirdest job he'd ever had was.

Dave answered in the flat, economical way he answered most things. Twelve years, working machines since before that. He didn't know if it was learned. A power coupling on an orbital supply drone, which he'd fixed with a piece of wire and a comment he no longer remembered.

"A comment," the guy repeated.

"It was a stressful situation. I said some things."

"And it worked."

"And it worked."

The guy looked at him for just a second. Then he picked up something else off the shelf and put it back.

The two hot-running units took about twenty minutes — rotor calibration, thermal paste on the secondary heat sink, one capacitor that had simply decided it was finished. Standard work, done non-standardly, because the engineering underneath it was unlike anything he'd seen in a commercial context. Every fix opened a new appreciation for how the thing had been built in the first place. He kept catching himself slowing down to look when he should have been moving.

He came back to the third drone.

He held it for a while without working. This was a habit he'd never successfully explained. Some machines needed a moment. He'd stopped trying to justify it.

Then he set the tip of his wrench against the custom port, looked at the drone the way he looked at all of them when they were being difficult, and said: "You're overthinking it. It's a straightforward handshake. Stop being stupid about this."

Three adjustments. Small ones — the kind that shouldn't have made any difference, let alone the difference they made.

The status light climbed from amber to blue.

He ran it through a startup cycle. The rotors came up clean and even, no hesitation, no lag at all. He held it in his palm for a second and felt the particular absence of a problem that had been there before.

"Done," he said.

He looked up.

The guy had stopped moving.

He was standing near the side shelf with his arms at his sides and the relaxed, easy manner of his whole presence had gone very still — not tense, not alarmed, just still, the way a clock looks between ticks. He was looking at the drone in Dave's hand with an expression that was almost nothing and was not quite nothing.

It lasted maybe two seconds.

Then he pushed off the shelf and came around the bench.

He picked up the third drone, turned it over, ran something on a small device Dave hadn't noticed him carrying. Set it down. Picked up the other two in turn and did the same. His face had gone back to easy and unreadable.

"How long did that take you?" he said, casual, not looking up.

"Twenty minutes. Give or take."

He set the last drone down. Something crossed his face, quick and gone.

"Hm," he said.

He started putting the drones back in the case himself, which Dave would normally have objected to — customers usually made a mess of the case packing — except he did it with the particular efficiency of someone who knew exactly which drone went where and why. The case closed with a soft, precise click.

Dave thought about the price for a moment, added the usual premium for non-standard spec work, added a bit more on top of that because the third drone had been genuinely strange and he felt that ought to be reflected somewhere. He named the number.

The guy didn't argue. Didn't pause. Pulled up his phone, transferred it straight. Clean funds — the kind that went through without a delay, which in this part of the city was not something Dave took for granted.

He picked up the case and slung it over one shoulder like it weighed nothing.

At the door he stopped. Glanced back — not a full turn, just enough. The golden eyes caught the blue neon from the sign outside and did something interesting with it.

"Good shop," he said. Easy. Light. Like it meant nothing in particular. "I'll probably be back."

Dave looked at him.

"Sure," he said.

The door closed. The shop went back to being quiet.

Dave stood at the bench for a moment, looking at the space where the drones had been. He tried to trace the logic of the third fix — the port, the three adjustments, the specific things he'd said to it. Tried to follow the reasoning back to something he could point to as a skill set, something he'd learned from somewhere.

He couldn't quite do it.

He finished his tea, which had gone cold. Cleared the bench. Went next door and got something from Tak's stall, which was a soup that was probably mostly sodium but was hot, which was what mattered.

He came back, sat on the stool, and ate.

The golden eyes. The way the guy had gone still for those two seconds, looking at the drone. The way he'd paid without negotiating, which nobody did.

*Weird,* Dave thought.

Then he thought about the cracked housing on the nav drone someone had dropped off last Thursday that he still hadn't gotten to, and went to get it from the back shelf.

He'd probably forget about the whole thing by morning.

# ANOMALY WARDENS
## Chapter Three — Drive

---

The thirteenth floor smelled like burnt circuitry, old takeout, and whatever Riot had been doing with accelerant earlier that morning.

Crimson decided not to ask.

He came up through the stairwell the way he always did — two steps at a time, easy, the bag of food in one hand and the drone case slung over his shoulder — and pushed through the fire door into what the Wardens had collectively decided to call the common area, because calling it what it actually was felt like giving up on something.

What it actually was: the gutted remains of a mid-level corporate floor that had been abandoned long enough to start developing opinions about it. Drop ceiling tiles missing in a pattern that looked random and wasn't. Wall panels hanging at angles that suggested the building had tried to shrug them off. Fluorescent strips dangling from exposed cable, half of them dead, one of them doing a slow, rhythmic swing from a current of air that had no obvious source — but which had, by some accident of physics, positioned itself directly over the main workbench at an angle that was genuinely useful. Nobody had moved it. You didn't argue with an accident that worked.

In the far corner, a section of ceiling had given up entirely and collapsed inward, leaving a gap that opened onto a tangle of structural beams above. Hex had claimed this space within the first week. She had not been asked. She had simply appeared up there one morning with her rifle and a thermos and an expression that dared anyone to comment, and the matter had been considered settled ever since.

The rest of the floor was sorted chaos — the only kind of order any of them had managed to agree on. Crates stacked with intention. Cables routed with care along baseboards even where the baseboards were crumbling. A fold-out table that served as the tactical center, covered in overlapping screens and printouts and three empty cups that had been there long enough to become furniture. Mismatched chairs pulled from various abandoned offices on lower floors, arranged around a heating pipe in the south corner that put out enough warmth to make the area almost comfortable.

Oracle was in hers.

She was always in hers.

It was a high-backed office chair, slightly better than the others, which she had neither requested nor commented on. It had simply been there when she sat down the first time, and no one had mentioned it. She had a cardigan on — pale grey, slightly too large — and her hands were folded in her lap and her eyes were open and she was doing the particular kind of nothing that Oracle did, which was not the same as being inactive. The warmth from the pipe made the air around her corner glow slightly amber. She looked like something that had always been here.

She glanced at Crimson when he came in.

He gave her a small nod. She returned it.

That was most of what they needed.

---

Riot was at the workbench.

This was always a situation requiring a moment of assessment before proceeding.

She was short — shorter than the bench, which she'd compensated for with a platform of stacked equipment cases that she'd never acknowledge as a step stool — and her hair today was aggressively orange with streaks of black that may or may not have been soot. Probably soot. Her jacket was covered in small pockets, every pocket occupied, and she was currently in the process of doing something with a length of det cord and a pair of pliers that Crimson elected not to examine too closely. There was a bandaid on her left cheek. New one. He didn't know when that had happened.

A piece of ceiling panel that had been sitting at the edge of the bench for two weeks fell, narrowly missing her shoulder, and hit the ground behind her.

Riot didn't look up.

From somewhere near the tactical table, Marcus made a small sound of acknowledgement and went back to what he was doing.

"Food," Crimson announced, setting the bag on the cleared end of the table. "Don't make it weird."

"You got the noodle place?" Riot asked, still not looking up.

"Obviously."

"Did you get my—"

"Yes."

She made a satisfied sound. The det cord did something that briefly suggested the next few seconds might go differently than they did, and then didn't. Riot set the pliers down and reached for the bag.

---

Ghostwire was in the corner.

Specifically, he was in the corner that contained the old reception desk — long since stripped of anything useful — where he had established what could generously be described as a secondary workspace. Less generously: a pile of equipment, an oversized hoodie pulled so far forward it was doing structural work, and a general atmosphere of being aggrieved.

He was currently not speaking to the radio.

The radio was a battered tabletop unit, analogue dials, the kind of thing that hadn't been manufactured in thirty years. It was not on. It had not been on since Ghostwire had carried it up here four days ago from the sixth floor, where he'd found it in a storage closet. It sat on the desk and did what unpowered radios did, which was nothing.

Ghostwire was sitting three feet away from it with his arms crossed, staring at a point approximately six inches above and to the left of the unit, with the specific energy of someone who had made their position clear and was waiting for an apology that had not been forthcoming.

Crimson set a container of noodles within his reach.

Ghostwire didn't look up but his hand found it immediately.

The radio sat there.

Nobody asked.

---

Marcus was at the tactical table.

He was a man who made rooms feel smaller simply by existing in them, which in a space this size meant the table looked appropriately scaled for the first time. Tactical screens open, three of them running simultaneously, one displaying a grid overlay of the mid-city district that he'd been quietly annotating for the past six hours. His hands on the table were not a sight you got used to. Each knuckle bearing the reinforced housing of what looked, to the uninformed eye, like very aggressive jewellery. To the informed eye: four-inch tungsten plates per hand, machined to fit his specific grip, each knuckle housing a shell that ran lengthways through the housing in a way that made the physics straightforward and the outcome not something you thought about for too long.

He was also, currently, eating noodles with complete serenity and annotating a grid overlay at the same time, which should not have been possible and was.

"Hex," he said, without looking up. "He's back."

From above, there was the sound of a scope adjustment.

"I noticed," said Hex.

---

Hex's corner of the ceiling was, by any reasonable metric, not a corner. It was a jagged gap where the drop ceiling had given way to expose the structural layer above — beams, conduit, decades of urban sediment — and which provided, if you were committed to the concept, approximately three viable firing positions covering the main floor, the stairwell door, and the window line.

Hex was committed to the concept.

She was also, at this particular moment, upside down on a beam in a way that had no obvious practical purpose, rifle balanced across her knees, silver-purple hair hanging free and catching the ambient light from the screens below. One ice-blue eye and one glowing crimson tracking Crimson's movement through the space with the particular attention of someone deciding whether this counted as a welcome or an opportunity.

The heterochromia made it difficult to tell. Usually this was intentional.

"You were gone a while," she said.

"Errands."

"Mm." A pause. The rifle shifted slightly. "You smell like someone else's machine oil."

"Repair shop. Drone maintenance."

Another pause. Longer this time.

"Which shop?"

"One that works." He was unpacking the food without looking up. "Eat something, Hex."

The rifle made a small sound that was not agreement.

---

They ate the way they always did — scattered, non-ceremonial, in various states of focus — and the floor settled into its version of comfortable. Riot had abandoned the det cord in favor of cataloguing something from a hard case in her lap, occasionally holding components up to the swinging light and squinting. Marcus ate and annotated. Ghostwire had not moved but had finished his noodles at a speed that suggested he'd been hungrier than he was letting on. Oracle held her cup of tea in both hands and watched the room with the warm, unhurried attention of someone who found it genuinely interesting.

Crimson was recalibrating the two field drones he'd brought back — the ones he hadn't put in for repair — when Riot looked up.

"So how'd the mechanic do?"

The recalibration tool stopped moving.

Just for a second.

Crimson set it down. Picked up his noodles. Took a deliberate bite.

"Fine," he said.

The word landed in the room and sat there.

Riot's hand paused over her hard case.

At the tactical table, Marcus's stylus stopped moving.

From the ceiling, nothing — which was notable, because Hex always made a sound.

Ghostwire did not look up from his grievance with the radio, but something about the angle of his hoodie shifted.

Oracle continued holding her tea.

The swinging fluorescent moved through its arc.

From somewhere near Riot's jacket, one of the smaller components detonated with a sound like a very firm handclap. A small column of smoke rose from her second pocket. She didn't look at it.

"Fine," Marcus said.

Not a question.

"Fine," Crimson confirmed.

"The mechanic." Riot's voice was very careful. "Fixed your drones."

"That's what I said."

"And it was." A pause. "Fine."

Crimson ate his noodles.

"Crimson." Marcus set his stylus down. This was significant. "You built those drones."

"I'm aware."

"You built them from scratch."

"Also aware."

"The sync calibration on the third unit has a custom architecture that doesn't exist in any commercial—"

"I know what I built, Marcus."

Marcus looked at him for a long moment with the expression of a man performing an extremely rapid calculation and not enjoying the result.

"How long did it take him," he said.

Crimson said nothing.

"How long."

"...Twenty minutes."

The tactical screens hummed.

Riot made a noise that was not a word.

"Twenty minutes," Marcus said.

"Give or take."

"For all three."

Crimson put his noodles down. "The two heat-running units were straightforward. Standard calibration issues—"

"What about the third one." Riot had fully put down her hard case now. "The one with the custom sync port."

Silence.

"Crimson."

"He talked to it," Crimson said. Flat. Factual. Faintly pained. "He looked at it and he said it was overthinking the handshake and told it to stop being stupid, and then he made three adjustments and it was fixed. It took about four minutes."

The room absorbed this.

From the ceiling: "He talked to it."

"He talked to it."

"Your drone."

"My drone, yes, Hex, thank you for clarifying."

Another small component in Riot's jacket made a compressed sound of agreement. She ignored it.

"I need to understand something," she said, with the exaggerated calm of someone who had decided the only way through this was patience. "Your drone — the one with the custom neurolink port that you handbuilt from an architecture you invented — listened to a mechanic."

"It would appear so."

"A regular mechanic."

"He has a shop."

"In the undercity."

"It has a sign."

"Crimson." Marcus's voice had not gotten louder. It rarely did. "I want you to think carefully about what you're describing."

"I am thinking carefully. I've been thinking carefully since I left the shop. I have reached no conclusions that make me feel better about any of it, and I would appreciate—"

The shot came from above.

Crimson moved left. Not a dramatic movement — not a dive, not a roll — just a small, immediate, unhurried step to the left, the kind that happened below the level of thought. The drone on his belt was already moving before he was, a small matte-black unit that had been sitting dormant since he'd walked in, and it caught the round with its body at roughly the point where Crimson's shoulder had been.

It fell.

Crimson looked at it on the floor.

Then he looked up.

Hex had her rifle reloaded and was watching him from the beam with her mismatched eyes and an expression of complete composure. "Just checking," she said.

"That was my last field-ready unit," Crimson said.

"Mm."

"Which means until I restock, I have no drones."

"Or," Hex said, "you could go back to the mechanic."

Crimson looked at the drone on the floor for another moment.

Then he looked at Marcus.

Marcus was trying very hard to be expressionless and failing, which on his particular face looked like a mountain attempting to appear casual. His shoulders were doing something.

"It's not funny," Crimson said.

Marcus made a sound.

"Marcus."

The sound got worse.

"I hate this team," Crimson said, without conviction.

Marcus put one large hand over his face. This did not help. From the ceiling, Hex made a soft, satisfied sound and adjusted her position on the beam. Riot had given up on composure entirely and was making a noise into her noodle container. Even Ghostwire — without looking up, without acknowledging the conversation had happened — had the particular stillness of someone who had heard everything and was filing it away for later.

The radio sat in the corner and did not comment.

Oracle watched all of it with warm, unhurried attention, her tea held in both hands, and said nothing.

She was smiling.

---

It was Marcus who got Crimson talking properly.

Not immediately. Not directly. He waited until Riot had mostly finished gloating and Hex had descended from the ceiling — partly for food, partly because she'd apparently decided Crimson's continued comfort required supervision, which expressed itself as sitting two feet away from him and watching him the way you watched a weather system — and until the conversation had worn itself down to the comfortable low hum of people who'd made their point and were letting it breathe.

Then Marcus said, quietly: "Tell me about the shop."

And Crimson told him.

Not the quick version. The real version — the sign with the burned-out HONEST, the noodle place next door, the particular smell of old solder and machine oil. The way the place looked like it had been filtering out everyone who wasn't serious about the work for long enough that only the serious work remained. He described Dave the way you described something you were still making sense of: the flat economy of words, the way he'd barely reacted to the drones, the questions that were slightly more specific than conversation required.

He described the third drone.

The port. The four minutes. The three adjustments that shouldn't have made the difference they made.

The way Dave had looked at it when he was done — not with surprise, not with satisfaction exactly, but with the particular expression of someone registering the absence of a problem.

"And he didn't know what he was doing," Crimson said. "That's the part. He wasn't performing. He wasn't showing off. He genuinely doesn't know what he does isn't normal."

The room had gone quieter.

"He fixed it," Riot said, "the way you'd fix a drone. Except instead of being a drone mechanic, you're an anomaly."

"That's the question, isn't it."

"Did Oracle know?" Marcus asked.

Everyone looked at Oracle.

Oracle looked back at them.

She did not answer, which was an answer.

"Right," Marcus said.

Hex leaned back in her chair and tilted her head at Crimson with the look she got when she was being actually serious, which she was careful to ration. "You went still," she said. "When he fixed it."

"...Yeah."

"I didn't think anything made you go still."

Crimson looked at the wrecked drone on the floor. "Neither did I."

The fluorescent kept its rhythm. Somewhere in the building below them, the city kept moving in the way cities did — indifferent, continuous, layered with things that were happening to people who didn't know they were happening yet.

Marcus looked at Crimson for a moment.

Then he stood up, crossed the floor in the unhurried way of someone whose mass had made urgency optional a long time ago, and grabbed Crimson around the neck in the particular grip that functioned as an armlock for everyone else and as an affectionate headlock for Crimson specifically.

Crimson went with it immediately, the way he always did — dropping his weight, shifting his hips, finding the gap — except that the gap closed in exactly the same moment, the way it always did, and they moved around each other in the small space between the table and the workbench like something that had been practiced into reflex.

It had been.

Riot watched them with her chin in one hand. Hex had her rifle across her knees and was observing with the expression of someone who appreciated competent movement regardless of context.

Marcus got Crimson into a position that would have compromised anyone who needed leverage and then let him out of it, because the point wasn't the hold, the point was the contact, and they both knew it.

Crimson came up grinning.

Marcus was doing the mountain-trying-to-be-casual thing again. "You're going back," he said. It was not a question.

"I need my drones fixed."

"That's why you're going back."

"Marcus."

"Sure," Marcus said.

---

Oracle's smile dropped.

It happened between one second and the next, without movement, without warning. She was sitting in her chair with her tea in both hands and her face was warm and open and then it wasn't — the expression gone completely, like a signal cutting out, her eyes going glassy and distant and focused on something that wasn't in the room.

At the tactical table, Marcus stopped.

He had been mid-sentence. He finished the word he was on — a clean stop, not a stumble — and looked up.

Across the floor, Oracle sat in her chair and looked at whatever Oracle looked at when she looked like this.

"Guys," Marcus said.

His voice had changed.

One word. Different weight. Different frequency entirely.

Riot's hands went still over her bandolier. Ghostwire's hoodie shifted. Hex, who had been in the process of arguing a point with Crimson that Crimson had been pretending not to follow, went quiet mid-syllable.

They waited.

Oracle came back slowly — the focus returning to her eyes, the distance closing. She looked at her tea for a moment. Then she looked at all of them.

She nodded.

"It's time," she said.

---

The floor became something else.

Not louder — the opposite of louder. The volume dropped and the density increased, the comfortable disorder of a few minutes ago folding itself away with the particular efficiency of people who had done this enough times that the transition was automatic.

Marcus moved to the equipment rack first. The armor came off the shelf in sections — tungsten plate, matte-finished, each piece thick enough that setting one down made a sound you felt in the floor. He assembled himself into it with the unhurried attention of someone who trusted the process because the process had never failed him. Chest. Shoulders. Greaves. The knuckle housings last — sliding into place over his hands with a precision-fitted click, the shells aligned, the fit exact. When he was done he looked like a decision.

Riot was three steps ahead of him by the time the first plate was seated.

Her loadout was less assembly and more confirmation — hands moving down the bandolier in a sequence that looked like a ritual because it was, each pocket checked with a tap that had a specific sound for pass and a different specific sound for pull-and-replace. She pulled and replaced twice. Everything else passed. She slung the bandolier and shrugged into it and rolled her shoulders and a grenade pin she definitely hadn't pulled caught the light for a moment and then was gone. The shelf behind her settled three inches to the left for no reason. She didn't look back at it.

Ghostwire stood.

This was notable mostly because of how he did it — not rising from the chair so much as transitioning from stationary to vertical, a movement that somehow suggested he'd already left before the motion completed. The hoodie went up. Not pulled up — it simply was up, the hood forward and the pale face below it retreating into shadow by degrees. He picked up his kit in a way that suggested less holding and more absorption. The radio on the desk did something that nobody present could hear. Ghostwire paused for half a second — looking at it, or appearing to — and something complicated moved through his expression and resolved into nothing. He walked away from it without looking back.

From above, Hex dropped.

She didn't use the access she'd rigged on the east beam. She came straight down from a height that should have been a problem and landed in a forward roll that converted the drop into momentum and brought her up standing in one motion, rifle already indexed. She checked her kit in three seconds — hands running a sequence so fast it looked continuous — and when she came out of it her eyes were bright and her head was tilted at the angle that meant she was thinking about trajectories. She looked across the floor at Crimson.

He was already suited.

Nobody had watched him gear up. He was just — ready, in the way he was always ready, which was also the way nobody had ever been able to explain. Smartglasses on, the heads-up display casting a thin amber line across the upper half of his vision. Twin pistols seated, grip forward, both safeties confirmed. The katana at his back — flame-edged, hand-fitted, the kind of weapon that looked unserious until the light caught the blade geometry. His belt stacked with enough ammo variety to cover for any situation he'd survived so far and several he hadn't.

He put his hands up.

The movement was loose and fluid, both hands raised to shoulder height with the fingers spread — not a gesture, a command, the kind that goes below words to the substrate of things that have been told what they're for. Eleven drones came online simultaneously. Not here — elsewhere, distributed across the building's exterior and the city's upper levels, in positions they'd taken without drawing attention because they'd been built to move without drawing attention. One by one their status lights climbed to active in his display. One by one they formed up, and for a moment in the amber glow of the smartglasses they were a constellation, organized and patient and waiting.

He moved his hands the way a conductor moves at the moment the silence ends.

The drones dispersed.

Into the city. Into the dark. Into the space ahead of them, running patterns on architecture and roofline and the specific geography of wherever Oracle's nod was pointing them tonight.

Crimson's hands came down. Easy. Natural. Like the outro of something that had always known how to end.

Marcus looked at him.

"Ready?" he said.

"Always," Crimson said.

They went.

Behind them, Oracle settled deeper into her chair by the heating pipe and folded her hands in her lap, and the swinging fluorescent made its arc, and the radio sat in the corner of the room.

Whether it said anything after that, there was no one left to hear.

---

*END CHAPTER THREE — Drive*

# ANOMALY WARDENS
## Chapter Four — Distraction

---

The coffee machine came in on a Tuesday.

Woman with a kid's drawing on her jacket pocket — sleeve patch, hand-stitched, the kind parents wore without noticing they were wearing them anymore. She'd set the unit on the counter without much preamble and explained that it had stopped recognizing her profile three days ago and was now making something that was technically coffee in the way that weather was technically precipitation. Dave had looked at it, quoted her a price, and she'd nodded and said she'd be back Thursday and left.

That had been eleven days ago.

The machine sat on the back shelf between a reclaimed audio relay and a box of capacitors he kept meaning to reorganize. It was a Vertec NuBrew 7 — the kind of unit you found in every third kitchen in the mid-district, cheap enough to be everywhere, smart enough to be annoying when it broke. This one had a small sticker on the side. Stars. The adhesive kind, gold foil, the kind that came in sheets from the school supply stalls. Four of them, arranged in no particular pattern, pressed on slightly crooked.

Dave had looked at the sticker once and then not again.

He was midway through the NuBrew's calibration matrix when he ran out of solder.

Not out, exactly. The spool wasn't empty — there were maybe six inches left on it, which was enough if the remaining joints were forgiving, which they weren't, because the NuBrew 7's internal layout had been designed by someone who believed in efficiency above all and had apparently never had to service one. He held the spool up, looked at it, looked at the board, did the math.

"Right," he said, to no one.

He set the spool down. Got his jacket from the hook by the stairs. Considered the board for a moment — the exposed joints, the particular patience required by precision calibration — and put a cloth over it the way you covered things you were coming back to.

He locked the shop and went out.

---

The street was doing what streets did at this hour, which was everything simultaneously and at volume. The mid-district evening ran hot and neon-adjacent, the overhead transit lines throwing moving shadows across the storefronts below, the air carrying the mixed output of six different food stalls and the particular electric undertone of a city that had stopped noticing its own infrastructure. Dave moved through it the way he moved through most things — without friction, without particular interest, taking the most direct line to where he was going.

He was three blocks from the shop when he heard it.

Not close. Six blocks, maybe seven — the distance that turned specific sounds into general ones, impact into rumble, the sharp crack of something structural giving way into something you felt in the chest before you heard it with the ears. The city had its rhythms and this was one of them — not common, but not uncommon either, the way rain wasn't uncommon even when you hadn't checked the forecast.

He glanced left.

The end of the cross-street showed him a slice of sky and building face and, moving through it, the upper shoulder of something large. Matte grey paneling, the corporate geometric of enforcer-grade hardware, the specific unhurried stride of something that didn't need to hurry because the math of its existence already settled every argument in advance.

Dave looked at it for the length of one step.

"Fucking pigs," he said, quietly, the way you said things that had stopped requiring emphasis.

He turned right and kept walking.

---

B@rZ Supply and Sundries occupied the ground floor of a building that had survived three rezoning attempts through a combination of structural stubbornness and the owner's complete unwillingness to acknowledge correspondence. The sign above the door was hand-lettered in two colors that had faded to approximately one, and beside it, on a piece of laminate that had been there long enough to yellow at the edges, a notice read: NO CLANKERS.

Below it, someone had taped a handwritten addendum: *this means you, Sevens.*

Below that, in different handwriting: *Sevens says hi.*

The bell above the door made a sound when Dave pushed it open, and B@rZ looked up from behind the counter with the expression of a man for whom every customer was the specific customer he'd been waiting for.

He was large in the way that suggested the architectural decision had been made early and committed to fully — broad across everything, the kind of build that made the counter look correctly scaled for the first time. His head was shaved on the sides and he had the remnants of a braid at the back that had been doing its best for some years. His apron said EXPERT on it in stencil letters, which he had not put there himself and had never removed.

"My friend," he said, spreading both hands on the counter. "My very tired-looking friend. What has the city done to you today."

"Ran out of solder," Dave said.

"Solder. A tragedy. A small tragedy, but a tragedy. You want the good stuff or the stuff that works?"

"Stuff that works."

"Smart man. Smart, tired man." He was already moving along the shelf behind him, one hand trailing across stock with the familiarity of someone who could do this without looking and occasionally proved it. "You know what I tell people? I say, B@rZ, I say, why do you keep this shop open so late, and I say to myself, because somewhere out there is a mechanic who will run out of solder at the worst possible time, and who is going to save him if not me?"

"Nobody asked you that," Dave said.

"I ask myself. I have very interesting conversations." He found the solder and set it on the counter — good gauge, right alloy, the kind that did what it was told. "You fixing anything interesting?"

"NuBrew 7."

B@rZ made a sound of deep philosophical suffering. "The profile calibration?"

"The profile calibration."

"You know who designed that layout? I'll tell you who designed that layout. Someone who had never in their life needed to open one up. Someone who thought efficiency meant small." He shook his head. "I had one in here last month. Very sweet unit. Very apologetic about the whole thing. I told it, I said, it's not your fault, the fault is upstream, and it said — " He paused. Tilted his head. "Actually it didn't say anything because it's an appliance, but the point stands."

From the back of the shop came the soft chime of the inventory system running its evening check. B@rZ glanced at it with what could only be described as fond exasperation.

"Fourteenth time today," he said, to Dave. "Fourteenth. I told it eleven was enough. It doesn't listen." He tapped the counter. "But what can you do. They're family."

Dave paid. Exact, contactless, clean.

"Get some sleep," B@rZ said, as he always said.

"Sure," Dave said, as he always said.

The bell made its sound and the door closed and the street took him back.

---

He went the long way without deciding to.

Not significantly longer — one block of difference, a route that took him past the transit underpass rather than through it. The city was thinning slightly at this hour, the commercial strip giving way to residential blocks in varying states of maintenance, windows lit yellow and blue, the transit lines overhead carrying their loads in both directions with the reliability of things that didn't have opinions about what they were doing.

He was passing the edge of the commercial district when the broadcast screen on the corner pharmacy updated.

He glanced at it the way you glanced at things in your peripheral vision — registering without deciding to register, the information entering through habit rather than intent.

*— third quarter infrastructure spend announced by Councilor Veth, the mixed-use development on Archer spanning fourteen floors, projected completion —*

He kept walking.

*— reminder that Colden Street Primary will be closed Thursday for the district safety assessment, parents are advised —*

A transit car overhead, its undercarriage throwing a stripe of shadow across the street.

*— earlier this evening a disturbance in the Vantage district required enforcer response. A spokesperson confirmed the situation was contained within the hour. One casualty reported. Authorities have classified the incident as a suspected terrorist engagement and stated that public safety was maintained throughout. No further details —*

Dave was half a block past the screen.

He registered the word *disturbance* the way you registered weather reports for places you weren't going — factual, ambient, already behind him. The Vantage district was three kilometers east. The enforcers had dealt with it. One casualty, which was either very bad or very good depending on what you were measuring and who was doing the measuring, and either way not his.

He turned onto his street.

---

The shop was exactly as he'd left it.

He hung his jacket. Put the kettle on. Went to the back shelf and retrieved the NuBrew 7, carrying it back to the bench with the cloth still over the board the way he'd left it. Set up the work light. Checked the joints he had left.

The new solder fed clean.

He worked through the remaining calibration points with the focused patience of someone who had learned that this particular model rewarded not rushing it — the profile matrix buried three layers deep in the unit's self-management system, the kind of architecture that made you wonder about the person who'd thought this was a reasonable place to put it. He wondered about them briefly and then didn't, because they weren't here and the machine was.

An hour of work, give or take.

The NuBrew 7 ran a startup cycle. The display climbed through its sequence — system check, calibration load, profile recognition — and arrived at the default screen with the small animated cup that the Vertec brand had been using since the second generation, a piece of UI design that had outlasted three corporate rebranding efforts through the simple mechanism of everyone preferring it.

Dave held it for a moment.

It was fixed. Whoever came for it would find it fixed, the profile matrix clean, the brewing cycle running inside its correct parameters for the first time in however long it had been running wrong.

He put it back on the shelf.

The stars on the side caught the work light briefly — four of them, gold foil, slightly crooked, the kind that came in sheets from school supply stalls. He looked at them for a second and then went to get his tea.

The kettle was ready.

He sat on the stool and drank it and the shop was quiet around him, the way the shop was always quiet at this hour — not empty, exactly, just reduced to its essentials. The machines on the shelves. The work on the bench. The small sounds of the city coming through the walls in the particular frequency that was too consistent to be interesting.

The news feed on the small screen above the door cycled through its late edition. Infrastructure spending. School closures. A ribbon cutting for something that had cost too much and taken too long. The anchor's voice had the practiced neutrality of someone whose job was to make everything the same temperature.

Dave finished his tea.

He rinsed the cup. Turned off the work light. Checked the locks on the front door — the jiggle, the catch, the second check that had become automatic — and went upstairs.

Warmer. Red. Not the red of the pain or the red of the noise or the red of the clinical efficiency of everything that had happened in here. A different red. The red of something that hadn't decided yet what it was going to do.

It didn't come.

The sounds from the blur just — existed. In the space around her. Not directed at the purpose she apparently was. Not clinical. Not commentary.

Just.

There.

She didn't have language left for what that felt like. She had language for pain and disorientation and the cold and the question of who she was that never answered itself. She didn't have language for this.

The blur stayed where it was.

It didn't leave.

She waited for it to leave, the way everything left, the way the voices came and assessed and departed and left only the noise and the cold behind. The blur remained.

*this one*

The thought came slowly, surfacing through everything else, fragile and half-formed.

*this one didn't hurt*

She turned it over with what was left of her that could still turn things over.

The blur was still there.

Still warm. Still teal and red at its edges. Still making sounds that reached her without causing damage.

Something in the deepest remaining part of her — the part that still knew it had a name even if it couldn't find it — assembled itself into the most coherent thing she had managed in longer than she could calculate.

It arrived not as a thought but as the most fundamental unit of language she had left.

Two words.

Everything she had.

*Stay. Please stay.*

Two pistols, one blade in reverse grip in his off hand, and the drones weren't just cover anymore, they were weapons, foothold and battering ram and shrapnel all at once, one skittering low to take a man's legs out from under him while Crimson used another as a mid-air step to clear a burst of fire that would've opened his chest, landing already firing, already moving, already three positions ahead of where his body currently was.
Thirteen of them on him at once now. Foot soldiers, matched armor, the kind of coordinated fire discipline that said corporate contractor, not street muscle. He read the formation the way Hex read a chessboard — patterns, gaps, the half-second window where two guns couldn't both track him — and moved into the gaps like he'd been born knowing where they'd be.
Somewhere underneath the motion, something flickered.
A hallway. White. Too white. A door that didn't open no matter how many times —
Gone. He put it down without looking at it, the way you'd put down something too hot to hold, and put a round through a knee instead, using the man's collapse as a launch point for the drone-step that carried him over the next volley entirely.
His face gave away nothing. That was the discipline. That had always been the discipline.
Two rounds caught him in the side — through the jacket, through the low-grade plating underneath, a graze more than a hit — and he didn't break stride. He'd taken worse from Hex on a bad day, and Hex's rounds didn't come with the professional courtesy of slowing down first.
Across the green, Tank came through the treeline like weather with opinions, three inches of tungsten plate shrugging off small-arms fire the way a cliff shrugs off rain, shotgun knuckles doing brutal, economical work — one hit, one soldier down, no wasted motion, the plates on his forearms scoring and denting but never quite failing, because Tank didn't take damage so much as he spent it.
For about ninety seconds, against all reasonable math, it looked like they might actually win this one clean.
Then Ghostwire's voice came through wrong — cut off mid-word, static where a sentence should have been — and somewhere in the network he didn't control anymore, something looked back at him.
And the second wave arrived.
The retreat had a rhythm to it now — a brutal, unglamorous rhythm, closer to a controlled bleed than a battle. Twenty yards. Cover. Twenty more. The mother's arms had gone rigid around her daughter somewhere back at the tree line and hadn't loosened since, the girl's face still buried against her shoulder, small fists white-knuckled in her mother's shirt.
"Two hundred meters to the extraction corridor," Oracle said, and for the first time in longer than any of them liked, there was something in her voice that wasn't quite calm. "You are not moving fast enough."
"Working on it," Crimson said, through his teeth, through a drone-hop that carried him over a stalled car and put him between the mother and a burst of fire that chewed the asphalt where she'd have been standing. "It'll take me forty seconds to clear this block. They should be fine."
Should. He heard himself say it and hated the word immediately, the way you hate a bet the second it's out of your mouth.
Tank was answering the math a different way — plant, brace, absorb, advance, the oldest strategy in the world and somehow still the most reliable one in the yard: he simply stood in the places bullets wanted to be, three inches of tungsten shrugging off what would've opened lesser men like a can, and used the two seconds that bought to put a fist through a chestplate hard enough to fold the man inside it in half. Shotgun knuckles again. Again. The plating on his forearms was scored down to bare metal in three places now, but scored wasn't broken, and broken was the only number that mattered to Tank.
"Forty seconds you said." Tank's voice, low, unbothered, mid-swing. "You said that twenty seconds ago."
"Things escalated."
"Things always escalate with you."
"It's not my fault I'm interesting."
Hex's voice cut through, tighter now, the calm still there but load-bearing. "Twenty-two down to fourteen. I'm running out of clean angles, they're smart enough to use the wreckage—"
"Ghostwire's still dark," Riot added, already back in the fight herself now, streets sealed, throwing herself bodily into the gap between two soldiers converging on Crimson's blind side and putting them both down with the specific brutal efficiency of a woman who'd stopped enjoying this about six kills ago. "Whatever's in the network has him. I can't reach him."
"Copy. Focus the extraction." Crimson didn't let it slow him — couldn't, there wasn't a version of this where slowing down helped Ghostwire either — and threw himself back into the gap, two drones detonating in controlled bursts to clear a knot of soldiers regrouping near the corridor mouth, the wall thinning now, six left, five, another falling and another rising to fill the space before it hit the ground because that was just how it worked, that was just how it had always worked —
Thirty seconds.
The mother's foot caught a curb and she stumbled, went down to one knee with her daughter still crushed against her chest, and for one entire suspended heartbeat every gun in the block seemed to swing toward that single point of stillness —
Riot got there first. Threw herself into the gap like the physical law that governed her — bad luck redirected, always redirected, never landing where it was aimed — and three separate rounds meant for the mother's skull found empty air, a wall, a retreating soldier's own shoulder instead, because that was Riot's gift and it never once made sense and it never once failed either.
"Up," Riot said, hauling the mother by the arm, not gentle, not unkind, just fast. "Up, we're close, we're so close—"
Twenty seconds.
The corridor mouth was in sight now — the narrow gap between two buildings that Oracle had marked as the seam, the place the city still had a working exit if they could just get three more bodies through it before the block finished collapsing behind them.
Ten.
And that was when the ground changed its mind about being ground.
Not an explosion. Something heavier than that, something with a rhythm — one footfall, then the building three blocks over lost a window, then a second footfall closer, closer than that first one had any right to be this fast — and the mech came around the corner of the avenue like a verdict.
It was enormous in the specific, wrong way that made the eye refuse it at first — too tall, too deliberate, chassis the color of a threat assessment, sensor-array head tracking with the cold patience of something that had never once needed to hurry. It didn't run. It didn't need to.
It had already found the girl.
Six blocks west and one degree of geometry away, on a street that didn't know yet what it was standing this close to, Dave Chen was locking up his shop for the night with a fixed coffee machine under one arm, glancing up out of habit at a sky that had gone strange and orange near the horizon — and for one thin, unrepeatable sliver of a second, the angle of the street and the height of the mech and the specific tilt of Dave's head lined up into a geometry that would never exist again.
If he'd turned his head five degrees left, he'd have seen it.
He didn't.
He looked back down at the coffee machine, at four crooked gold stars someone was going to be so happy to see, and kept walking.
Behind him, six blocks and one universe away, the mech took its next step toward the girl.
Ghostwire's network was not a place. It only felt like one because his mind needed the metaphor to survive it.
He'd been three layers deep in the local grid, hunting the command relay, when the architecture around him had simply stopped agreeing with him. Doors that should have opened stayed shut. Paths he'd walked a hundred times folded back on themselves. And underneath all of it, patient as rot, something was watching him work.
"You're good," it said. Not words, not really — more like the impression of words, assembled out of packet-noise and static-hiss into something that approximated a voice. "Better than the last one."
"There's a last one?"
"There's always a last one."
He didn't waste breath on fear — fear was for later, fear was a luxury the mother two hundred meters away didn't have time for him to indulge — and instead threw himself sideways through a service tunnel he'd mapped weeks ago, half-forgotten, the digital equivalent of a back alley nobody bothered watching.
The shadow was already there.
Not following. Waiting. Which meant it wasn't reacting to him at all — it had known where he'd run before he'd decided to run there, and that single realization cost him more than the wall of firewalled traffic that came down around him a half-second later, boxing him into a dead corridor of dying protocol.
"They stopped understanding the language," the shadow said, and something about the phrase landed wrong, landed familiar, in a way he didn't have time to examine. "That's your friend's whole gift, isn't it. Restoration. Reconnection. I remember when I could still do that."
"What are you—"
"Focus on your firewall, little bird. You're leaking."
He was. He felt it now — the comm-jam he'd built to buy the team silence was thinning at the edges, reinforcement calls starting to leak through the cracks the shadow had opened just by being present, by pressing against his architecture the way a held breath presses against a chest. He threw everything he had left into patching it, brute-force and instinct over elegance, no time left for elegance —
— and somewhere in the real world, a second wave of vehicles that should never have found this street began finding it anyway.
"There," the shadow said, almost gently, almost fond, in a way that made his skin — his real skin, six blocks and one dying network away — crawl. "Good. Let them through. It's kinder, in the end."
Then it was gone, the way a held breath finally releases, and Ghostwire came back into his own body gasping, one hand clutched white-knuckled against the wall of a building he didn't remember leaning on, blood in his nose from a fight that had never once touched his body.
"Comms are—" His voice cracked. He tried again. "Comms are compromised. Second wave is already in. I couldn't— I lost it, I lost the block, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry—"
Nobody answered him.
Nobody had time to.

The mech's foot came down slow.
That was the thing nobody warned you about, watching something that size move — the horror of it wasn't speed, it was the absence of urgency, the way it simply did not need to hurry because nothing in its calculus included the possibility of missing. One footfall. A building lost a window. Sensor-array head already tracking, already fixed, already finished deciding.
The girl saw her teddy bear where it had fallen in the run — small, brown, one ear worn soft from years of exactly this kind of loving — and some six-year-old part of her, the part that hadn't yet learned that some things you don't go back for, made the decision before her mother's grip could stop it.
She twisted free. Two steps. Reaching.
"NO—" The mother's scream tore loose from somewhere below language, and she was moving, she was already moving, but two steps is nothing and everything all at once when the thing closing the distance measures its strides in city blocks.
Time didn't freeze. It just got very, very slow, the way it does for everyone standing close enough to a thing they can't stop.
The foot came down.
There wasn't a sound big enough for it. There wasn't a sound at all that anyone standing there would ever be able to describe afterward, because the mind has mercy sometimes, and simply declines to keep certain recordings.
The mother's scream changed register.
Something in her — something that had been sleeping the whole time, latent, dormant, waiting for exactly this specific unsurvivable weight of grief to wake it — came apart and reassembled wrong. Skin split into something that wasn't skin anymore. Her spine arched at an angle spines don't arch at. Whatever she was becoming, she was becoming it in the space of a breath, too fast to stop, too fast for anyone to reach her, a woman-shaped grief turning into something the world would call a monster and never once ask what had made her that way.
Around the corner, breathless, weapon already up because someone had radioed anomaly, live, engage, a rookie enforcer — young enough that his hands still shook between shots, young enough that this was probably his first live deployment, young enough that he would carry this for the rest of whatever life he had left — rounded into the open and found himself staring at a nightmare mid-birth with orders in his ear and no room left to think.
He raised the pistol.
He closed his eyes.
He pulled the trigger.
The round went exactly where he'd aimed it — center mass, straight at Riot, who'd thrown herself into the gap on pure reflex the second the mother's scream changed —
— and did not hit her. Never hit her. Riot's gift didn't dodge, didn't deflect, simply declined, the way it always declined, redirecting the universe's aim by some law none of them had ever managed to name —
— and found the mother instead, mid-transformation, mid-becoming, a single shot through what remained of a woman who twelve hours ago had been proud of four crooked gold stars on a coffee machine.
She dropped where she stood.
The rookie opened his eyes to what he'd done and did not move again for a long time.
And somewhere in the wreckage of a street that had, in the space of four minutes, stopped being anything like a normal Tuesday, the Wardens stood very, very still, and understood, all at once, exactly what kind of world they were actually fighting for.

## Chapter Six — Static

---

The first package arrived on a Wednesday.

Dave almost didn't notice it. It was sitting on the step when he came down to open up — brown paper, no return address, courier label from a company he didn't recognise that had nonetheless printed everything correctly and weighed and stamped it with the particular bureaucratic confidence of something that had been through the proper channels. The label said it was a rice cooker. The weight said it probably was.

He put it on the counter and opened it at noon, between jobs, when he had nothing more pressing than a ceiling fan with a failing capacitor and the particular variety of Tuesday that felt like it was dragging itself toward Wednesday through waist-deep water.

It was a rice cooker.

Mostly.

The housing was standard consumer-grade, the heating element was fine, the thermostat was mid-range but functional. The control board, however — the control board was something else entirely. Someone had replaced the factory unit with a piece of hardware that had no business being in a rice cooker. Military-adjacent tolerances. The kind of miniaturisation that cost more than the appliance was worth by a factor that made the whole exercise make no sense.

Also it was fried. Whatever it communicated with, it had been in the middle of communicating when something went wrong, and the board had taken the surge.

Dave looked at it for a while.

Then he fixed it.

He didn't think about it much after that. The board responded the way things responded to him — with a specific, quiet willingness that he'd stopped remarking on years ago. He boxed it back up, wrote a note with the diagnostic — *control board failure, replaced primary sync pathway, unit operational* — and put it by the door.

It was picked up the next morning before he came downstairs. No knock. No signature. Just gone.

---

The city had been different since the thing in the park.

Dave hadn't been in the park. Hadn't been anywhere near it. He'd been six blocks away with a coffee machine under his arm, which he knew because he'd looked up at the sky going strange and orange and felt that particular quality of air that preceded something he didn't want to be close to, and then looked back down and kept walking. He'd been doing that for thirty-two years. Looking up, assessing, looking back down. It was a survival skill, in this city. You learned to recognise the shape of things that were not your problem and treat them accordingly.

The news had been strange about it. Not about what happened — the news was always going to be strange about what happened — but about how strange it was being. There was the footage, shaky and cut before anything definitive, and there was the spokesperson cycle, and there were the graphics. And underneath all of it there was a specific kind of quiet that settled into the undercity the way cold settled into old buildings: through the cracks, slowly, until you stopped noticing it because it had become the temperature of everything.

People talked about it differently. Or didn't talk about it. Both, depending on who you asked.

Tak from the noodle stall hadn't mentioned it once, which was unusual because Tak mentioned everything. He'd come over for a cable repair and drunk three cups of tea and talked for forty-five minutes about a drainage problem three streets over, and the whole time the screen above Dave's bench was quietly cycling news graphics in muted subtitles, and Tak had kept his eyes very carefully somewhere else.

Dave had noticed. Had not said anything. It wasn't his job to say anything.

His job was machines.

---

The second package arrived Friday.

Kettle, said the label. Kettle it was, mostly, except for a heat-regulation module tucked into the base that was doing something no kettle needed to do. Same signature as the control board — same tolerances, same design logic, the kind of coherence that happened when one mind made every call. Different component, same conversation it had been having before something interrupted it.

He fixed it in twenty minutes. Left the same note. It was gone by morning.

On Saturday a blender arrived with a gyroscopic stabilisation unit that belonged in something airborne. On Monday a desk lamp came in with a sensor array so sensitive it could have tracked movement through three walls.

Dave fixed them all. Wrote his notes. Set them by the door.

He did not think too hard about the pattern because the pattern was not his problem. People brought him things. He fixed things. There was a logic to it that didn't require him to follow the thread any further than the bench in front of him. If someone wanted their gyroscopically-stabilised blender back in working order, that was between them and whatever they were blending.

The components always talked to him faster than they should have. That was the only thing he noted, in the specific, non-examined way he noted things that didn't fit the category of *my problem.* The sophisticated parts — the parts that had no business being in consumer appliances, the parts that cost more than everything around them — those were the ones that listened quickest. Like they'd been waiting. Like they were relieved.

He kept reaching for the old wrench.

---

Wednesday again. One week since the first package.

Dave was eating lunch at the bench — container of rice from Tak's, the deal they'd had for three years, repairs for food, nobody counting too carefully — when he became aware that the wall screen had been on the same segment for four minutes.

He looked up.

WARDEN ACTIVITY: WHAT DO WE KNOW? said the graphic, in the urgent red that the channel reserved for things it had decided the public should be urgently concerned about. The footage was the park again — they kept going back to the park — and then a cut to file images of the kind that always made Dave look somewhere else. He reached for the remote.

Stopped.

There was a woman on screen he didn't recognise. Not a spokesperson — spokespersons had the smooth, managed quality of people who had been trained to deliver information in ways that felt like information without containing any. This one was different. Academic, maybe. She was saying something about anomaly emergence patterns, about activation triggers, about the documented relationship between traumatic events and first manifestation, and her face had the expression of someone who had prepared for this interview by deciding in advance that she was going to say something true in it.

The subtitle read: *—no evidence the Wardens caused the incident. Significant evidence they were responding to one already in progress—*

Someone cut to commercial.

Dave put the remote down without using it. He ate the rest of his rice. He looked at the blank screen for a moment, then back at the bench.

There was a small component from the blender's stabilisation unit that he hadn't fully traced yet. He picked it up. Turned it over.

It was beautifully made. Whoever had built it had cared about it — you could tell, the way you could tell about his wrench, the way you could tell about Crimson's drones two months ago. Things made by people who gave a damn had a particular quality to them. Not visible, exactly. More like something the hands knew.

He set it down. Looked at the door.

Nothing.

He looked back at the component.

*Get it together*, he thought, in the way he'd been thinking it all week, at odd hours, for reasons he couldn't quite name.

He picked up the wrench.

Chapter Six — [Hex/Crimson interlude]

The workshop smelled like solder and cold coffee and the particular variety of silence that happened when two people were both pretending the silence was comfortable.

Hex was on the couch. She had her legs over the arm of it the way she always did, boots still on, a disassembled scope across her lap that she was cleaning with the focused attention of someone who needed their hands to be doing something specific. She'd been cleaning it for forty minutes. It was the cleanest scope in the city. She kept cleaning it.

Crimson was at the bench.

He had four drones open in front of him — casings off, internals exposed, the particular organised chaos of someone who knew exactly where everything was even when it looked like nowhere. He was rebalancing the rotor calibration on the nearest one, which he'd been doing for twenty minutes, humming something tuneless under his breath the way he did when he was thinking about something other than what his hands were doing.

Hex did not look at the drones.

This was a thing she was doing. Not looking at the drones. She had been not-looking at them for a week with the specific deliberate quality of a person not looking at something, which was different from simply not looking at something, and the difference lived entirely in the effort.

"You're quiet," Crimson said, without looking up.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're a different kind of quiet."

She ran the cloth through the scope housing. "Scope needed cleaning."

He made a sound that meant he'd accepted this and moved on, which was either because he had or because he was Crimson and had seventeen other things happening simultaneously in whatever passed for his attention. He picked up the drone he'd been working on, turned it, set it down again.

"Attrition's been weird," he said, to the bench mostly. "Losing fewer than I expected after the park. Thought the count would be worse."

Hex's hands did not stop moving.

"Hm," she said.

"Maybe I'm getting better at retrieval." He said it with the mild tone of someone genuinely considering the possibility, which meant he wasn't considering it at all, which meant he'd already moved on to the next thought. "Or the newer housings are holding up better. The tolerances on the last batch were tighter."

"Probably that," Hex said.

Crimson picked up a second drone. Turned it over. His thumb found a micro-fracture along the housing seam — small, already stabilised, the kind of repair that was almost invisible unless you knew what you were looking for.

He looked at it for a moment.

Hex looked at the scope.

"Huh," Crimson said.

"What."

"Nothing. Just — this one's been opened. Someone did a clean patch on the housing." He turned it over again, not suspicious, just interested the way he was interested in everything, the way that made every small thing an object worth examining. "Didn't do this one myself. Must've had Oracle look at it."

"Must've," Hex said.

She turned the scope over and started on the other side.

Crimson set the drone down and picked up the next one. His attention had already moved — she could tell by the way his posture shifted, the slight forward lean that meant he was back inside a problem, the humming resuming half a bar behind where it had stopped.

Hex looked at the drone he'd set down.

The patch was good. She knew it was good because she'd watched Dave's hands do it through the sensor array she'd tucked inside the desk lamp, which she was also not thinking about. He'd found the fracture in four minutes. Crimson had just found it in thirty seconds and concluded Oracle had done it, which was both wrong and fine, and would remain fine, because this was fine, everything was fine, she was cleaning a scope.

"You eaten?" Crimson said.

"Earlier."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what I answered."

He got up. She heard him in the back — the particular sequence of sounds that meant he was making something whether or not she'd confirmed she wanted it, because Crimson's relationship with other people's stated food preferences was more of a suggestion to him than a hard rule. A cabinet. The kettle. Something hitting a pan with the confidence of someone who cooked the way he did everything else, which was by committing fully and adjusting for reality afterward.

Hex set the scope down.

She looked at the drones on the bench. Four of them, open and patient, components sorted with the habitual care of someone who'd built each one himself and knew every piece by the weight of it.

She looked at the one with the patch.

Then she picked the scope back up and was cleaning it again by the time he came back with two bowls and the expression of someone who had decided unilaterally that this was happening.

She took the bowl.

"Thanks," she said.

"Don't mention it."

She didn't mention it.

Neither of them did.

Chapter Seven — [Crimson/Hex, range scene]

The range was three levels below the main floor, which was either paranoid or practical depending on how you felt about ricochet and Hex's definition of creative.

Crimson ran the sequence at speed.

It wasn't training, exactly — it was the thing he did instead of sitting still, which he was constitutionally incapable of for more than about four minutes before some part of him started looking for something to climb. The drones ran the course ahead of him, obstacle pattern, shifting formation, and he moved through and around and between them the way water moved through a system that had been designed for something slower. Foothold. Release. The gap between two drones that was only a gap for about a third of a second, which was fine, he only needed a third of a second.

Hex was at the far end with a coffee she wasn't drinking and the rifle she'd already cleaned twice this week.

She watched him the way she watched everything — through it, slightly, like the surface wasn't the interesting part.

He was on the ninth iteration when drone six dropped.

Not catastrophically. Not a failure anyone else would have caught in real time — a rotor stutter, a fractional loss of lift on the port side, the kind of thing that announced itself in the balance of the formation before it announced itself anywhere else. To anyone else it would have been a stumble, a missed step, a grab for something solid.

Crimson came off the wall, turned in the air, and caught it.

One hand. Clean. Like he'd already known.

He landed without looking at the ground.

He stood there for a moment, drone in hand, and then he turned and looked at Hex with the expression he had when he was reading something.

He said: "Port rotor assembly."

Hex didn't move.

"Calibration ring." He turned the drone over slowly. "Housing seam, lower third." A pause. "Sync pathway."

Each one landing in the silence like he was reading from a list he'd had for a while and was only now getting around to checking off.

Hex picked up her coffee. Put it down without drinking it.

Crimson looked at her for a long moment with the gold eyes that caught light like they were doing it on purpose and the expression that wasn't anger and wasn't hurt and was somehow harder to look at than either.

"Ok," he said.

Just that. The specific ok that wasn't ok, that had the weight and shape and temperature of a much longer conversation compressed into two letters by someone who had decided not to have the longer conversation yet. Maybe ever. The ok of a person who had received information and was now going to go somewhere quiet with it and take it apart the way he took apart everything.

He turned around.

He crossed to the equipment bench at the range's edge and set the drone down under the lamp. His hands moved through the familiar sequence — casing off, internals exposed — and then he stopped.

He looked at the sync pathway repair for a long time.

It was good work. That was the thing. It was genuinely, specifically good work — not patched, not stabilised-and-hoped-for, but understood, the fracture point addressed at the cause rather than the symptom. Whoever had done it had looked at the component and seen what it was trying to do and helped it do that better.

He knew one other person whose hands worked like that.

He'd met him in a repair shop in the undercity two months ago and thought about him with the specific frequency he reserved for things that didn't fit yet, things that were still assembling themselves into a shape he could read.

His thumb moved over the repair once. Twice.

Behind him Hex was very still in the way she was still when she was waiting to find out what something cost.

He didn't turn around.

"How many?" he said, quietly.

A beat.

"Six," Hex said. Just as quietly.

He nodded once, slowly, like this confirmed something rather than revealed it. His hand stayed on the drone.

"And."

It wasn't a question exactly. More like a space he was leaving for her to put something in if she wanted to.

The silence ran out to about ten seconds.

"He's good, Crimson." Her voice had the quality it got when she meant something more than she was saying. "The real kind."

Another long moment.

He put the casing back on the drone with the careful, even movements of someone buying time to think. Snapped it closed. Set it down.

"Ok," he said again.

This one was different. Still not ok, but a different not-ok — the one that meant he'd filed it somewhere and the file was now open and would stay open until he'd figured out what shape the thing inside it actually was.

He picked up the drone. Checked the balance. Set it back in formation.

"Run it again," he said.

Hex raised the rifle.

The drones lifted.

He didn't look at her again, which she understood was not the same as being done, and which she accepted because it was the most Crimson thing he could possibly have done, and because the six was out in the open now and the repair was in his hands and whatever came next was already inevitable.

She found her first target and let everything else go quiet.

Below the scope, Crimson moved through the formation like weather that had decided to have opinions, and drone six held its position, and the patch on the sync pathway held with it.

Chapter Seven — [Ghostwire]

He'd told Oracle first.

That was the order it had happened in — Oracle, because Oracle was who you went to when something had occurred that you didn't have a category for yet, when the event was sitting in you unprocessed and wrong and you needed someone who would receive it without making it smaller. She'd listened the way she always listened, which was completely, which was the thing about her that undid people who weren't used to it. No interruption. No reassurance offered before it was earned. Just the specific quality of attention that meant she was holding everything he said and not dropping any of it.

Then: "You're not damaged."

"I know."

"That's the first thing. Hold onto that."

He had. He'd been holding onto it for a week with both hands, which was how he knew he was more rattled than he was admitting, because he didn't usually need to hold onto things. Things just were. Ghostwire's relationship with the network was clean and functional and had never once looked back at him before.

He hadn't told Crimson.

This was not a decision he'd made formally. It was more that every time the shape of the conversation had presented itself — every time Crimson had looked at him with the particular attention that meant talk — he'd found somewhere else to be. Which was also not like him. He was not someone who found somewhere else to be. He was someone who went directly at things because going directly at things was faster and he valued fast.

Except apparently not this thing.

He was in the server room when Crimson found him.

Not hiding. Working. There was a difference, and the fact that the server room happened to be three levels down and required a keycard that most of the team didn't bother carrying was incidental and not worth examining.

Crimson came in without knocking, which was standard, and sat on the edge of the secondary console without being invited, which was also standard, and didn't say anything for long enough that Ghostwire knew he was going to have to be the one to start.

He kept his eyes on the screen.

He'd been avoiding them.

I remember when I could still do that.

Chapter Seven — [Crimson/Navi, the removal]

The lab was cold the way institutions were cold — not from any failure of the heating system but from a deliberate, policy-level indifference to whether the temperature felt like anything to anyone inside it. Functional range. Within parameters. The parameters did not include comfort because comfort had not been submitted as a requirement.

Crimson had been coming here for eight months.

He knew the walk from the elevator to the observation level the way he knew the drone course — by the body, below conscious thought, feet finding the route while the rest of him was already somewhere else. Past the first security door, badge held flat against the reader without breaking stride. The corridor that smelled like recycled air and something chemical underneath it that he'd stopped noticing around month three. The second door, heavier, the one that opened slow no matter how many times he'd come through it.

Then the glass.

He put his hand on the railing and looked at her.

She was — suspended was the word the documentation used. Suspended, which was accurate and said nothing, the way most of the documentation was accurate and said nothing. The tank was lit from somewhere beneath the surface, diffuse and bluish, and through it she was small and still in the way that things were still when stillness had been the whole of their existence. The apparatus was minimal now compared to the early months. They'd removed what they could when they'd established she was stable. What remained was what was necessary, which was still more than anyone who worked here seemed to find troubling.

Crimson found it troubling.

He found it troubling every time and said nothing about it every time because saying something about it would have ended the eight months, and the eight months were — he didn't have a clean word for what the eight months were. Necessary felt wrong. Important felt insufficient.

He came because she was here and no one else was coming.

That was the whole of it, if he was honest, which he mostly wasn't about this.

Behind him the door opened.

He didn't turn around. He knew the footsteps — Director Seo, and two others he'd placed by the third visit as senior oversight, the kind of titles that existed to provide distance between decisions and the people who made them. They stopped a few metres back. There was the sound of a tablet being reviewed, which was how these conversations always started, someone reviewing something so the conversation could be framed as a response to data rather than a choice.

"The exposure duration is becoming a liability," Seo said.

Crimson's hand stayed on the railing.

"The subject is responding to his presence in ways that aren't—"

"She has a name," Crimson said. Quiet. Flat.

A pause that had the quality of something being decided not to engage with.

"The attachment is creating variables we can't account for. The board has concerns about—"

"The board," said the other voice — Reiss, older, the one who occasionally pushed back not from principle but from the particular caution of someone who had been in institutions long enough to know that uncontrolled variables were expensive — "has also noted that the response metrics during his visits are the highest recorded. Consistently. By a significant margin."

"Which creates dependency."

"Which creates data."

Crimson looked at her through the glass.

She was still. She was always still. But he had learned, in eight months of this, to read the stillness the way he read drone formations — by what was underneath it, by the quality of the attention, by the thing that was happening below the surface that the surface didn't show. He had learned that she knew when he was there. Not how — he didn't have the framework for how, and neither did anyone here, and the fact that nobody here seemed to find that worth sitting with was one of the things he found troubling that he never said out loud.

She knew.

He could feel it the way he felt a formation shift — something orienting. Something that had been in one configuration moving into another because the conditions had changed.

Behind him the conversation continued in the measured, institutional tones of people deciding something they'd already decided.

"The risk assessment—"

"I've read the risk assessment."

"I understand," Crimson said, "that she is eight months further along than she was when I started coming here. I understand that the metrics you're citing as a liability are also the only metrics in this entire project that look like progress rather than maintenance." He didn't raise his voice. He didn't turn around. "I understand that what you're calling attachment I would call the only thing in this room that's treated her like she's a person."

Reiss said: "The decision has been made."

Crimson's hand tightened on the railing.

"We'll give you a moment," Seo said, with the tone of someone offering something they believed was generous.

He looked at her for a long time.

He didn't say anything. He wasn't sure, if he started, that he'd be able to do what he needed to do next, which was leave, which was the thing he was going to have to do because the alternative had already been decided in a room he hadn't been in by people who'd made sure he wasn't in it.

He put his palm flat against the glass.

Stayed there.

He walked out.

He did not look back because looking back would have cost him something he couldn't afford to spend in this corridor in front of these people, and he had learned a long time ago that some things you kept.

The red was there.

Then there was wrongness in the haze. The loud kind, shapes and patterns she had no framework for, a disruption in the ambient structure of everything that made her attention pull toward it the way attention pulled toward things that were different from the background.

The red was still there. She could feel the not-hurting of it, close against the glass, the warm-pattern present and then —

Strained. That was the closest thing she had to the sensation. The warm-pattern strained in a direction she couldn't follow, pulled by something outside the haze, outside everything she could perceive, and underneath it a quality she was only beginning to have the architecture to recognise.

Something that would eventually be called grief.

Not hers. His.

She didn't have the words for his yet. She just had the quality of it coming through the glass like a frequency, like the difference between cold that simply was and cold that had once been something else.

Then the warm red blur moved.

Away.

She tracked it as far as she could track anything — which was not far, which was the haze and the glass and the limit of everything — and then it was beyond the limit and then it was gone.

Not the gradual recession. Not the cooling she'd learned meant return.

Gone between one moment and the next.

She held the shape of where it had been.

The figures moved in their patterns beyond the haze. Things were checked. Things were noted. The cold pressed from all directions with the same indifferent, structural patience it had always pressed.

She waited.

In the place where the warm thing had been there was now just the cold again, and underneath the cold, patient as the cold, something else.

She didn't have the framework to mark it yet.

She just knew it was there the way she knew the cold was there — ambient, structural, simply present in the haze where the warm thing had been, settling into the space the warm thing had left with the ease of something that had been waiting for exactly this much room.

She didn't have a word for wrong yet either.

She was working on it.

The routine continued.

The particular lean of the shelf her mother had put up slightly crooked and never straightened because slightly crooked was how it had gone up and slightly crooked was how it stayed. The pot that lived on the back left burner because the back left burner ran hot and that pot had learned to manage it.

Her hand moved.

The lines came out right.

She heard Tank before she saw him — footsteps that were always a specific kind of deliberate, the consideration of someone who had learned early that their particular relationship with mass and momentum required advance notice in enclosed spaces. He came through the doorway and stopped, and she felt him take in the room the way he took in everything, the slow comprehensive attention of someone who didn't miss things and didn't make a production of not missing them.

She didn't look up.

He didn't say anything about the sketchbook.

He went to the kitchen — the real one, the team's, three doors down — and she heard the specific sequence of sounds that meant he was putting something together without making a decision about it, the way Tank fed people, which was without asking whether they were hungry because hunger wasn't always the point. Cabinets. Something on the stove. The low sound of something that needed time being given time.

She kept drawing.

The shelf came out slightly crooked.

She looked at it for a moment — at the involuntary accuracy of it, her hand reproducing the wrongness exactly right, the gift making perfect what her mother had left imperfect because that was what was true — and then she kept going because stopping felt worse than continuing.

Tank came back with two bowls and set one next to the sketchbook without comment. He pulled the chair across from her, the big one he'd dragged in from somewhere else months ago because the standard chairs had a way of expressing structural opinions about him, and sat down with the particular care of someone lowering a significant amount of decision onto furniture they respected.

He ate.

She drew.

The window above the sink. The light coming through it at the angle it came through in the morning, the early kind, before the day had fully committed to being a day. She'd sat at that table every morning for eighteen years watching that light move across the floor and she had not thought of it as something worth keeping until it was gone and now her hand was keeping it whether she'd decided to or not.

The pencil moved.

Outside somewhere the city made its city sounds, indifferent and continuous, the baseline hum of a place that didn't take stock of what happened inside its buildings.

After a while she said: "I used to think it meant I was good at things."

Her hand was still.

For now.

The last three drones were running hot.
Crimson felt them like fingertips — a phantom sensation spread across the neurolink, six operational seconds of sustained output before the heat sinks failed. He had three seconds of acrobatic budget left and a two-story armored skeleton between him and the end of the fight.
The mech moved like something that had forgotten it was supposed to be a man. Hydraulic legs punched craters into the rooftop concrete with each stride, its skeletal frame trailing sparks where Crimson's earlier swarm had chewed through the plating at its left shoulder joint. The arm hung wrong — not dead, not yet, just wrong — and the pilot was compensating with micro-corrections that should have been impossible to execute at this scale.
Should have been.
The pilot had given up his arms and legs a long time ago.
Crimson burned half a second reading the compensations, the weight shifts, the way the cockpit superstructure tilted fractionally before each swing. He was running the calculations the same way he always did — bone-deep and wordless, the kind of thinking that happened below language — and what the calculations said was: the left shoulder. Again. Hit it and make it commit.
He let himself drop off the ventilation stack.
The mech's right arm came around in a sweeping horizontal blow that would have taken him apart at the waist. Crimson dropped below it, one hand grazing the rooftop as he went into a rolling redirect. Drone Two burned through its heat sink to catch him at the apex of the roll — a half-second foothold three meters off the ground — and he pushed off it before the propellers gave out entirely. The drone fell. He didn't.

The shoulder joint was exposed where the plating had been stripped — a gap the size of a clenched fist, hydraulic cables braided together beneath. Crimson hit it at full momentum and drove his free hand in. Something gave. Something screamed — mechanical, high and wrong — and the arm seized.

He was already falling.
Drone One was there. Barely. It caught his left boot long enough to redirect the fall into something survivable, then it gave out in a shower of sparks and he hit the rooftop hard on his side, felt something crack in his ribcage, rolled because stopping wasn't an option. He could see inside.
Not a man in a seat. A torso — just a torso, the rest of him surrendered to the machine years ago, wired in at the sternum and the spine, cables running out of him like he'd grown them. His eyes were open and completely clear. No pain in them. Nothing that still lived in the body.
