# Chapter: The Angel in the Loop

The year was 2035.

Not *our* 2035—but on Terra Firma, a close cousin in an alternate timeline. A world not thriving, not collapsing… just existing. Still. Suspended in a kind of collective breath-hold, civilization moved like a rusted clock: functional, uninspired, eternally treading water.

Crimson was a fresh-faced rookie in the Regular Forces—an optimistic grunt with the brains of a himbo and the biceps to match. And he knew it. Owned it, even. Self-awareness was perhaps his greatest weapon, though he'd never need to draw it in combat.

It was a day off. The sun was being lazy, the skies just shy of sulking. So, with no orders and too much energy, Crimson decided to wander the town—a strange patchwork of polished marble and cracked chrome, of rusting scaffolds stitched with LED vines and neon signs flickering half-hearted promises. A city that had lived through too many aesthetic trends and refused to pick just one.

A new museum had just opened downtown. The exhibits were all recovered relics from a recent conflict fought on a neighboring planet—another quiet little war against yet another group of so-called "rebels." They were crushed with surgical efficiency, barely earning a second-page blurb in the morning newslate. No flags. No parades. The rebels had been erased, not just from existence, but from memory itself.

Still, curiosity tugged harder than indifference. Crimson paid the entry toll with a chunk of his modest soldier's pay and stepped into the towering structure—a coliseum of stone and circuit, echoing with Greek ambition and post-war sorrow.

The interior was as sterile as it was solemn. Hollow silence echoed between lonely pedestals. The exhibits were ornate, yet empty—dead things dressed like gods. Gilded, alien staff-weapons mounted on sterile displays, each spaced awkwardly far from the next as if proximity itself might trigger something... unfortunate. He passed a glass case containing what looked like a charred alien bookmark—or maybe a ceremonial blade that hummed faintly as he walked by. It was hard to tell, and harder still to care.

Then, something changed.

Tucked away in a darkened alcove—not listed on any visitor map—was a room unlike the rest. Gone were the minimalist spotlights and curated wall plaques. Here, the air felt heavier, electric with something unnamed. The room was completely dark, save for a single beam of light illuminating the center.

Floating within that spotlight was a screen.

And on the screen—her.

She was beautiful in a way that didn't quite feel safe. Celestial. Ethereal. A digital goddess rendered in such high fidelity she shimmered like captured starlight. Angelic, glowing softly, her image played on loop—just four seconds long—drifting downward in slow motion as a distorted vox-voice listed off battle dates and planetary coordinates in the background.

Other visitors moved quietly around the edges of the room. A small family, their child tugging gently at a sleeve. A university student scribbling notes. A couple of wine-sipping connoisseurs nodding solemnly at the craftsmanship, discussing render quality and artistic merit.

But something was off.

The air itched. The hairs on Crimson's neck rose in quiet rebellion. 

Had the loop... glitched?

He blinked. Watched again.

There. A flicker. Barely perceptible—but it was there. A fracture in the perfection, like a crack in divine porcelain.

And no one else had noticed.

Compelled by instinct—or perhaps fate—Crimson stepped closer, into the light.

A blur of motion interrupted him.

"Excuse me, sir, but you can't be touching the displays here, alright?" A museum guard materialized, positioning himself squarely between Crimson and the screen.

Crimson's eyes narrowed, hand still extended. His mind raced with violent possibilities. *Boy, I would fold you into a tessellated pretzel in half a Planck second...* But outwardly, he said nothing. After a long, quiet beat, he retracted his hand slowly, deliberately, locking eyes with the guard until the man awkwardly broke contact and shuffled back to his post.

Alone once more, Crimson turned his gaze back to the screen. He focused on the corner where he had seen the distortion—just a flicker, but it had been real.

"Hello?" he said, cautiously.

The display erupted.

**"PISSSSS OFFFF!"**

The voice cracked through the silence like a lightning bolt, distorted, mechanical, furious—and impossibly, heartbreakingly human beneath the static.

Crimson flinched—visibly, reflexively.

Every head in the room turned to look at him. A few arched eyebrows. One stifled chuckle. The family backed away slightly, the student furrowed his brow, and the wine-sippers returned to their art-sipping with disapproving murmurs.

But no one else had heard it.

No one else had heard *her*.

Blushing through his freshly dented pride, Crimson cleared his throat and gave a sheepish half-nod, as if he'd sneezed too loud or misread a social cue. But inside, a storm was already brewing.

This wasn't just a relic.

This was a cage—and something inside it was very much alive.

He left. But halfway to the exit, it came again—this time barely a whisper, threaded with exhaustion:

*"Please... just piss off."*

He froze. The voice didn't curse in hate—it begged. It carried the weight of eons, the hollow echo of someone who had screamed until her throat bled and now could barely manage a whisper.

A flood of memories surged up like bile. Fights. Screams. Moments of cruelty he'd buried deep in the trenches of his own psyche. But this voice... this was different. This was pain asking to be left alone to die in peace.

*Not this time.*

He turned, steadied his wobble, and marched back.

The alcove was now empty. The guard gone. The other visitors vanished like morning mist. Only the spotlight remained, the angelic loop still playing its four-second eternity.

Without hesitation, he reached out again. His hand passed through the projection.

"Oi," he said, sheepish.

No answer.

"OI!"

Static crackled. Then...

*"...please just stop it..."*

Something bloomed in him. Not hope—something bigger. Redemption, perhaps. Or recognition.

"OI! You in there! I can hear you!"

The loop froze. Everything stilled.

Silence stretched like a held breath.

Then darkness.

Then:

**"LEAVE! YOU'RE ANNOYING!!"**

The scream was cataclysmic. It flung him across the room, slammed him into marble with bone-jarring force. Pain exploded up his spine, stars burst behind his eyes.

But he stood. Bruised. Angry. Defiant.

He strode forward again.

This time, when he reached out, his hand *caught* something.

Cold. Rusted. Dripping.

A metal harpoon.

The illusion shattered like glass.

The radiant angel was no longer a looped avatar—she was real. Impaled. Tortured. Chained to mechanisms of suffering that hummed with malevolent purpose. Masked. Broken. Surrounded by instruments designed not just to cause pain, but to harvest it, distill it, display it as art.

She wasn't just a prisoner.

She was a warning. A trophy. A god reduced to exhibition.

And Crimson, whose fingers now gripped the harpoon lodged in her chest, felt sick. Rage. Sorrow. Something ancient and terrible stirring in his bones.

"You're beautiful to me," he whispered, the words falling like an oath into the sudden, profound silence.

Her eyes—what he could see of them through the mask—widened. Not with hope, but with something more dangerous: the faintest ember of belief.

Somewhere in the rafters, a siren began to wail.

## Days Beside the Cage

He came back.

Every day.

At first, the toll booth attendant mocked him. "Back to visit your holographic girlfriend, soldier boy?" The man's laughter was cruel, casual—the kind that comes easy when you've never looked closely enough to see the truth.

Crimson grinned and paid in coins, saying nothing. Let them think what they wanted.

Sometimes, he brought sandwiches. Other days, a carved trinket from the market—nothing valuable, just tokens of attention. Once, he crafted a wind chime from quartz and hollow bird bones, following her whispered descriptions of something from her home world, a place that existed now only in her memories.

She never left the cage. The harpoons stayed. The agony remained, a constant companion that had long since worn grooves in her soul.

But so did he.

He never tried to free her—not yet. He knew better. That wasn't the point, and perhaps more importantly, it wasn't what she needed. Not from him. Not now.

He just sat. Shared stories of his mundane military life, his terrible jokes that made her mouth twitch despite herself. Brought sunlight in the form of his presence and the kind of terrible puns that could make even gods groan.

He listened.

And slowly, impossibly, her head began to lift. The static in her voice softened from grinding machinery to something almost musical. The rage no longer exploded—it unraveled, thread by careful thread, revealing the person underneath.

They talked about small things: the way light bent through the museum's windows, the sound rain made on different worlds, the particular shade of loneliness that comes with being the last of your kind. He told her about drill sergeants and ration complaints. She told him about cosmic battles and the weight of divine responsibility.

They didn't exchange hearts metaphorically.

They *did*.

He had no power to save her—not yet, perhaps not ever.

But he gave her something else: Time. Attention. Presence. The radical act of witnessing without trying to fix, of seeing without turning away.

And somehow, in those hours spent beside her cage, sharing the quiet intimacy of acknowledged suffering, he came to understand the truth that the museum's sterile plaques could never capture:

She had once been a *god.*

And she had *fallen.*

But even a fallen star can burn bright when someone dares to look past the smoke and see the fire that still lives within.

The war that had "ended" with surgical efficiency was still being fought—not on distant planets, but here, in the space between seeing and understanding, between walking past and walking toward.

Every day he returned was a small victory against the machinery of forgetting.

Every day she lifted her head a little higher was proof that some things—love, hope, the stubborn refusal to let cruelty have the final word—cannot be conquered, only buried.

And what is buried can always, eventually, be unearthed.
Chapter Two: The Wolf Daimibu
The museum had become a routine. Crimson would wander in, nod to the same tollbooth clerk, and make his way to the alcove that wasn’t on any map. By now, he knew the rhythm: the hush of the marble halls, the faint hum of the angel’s cage, her breathing caught between static and silence.
Today was different. The quiet felt stretched too thin.
Crimson sat cross-legged in the halo of light, a sandwich balanced on his knee. “So then,” he was saying, mouth half full, “the idiot bites into what he thinks is a ration bar and—”
Through her mask, Sable shifted. Shoulders trembling just enough to betray it. A spark of sound escaped her, sharp and cracked. “…idiot…”
Crimson grinned like a fool caught stealing fire. That tiny flicker was enough to make his whole week.
Then the glass shattered.
Smoke rolled in first—acrid, stinging his eyes—followed by a thunder of boots. Figures poured into the hall, ragged in mismatched armor, masks painted with slogans they half believed.

“DOWN WITH THE TYRANTS!”
“FREE THE ANGEL!”
“TEAR THE CHAINS!”

They were amateurs. Not soldiers, but strays chasing the theater of rebellion. Their banners flapped like cheap imitations of conviction.
Sable jerked in her bonds. Static tore through her voice: “…no… not again…”
Crimson rose, the sandwich falling to the floor. For one last second, he wore the grin of the himbo who carved windchimes. Then it dropped, and something older stepped forward.
The Wolf Daimibu.
He didn’t roar. He didn’t posture. He moved.
The first rebel’s jaw snapped under his elbow. The second slammed spine-first into a column. By the time the third realized this wasn’t a protest but a slaughter, Crimson’s boot had already crushed his knee sideways.
The smoke turned copper-red. Screams tangled with slogans, then cut off short. It was over in minutes. A massacre, precise and merciless.
When the echoes died, Crimson stood slick with blood, chest heaving, hands trembling not with effort but with what she had just seen.
Sable’s head was tilted toward him, mask hiding most of her face, but her silence was thunderous. For the first time since he met her, she recoiled.
Before he could speak, the doors burst wide. Officers in polished brass, reporters with lenses, holo-drones buzzing like flies. They saw the carnage, saw the soldier still dripping with violence, and crowned him their champion.

“Hero of the Museum!”
“Guardian of the Angel!”
“Protector of our culture!”

They pinned medals on his chest while stepping over the groaning rebels. Holo-screens lit up with his face, his name, his “heroism.” The empire swallowed him whole in a single, well-rehearsed cheer.
And across the hall, Sable stared through the mask, through the harpoons, through the lies.
She had been made spectacle once more.
And now, so had he.
Crimson smiled for the cameras, but inside he was hollow. Every cheer was a chain. Every medal another bar welded across her cage.
And the only eyes that mattered would not meet his.

Chapter 2.5: The Wolf Becomes the Icon
The screens flickered to life behind him.
Crimson stood at attention on the marble stage, medals pinned fresh across his chest, each one a small anchor dragging him deeper into the thing he was becoming. The crowd stretched out before him—thousands of faces blurred into a single organism of approval, their applause a wave that crashed and crashed and never receded.
Behind him, twenty feet tall on the museum’s projection wall, the archive footage began to play.
[CLASSIFIED FOOTAGE — NEO-DAIMIBU CONFLICT — YEAR 2029]
The officials thought it would amplify the celebration. Look at our hero’s glorious history. But Crimson knew what was coming. His jaw clenched so hard his teeth ached.
The first frame: smoke rolling through narrow cyber-district streets, neon signs still flickering advertisements over rubble. Bodies sprawled in positions that suggested they’d been running. Not soldiers. Civilians caught between empire and insurgent, between ideology and survival.
The timestamp read 03:47. The Wolf hunted best in the dark.
SIX YEARS AGO: NEO-DAIMIBU
The city had been beautiful once. A jewel of the Eastern Sectors, where traditional architecture fought for space with server towers, where cherry blossoms fell on streets lit by holographic koi. Then the insurgency started—something about regional autonomy, about preserving culture against imperial homogenization. Crimson never cared about the reasons. He was eighteen, freshly enlisted, and hungry for purpose.
They called his unit in when the regular forces couldn’t root out the rebels. “Pacification specialists,” the brief said. The truth was simpler: they wanted someone willing to do what needed doing without asking questions.
Crimson discovered he was very, very good at not asking questions.
The first night, his squad swept through the Sakura District. Intel said rebels were using the market as a supply cache. His sergeant gave the order: “Secure the area. No one leaves.”
It started professional. Checkpoints. Searches. Then someone threw a bottle. Then someone else pulled a blade. Then the sergeant went down with a shard of glass in his throat, gurgling, drowning on air.
Something in Crimson woke up.
He didn’t remember giving the order to open fire. He remembered the weight of the rifle, the way it kicked against his shoulder like a heartbeat, the poetry of violence unfolding in slow motion. Twelve civilians dead in six seconds. Clean. Efficient. Necessary, he told himself.
But that wasn’t the Wolf yet. That was just a soldier doing soldier things.
The Wolf came later.
PRESENT: THE MEDAL CEREMONY
The footage shifted. Crimson watched his younger self on the screen—barely recognizable, thinner, wilder, covered in soot and something darker. The crowd murmured in appreciation. “Look how young he was!” “Such dedication!”
They didn’t see what he saw: the smile.
From her cage at the side of the stage, Sable watched the screens. She’d been wheeled out for the ceremony, a living backdrop to his triumph. The harpoons dug deeper with every jostle of the chains, but she’d learned to breathe through pain. This was different, though. This was watching the myth of her savior unravel in real time.
On screen, young-Crimson crouched in an alley, rifle forgotten, hands red to the elbows. A rebel—or maybe just someone in the wrong place—lay twitching at his feet. And Crimson was smiling.
Not a grin. Not the himbo fool’s expression she’d come to recognize. This was something primordial. Joy without humanity. The expression of a predator who had just discovered it loved the hunt more than the meal.
Static rippled through her mask. What are you?
NEO-DAIMIBU: WEEK TWO
The insurgency didn’t collapse. It metastasized. Every rebel cell they crushed spawned two more. Command grew frustrated. Casualties mounted. The rules of engagement became suggestions, then memories, then fairy tales soldiers told to feel civilized.
Crimson’s squad got the reputation. Not for effectiveness—though they were effective—but for enthusiasm.
“Wolf Pack,” the other units called them, half admiration, half disgust. But really, there was only one Wolf. The rest were just trying to keep up.
He stopped sleeping in the barracks. Started disappearing for hours, returning with blood under his nails and reports of “enemy combatants neutralized.” Command didn’t ask for details. The numbers looked good on paper.
The locals started whispering. Ōkami no Daimibu. The Wolf of Daimibu. A ghost story told to keep children indoors after dark. A monster that wore the empire’s colors but answered to no flag, no cause, no god but violence itself.
Crimson heard the stories and felt something warm bloom in his chest. They fear me. Good.
The night it all went wrong started like any other. Intelligence—always intelligence, always wrong—pointed to a “high-value target” in the old temple district. Crimson took six soldiers. They needed two.
He kicked down the door to the safe house and found a family: mother, father, two children. The father held a comm-device. “Rebel coordination,” someone shouted, and that was enough.
Crimson pulled the trigger before he realized he’d made the decision.
The father dropped. The mother screamed and lunged—not with a weapon, just with grief-mad hands—and someone else fired. The children ran for the door.
Crimson moved without thinking. Not to stop them. To chase.
The blood haze took him fast.
The world narrowed to scent and motion. Small prey. Running. The oldest, oldest instinct rising through the training like a tide. He felt the Wolf surge up from the base of his spine, hot and laughing, and he let it. The override was clean. The chase was pure.
He caught the woman first.
She fought. He took what he wanted anyway—teeth at her throat, hands pinning, the animal rhythm of it blotting out everything else. When it was done he left her broken on the floor and went after the children. They were small. Fast for their size. He ran them down through the rain-slick alleys like it was the only thing that had ever mattered. One. Then the other. The second one made a sound that cut off too quickly.
By the time it was over, the Wolf was still riding him.
He stood under a flickering streetlight, blood on his hands, blood on his face, blood cooling on the wet pavement. The two small bodies lay where they had fallen. He looked at them and giggled—soft at first, then louder, a bright, broken sound that didn’t belong to any soldier he had ever been.
His squadmates found him like that. Sitting in the rain under the streetlight, two small corpses at his feet, giggling like a child who had just discovered something wonderful and terrible at the same time.
They never mentioned it in the report. Some things were too honest for paperwork.
PRESENT: DAY TWO POST-CEREMONY
The press conference was held in the museum’s grand hall. Crimson sat at a table draped in imperial purple, microphones thrust toward him like accusing fingers.
“Tell us, hero—what drove you to protect our Angel with such ferocity?”
The script was in his pocket, provided by the Ministry of Culture. He pulled it out with hands that didn’t shake anymore. They’d stopped shaking after Daimibu. “Duty,” he recited. “Love of empire. Respect for history.”
The words tasted like ash, but he smiled. The cameras loved him. Behind him, on every screen, they played highlight reels: Crimson standing victorious over the museum rebels, Crimson accepting medals, Crimson waving to children who chanted his name.
In the corner, Sable’s cage sat silent. They’d positioned her perfectly—close enough for him to glance at, far enough that they couldn’t actually speak. Her mask concealed expression, but he felt her stare like a brand.
A reporter leaned in, voice dripping curiosity: “And the Wolf of Daimibu? We’ve heard stories. What do you say to those who claim you were… excessive?”
The room held its breath.
Crimson felt the old thing stir in his chest, stretching claws against his ribs. For a heartbeat, he imagined leaping the table, imagined showing them excessive, imagined reminding this soft city-creature what real fear tasted like.
Instead: “War requires difficult choices. I made them so others wouldn’t have to.”
Applause. They always applauded the lies that let them sleep better.
Sable’s static broke through the noise, too low for anyone else to hear: “Liar.”
He met her eyes. Didn’t deny it.
NEO-DAIMIBU: THE FINAL NIGHT
Three months into the occupation, command declared victory. The insurgency was “dismantled.” Survivors were rounded up for processing. Crimson and his squad were to sweep the outer districts one last time, mop up stragglers, then ship home as heroes.
He found the last rebel cell in an abandoned subway station. Seven fighters, teenage to middle-aged, exhausted and out of ammunition. They surrendered immediately.
Protocol said: detain, document, deliver to military courts.
Crimson looked at them—these people who’d fought for something they believed in, however misguided—and felt nothing but contempt. Not for their cause. For their weakness. They’d lost, which meant they deserved to lose, which meant they deserved whatever came next.
“Line them up,” he told his squad.
“Crim, they surrendered. Rules of—”
“Line. Them. Up.”
They obeyed. They always obeyed the Wolf.
Seven shots. Seven bodies. Seven fewer complications for the bureaucrats to process. His report would call it a firefight. Command would believe it because believing was easier than investigating.
He walked out of that subway covered in more than blood. He wore guilt like a second skin, shame like a crown, and beneath both: the terrible, undeniable truth that some part of him had loved every second.
The empire pinned a medal on him three days later. “For exemplary service in the pacification of Neo-Daimibu.”
He accepted it with a smile that didn’t reach his eyes and a promise to himself: Never again.
He’d kept that promise for six years. Right up until the museum. Right up until Sable.
PRESENT: DAY FOUR — THE PARADE
They put him in an open vehicle, the kind conquerors ride through subjugated cities. The route wound through downtown, past government buildings draped in banners bearing his face, past crowds ten-deep screaming adoration.
“CRIMSON! CRIMSON! CRIMSON!”
Confetti rained like ash. Children waved flags. Someone threw flowers that landed at his feet—red blooms that looked too much like spilled organs.
He waved. Smiled. Played the part. Inside, the Wolf paced its cage, snarling at the bars.
In the vehicle behind him, they paraded Sable’s cage. The crowds cheered for her too, though differently. For him: respect, admiration. For her: reverence. She was their saint of suffering, their beautiful tragedy, their proof that even gods could be broken and made useful.
Crimson glanced back once. Their eyes met through the sea of noise.
In that look: recognition. Not forgiveness. Not absolution. Just the acknowledgment that they’d both been caught by the same machinery, that the empire didn’t just conquer people—it repurposed them. It took the Wolf and made him a shepherd. It took the Angel and made her a relic.
The parade rolled on. The crowd roared. The cameras captured every second for posterity.
Neither of them smiled.
PRESENT: DAY FIVE — REHEARSAL
The festival stage was enormous, built specifically for the Order of the Angel’s inaugural ceremony. Crimson stood at the mark where they’d positioned him, a mayor’s assistant fussing with his collar.
“When the choir reaches the crescendo, you approach her cage. Slowly—reverence, remember. Then the kiss. Just on the mask, nothing inappropriate, but tender. We want the crowd to feel the devotion.”
They’d practiced this six times already. Each time felt like swallowing glass.
Across the stage, Sable hung in her apparatus. The harpoons had been polished for the occasion. The mask freshly cleaned. Her suffering, dressed up for public consumption.
The assistant clapped. “Alright, one more time from the top!”
Crimson walked his mark. Approached the cage. Leaned in.
Up close, he could see through the mask’s eye-slits. Could see her watching him. Could see the question burning there: Is this what you are? Just another tool for them to use?
He wanted to explain. Wanted to tell her about Daimibu, about the promise he’d made, about how every day he sat with her had been an act of penance, of proving to himself he could be gentle, that the Wolf could learn to be quiet.
But explanations were luxuries neither of them had.
His lips touched the cold metal of her mask.
The assistant applauded. “Perfect! That’s the shot!”
Crimson stepped back. Sable’s head tilted slightly—a gesture so small only he caught it. In it: disgust. Or perhaps pity. He wasn’t sure which was worse.
THAT NIGHT: ARCHIVAL REVIEW
Sable’s cage was returned to its alcove after rehearsal. Alone in the dark, she accessed the museum’s systems—a trick she’d learned weeks ago, her consciousness still digital enough to slip through networks when the pain wasn’t too consuming.
She pulled up every file on Crimson she could find.
Neo-Daimibu incident reports. Squad evaluations. Redacted documents marked [CLASSIFIED]. And buried deep: helmet-cam footage from the final night.
She watched the subway execution. Watched his face. Watched the thing that lived behind his eyes.
This is my guardian, she thought, static crackling through her mind. This is the boy who brings sandwiches.
Both were true. That was the horror.
He wasn’t a monster wearing a human mask. He was a human who’d become a monster, then fought his way back to something resembling humanity, only to be turned into a symbol by the very system that had created him.
The empire didn’t need to break him. He’d already been broken. They just needed to aim him.
And now they’d aimed him at her.
The footage ended. The screens went dark. Sable hung in her chains and understood:
Tomorrow, they would make him kiss her in front of thousands. Would make him the priest of her perpetual passion play. Would forge a mythology where his redemption was built on her continued suffering.
He would let them.
Not because he was cruel, but because he’d been trained so well to obey that disobedience wasn’t even a thought anymore—just a ghost of a possibility, haunting the edges of his exhausted mind.
I will never forgive you, she thought, not as vow but as statement of fact. Not because you’re evil. But because you’re weak. And I cannot afford to love weakness anymore.
The museum settled into night-silence. Tomorrow would bring pageantry, worship, the birth of a new order.
Tonight, in the dark, two cages sat side by side:
One made of metal and torment.
One made of medals and applause.
Both unbreakable.
Both eternal.
Both exquisitely designed to hurt.
Tomorrow, they would write a new chapter of the Order. And the first verse would not be his violence. It would be his kiss.

Chapter Three: The Order of the Angel

They dragged her cage into daylight.

Chains screamed against stone as soldiers wrestled the machinery of her torment onto the marble balcony. Harpoons tore at her chest with every jolt. The crowd below cheered as though pain were music.

From behind her mask, Sable could see the city gathered—faces blurred into a single beast of hunger, shouting, weeping, exalting. The empire had named the festival in her honor: The Order of the Angel.

Crimson stood beside her. Not the boy who brought trinkets and bad jokes. Not the soldier who sat in silence. No—today he wore a polished uniform, medals pinned across his chest like nails hammered into a coffin.

She could see the tremor in his jaw, the way his eyes kept flicking toward her—pleading, ashamed—but the crowd saw none of that. They saw a savior.

He stepped forward to the podium.

“Brothers, sisters,” his voice boomed through the amplifiers, “today we stand united by sacrifice. By her sacrifice.”

The words weren’t his. They dripped from cue cards handed to him by officials, staged like liturgy. The crowd roared approval.

Sable’s stomach turned. They weren’t celebrating her—they were consecrating her cage. The pain was the point.

And then the final blow:

The mayor, fat with ceremony, leaned toward Crimson with a grin too wide. “Show them what devotion looks like,” he whispered into the mic, stage directions hidden in plain sight.

Crimson froze. The crowd leaned in.

He turned. Looked at her through the mask, the harpoons, the agony. For a heartbeat, she saw the boy who once sat cross-legged with a sandwich on his knee. Then he leaned down and pressed his lips gently against the rusted mask that covered her mouth.

The crowd erupted. Thunderous applause. Chants of “The Angel blesses us!” Banners waved, drones spun overhead capturing the moment from every angle.

Sable’s heart split.

Not because of the kiss—but because she understood now: to them, he was her priest, her consort, her keeper. And she was their holy reliquary of suffering.

The boy she had begun to trust was becoming their weapon.

Inside, her mind screamed. Is this all you are? Another cage, softer, but still a cage? Another mouth that speaks for me while I choke on silence?

But outwardly, she said nothing. Her voice, her fury, her plea—all were drowned in the chants of the faithful.

Crimson raised his hand in salute, the cameras feasting on him. His smile was practiced, hollow, breaking at the edges.

Sable saw it, but the crowd did not.

And in that moment, she understood the cruelty of empire more than ever: it didn’t just chain bodies. It chained love. It forced affection into theater. It made even tenderness a weapon.

The Order of the Angel was born, baptized not in hope but in humiliation.

And from her cage, Sable swore she would never forgive the boy who let them make her a saint of suffering.

Chapter Four: Ashes Behind the Curtain

The festival was over.

The streets outside still throbbed with drunken songs and the echoes of banners cracking in the wind, but the museum had fallen silent again. The marble corridors, emptied of crowds, echoed only with the shuffle of guards and the low hum of cooling projectors.

Crimson walked back alone. The medals weighed heavier tonight. He hadn’t taken them off — hadn’t dared — but each one felt like a brand pressed into his chest.

The alcove glowed faintly. The cage had been returned to its pedestal. Sable hung in it, harpoons gleaming with the polish of ceremonial cruelty, mask still slick from the breath of speeches she hadn’t spoken.

She didn’t turn when he entered.

For a long time, neither spoke. The air between them was thicker than smoke.

Finally, Crimson broke. “It wasn’t supposed to be—”

Her voice cut him like a razor, static laced with fury. “Supposed to be what? A kiss for empire? A holy relic for them to worship while I choke on chains?”

He flinched, but pressed on. “If I’d refused, they would’ve taken me away. I had to—”

“Had to what?” The words rattled the glass, carried more force than any siren. “Had to lie? Had to smile? Had to let them build their Order on my body and your obedience?”

Crimson dropped to his knees, head bowed, medals clinking like shackles. “I only did it so I could keep coming back to you.”

Her silence was worse than rage.

Inside her, memory and fury churned. She saw the boy with sandwiches and trinkets — the one who sat cross-legged in defiance of despair. She saw the Wolf on the screens, smiling in Daimibu’s blood. She saw the priest on the balcony, lips against her mask as the crowd howled devotion.

Sable studied him like a problem she had once given up on but was now, inconveniently, re‑evaluating. “If we go down there,” she said at last, “I can try to invert the control flow. Turn the cage from a broadcast device into a receiver. Instead of them projecting myth through me, I pull voices in. Let people speak through the same channels they used to silence me.”

Crimson blinked. “You want to hijack the state religion and turn it into open mic night?”

“Revolutions have started with less,” she said. ⚡

A search drone buzzed past the mouth of the alley, spotlight slicing white across brick. Crimson froze, one hand on the cage frame like it was a living thing that might spook.

When the light passed, he exhaled. “Okay. Sub‑basement vaults. We ghost our way back toward the museum, just not through the front door this time.”

“You will have to fight,” she warned.

“I know.”

“You will like it.”

He didn’t answer that. Did not deny it either.

She watched his silence, then gave a small, tired nod. “I’ll ration power. Short bursts to mask us from thermal scans. After that I’m running on fumes until we reach the node.”

“Story of my life,” he muttered, sliding his shoulder under the cage frame again. Muscles screamed in protest as he lifted. “You fry gods. I carry furniture. Teamwork.” 💀

This time the sound she made was unmistakably a laugh, thin and broken but real.

They slipped from the alley into the maze of service streets, moving opposite the flow of patrols, guided by half‑remembered maintenance maps from her stolen schematics and his soldier’s instinct for blind spots.

Above them, the city churned. Sirens. Broadcasts. Conflicting rumors racing faster than any official statement.

Some said the Angel had spoken.
Some said the hero had snapped.
Some said the end times had begun.

Below it all, beneath myth and panic and marching boots, two figures moved toward the roots of the machine that had defined them.

Not to become symbols again.

But to make the system listen back.
