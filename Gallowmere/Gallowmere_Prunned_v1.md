# Gallowmere
Gallowmere Abbey Academy did not teach you how to win. It taught you how to make the loss of your opponent inevitable.

Its curriculum was built on three overlapping disciplines: the Severance, blade work refined until it could cut intention and identity as cleanly as flesh; the Veil, misdirection and moving through an opponent's perception; and the Reckoning, the rarest path, where a blade became a ritual instrument that could carve temporary laws into localized reality at terrible personal cost.

The crest was a black rose wrapped around a broken sword, petals dripping liquid light. The place had begun as a cloistered order preserving the geometry of severance. During the Fracturing it had been seized and sharpened into something else. Most who graduated did not become duelists. They became Wards, Cutters, or Witnesses who operated in the spaces between the Redverse's layers.

The current Head Instructor was known only as Dame Vesper. She had not removed her black lacquer mask in thirty-one years.

Crimson Valentine had never attended. Sable had walked its halls in dreamspace more than once.

---

Rain turned the courtyard stones dark and slick. Two final-year students moved in near silence. One had already taken three clean cuts. The shorter one was faster but beginning to tire.

Dame Vesper watched from the upper cloister without expression.

The shorter student lunged. The taller one stepped into the line, accepting a shallow cut across the shoulder to land a single perfect thrust beneath the sternum. The body folded. Blood ran toward the drainage grate beneath the eastern arch.

Vesper had watched this forty-one times. It was not cruelty. It was clarity. The world outside already carved people apart slowly. Here, when the blade fell, the one who remained had earned the right to carry what they had done.

A soft chime at her throat.

“Vesper.”

Sable’s voice, low and velvet-distorted.

“You only call when you want something carved,” Vesper said.

“I want you to look at someone.”

“I do not take students on recommendation. Not even from you.”

“This one isn’t a student. Not yet. And he may never be. But he already walks with a blade that cuts illusion. I want you to see him properly.”

“Name.”

“Crimson Valentine.”

The name settled like a blade laid flat.

Vesper was quiet. “I have heard it. The one who burned his own life down and kept walking. The one who let you choose him back.”

Sable’s voice softened. “He is not like the others chasing power or forgiveness. He already knows how to bleed. What he does not know is how to cut cleanly when the thing in front of him is wearing his own face.”

“You want me to test him.”

“I want you to see him. If he is worthy of the Reckoning, then he is worthy of Gallowmere’s attention. If he is not… then I will keep him myself.”

Vesper turned from the courtyard at last.

“Send him the invitation,” she said. “But do not promise him welcome. Gallowmere does not offer welcome. Only the chance to prove he should not have come.”

On the other end, Sable sounded satisfied. “That’s all I ask.”

The link died.

Vesper remained a long time after the body was removed. The rain had not eased.

The masked comm-link pulsed again, softer.

“…You’re certain about this one.”

“I’m certain he’s already bleeding,” Sable said. “I’m certain he’s still standing. And I’m certain that if someone doesn’t show him how to cut without destroying himself in the process, he will keep using that blade on everything that gets too close — including me.”

“You are asking me to take responsibility for him.”

“I’m asking you to look at him. If you decide he is not worthy, I will take him back as he is. But if there is even a chance he could learn to wield that blade without it turning on him… then I want that chance for him.”

Another silence.

“Send the invitation,” Vesper said at last. “But understand this, Sable — if he steps onto these grounds, he will not be protected from what happens here. Not by me. Not by you.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“…I’m trying to.”

The link went quiet.

Vesper stood alone in the rain, wondering which version of him would walk through the gates.

---

Sable moved through the upper corridors like smoke with purpose. She would not deliver the invitation herself. That would be too direct. If Crimson was going to step onto Gallowmere ground, it had to be his choice.

She descended the narrow spiral stair into the lower archives. The stones here were older than the Academy above. This was where the original order had kept its true records.

She stopped before a low iron door. No handle. A single symbol carved into the metal: a black rose whose petals had been deliberately cut away, leaving only stem and thorns.

Sable placed her palm against it. The door opened with absence.

Inside, a circular room. An obsidian table. Seven empty chairs. A silver stylus and a sheet of vellum that had never been written on.

She drew out a small, folded piece of crimson silk from her coat and placed it in the center. Old fabric, worn thin, carrying the faint scent of smoke and rain.

“I need this to reach him without my hand being too obvious,” she said. “He will not come if he thinks this is a leash. He will only come if he believes the choice is still his.”

The air shifted. One of the chairs creaked softly.

“I know what it will cost if he accepts,” she continued. “I also know what it will cost if he keeps swinging that blade without ever learning how to sheath it.”

The vellum moved on its own. The stylus rose and began to write in clean, deliberate strokes.

When it lowered, the invitation was complete.

She folded it and slipped it into her coat. The silk remained on the table.

“Thank you.”

As she turned, a voice that was not quite a voice brushed her mind — dry, ancient.

*He will hate you for this.*

Sable’s smile was sharp and fond. “I know. That’s how I’ll know he read it.”

She stepped back through the iron door.

The old channels did not work like normal messages. They moved through absence — through places where attention had been deliberately withdrawn for so long that something else had grown in the gaps. They responded to offerings that carried weight.

The crimson silk was a piece of the coat Crimson had worn the night he walked away from everything he had built. That moment had left a severance so sharp the channels could still taste it. By offering it, Sable was feeding the absence.

The old debts do not care about morality. They care about weight.

---

The invitation arrived as a plain envelope slipped beneath the door of the half-collapsed workshop Crimson had been using. The black wax was still slightly warm.

Inside, a single sheet of vellum. No greeting. No signature. Seven lines:

You have been seen.

Gallowmere Abbey Academy extends no welcome.

If you choose to come, you will be tested.

If you survive the testing, you will be offered a choice.

If you accept the choice, you will not leave as you arrived.

The decision is yours.

Do not bring anything you are not willing to lose.

Crimson read it twice. The third time he held it to the weak light. It did not shimmer. It was just ink, but it sat heavier than paper had any right to feel.

He laughed, quiet and rough.

“Of course she didn’t deliver it herself. She knew I’d throw it back in her face if she did.”

He folded the letter and slipped it into the inner pocket of his coat, over his heart.

For a long moment he stared at the rain beyond the cracked window.

“If this is some old debt trying to collect through me,” he said aloud to the empty room, “if someone up there thinks I’m going to finish what they started with my family… I won’t. I don’t care what the numbers say. I don’t care who would finally sleep easier. There are some cuts I’m not willing to make. That’s final.”

He didn’t pack much. A change of clothes, the small blade he trusted, and the letter. He had gotten good at walking away from things that tried to own him.

Before he stepped into the rain he paused.

“…If you’re listening,” he said quietly, “just know I’m not coming to settle anyone’s score. I’m coming because I want to know if there’s a version of this blade that doesn’t eventually turn on the people I care about. That’s it.”

He pulled his hood up.

“And if that’s not enough for them… then I’ll walk back out the same way I walked in.”

The door clicked shut.

Sable felt it the moment he made the decision. Not through vision. Just a quiet shift in threads she had learned to notice.

She stood on the balcony of the old cathedral spire. The city stretched below in neon and shadow.

“…Don’t let them turn you into something you’ll hate,” she whispered.

---

The obsidian table in the lower archives did not go still.

When Crimson spoke his refusal in the workshop, the room felt it. One of the seven chairs creaked. The temperature dropped. The vellum fluttered.

*He refuses the cut.*

*Valentine blood has always been stubborn. That is why we marked them unfit.*

*Unfit for sharpening. Not unfit for carrying weight.*

A colder presence moved through the stones.

*He was given the chance to balance an old ledger. He chose instead to keep the wound open. He chose to keep his soul heavier than it needed to be.*

The oldest voice answered from deeper in the walls.

*That is precisely why the invitation was sent.*

Silence listened.

*He will not perform the severance we once considered necessary. He will not cut the head from the betrayer. He has already decided this.*

*What, then, does he bring to Gallowmere?*

*He brings a blade that already knows how to cut illusion… and has chosen, repeatedly, not to use it on the people he loves. That is rarer than power. That is what makes him dangerous to us.*

The room grew still.

*He refused the clean ending. He chose the heavier path. That choice has weight. It feeds the old channels whether he intends it or not.*

*Gallowmere will test him anyway.*

*Of course it will. But the question is no longer whether he can survive the testing. It is whether the testing will survive him.*

New words appeared beneath the invitation, faint unless the light hit at exactly the right angle:

He has already passed the first test.

He refused to become what we once needed him to be.

The room reacted with something closer to hunger.

The old channels had not sent the invitation to settle an old score. They had sent it because something in them recognized a rare thing: a man who could carry impossible weight without letting it turn him into a monster.

And monsters were what Gallowmere had been quietly collecting for centuries.

---

Rain.

Not this rain.

Another rain. Thicker. Wrong color. It fell upward in places.

A chamber that wasn’t a chamber. Walls made of decisions already unmade. Someone screaming without sound. A blade that wasn’t a blade cut through something that wasn’t flesh but still bled names.

A man stood at the edge. He did not move. He did not pick up a weapon. Not tonight. Not for this.

The severance tore through everything at once.

When it was over, the man was still standing. Most of what he had been standing beside was gone. Absent. The kind of absence that left holes in reality where memory used to live.

He looked down at his shaking hands.

He did not cry. He did not rage. He simply turned and walked away from the place where something fundamental had been cut out of the world.

Behind him, the old channels stirred for the first time in centuries, tasting the weight of what had just been witnessed and left unbalanced.

The man never spoke of it again.

But the debt remembered.

---

Crimson stopped at the edge of the old path.

The rain here was normal. Cold. Real. In front of him the gates of Gallowmere rose out of the mist like the bones of something that had died standing up. Black iron twisted into roses and thorns. No guards. No lights. Just the quiet certainty that the stones were watching.

He touched the letter through his coat.

The gates did not open. They simply were open, in the way some doors only reveal themselves once you have already decided to walk through them.

He stepped forward.

The moment his boot touched the stone on the other side, the rain stopped. The air grew heavier and carried the faint metallic scent of old blood and wet iron. The path ahead was lined with black rose sigils glowing faintly beneath the stone.

At the far end stood a single figure.

Dame Vesper waited beneath the broken arch of the eastern bell tower, hands clasped behind her back.

Crimson stopped ten paces away.

“You came,” she said.

He looked at her, rain still dripping from his hood, that familiar sharp-edged grin pulling at the corner of his mouth.

“You already refused one of our older suggestions,” Vesper said. “The clean ending. The balancing of the old Valentine ledger. You spoke it aloud. The channels heard you.”

“I’m not here to finish what someone else started,” Crimson said. “I don’t care how many people would sleep better if I did. I’m not cutting that head off. Not for you. Not for the debts. Not for anyone.”

The mask tilted slightly.

“You understand that this place was built on the very kind of severance you are refusing.”

“I understand. And I’m still here.”

Vesper moved just enough to gesture toward the dark archway behind her.

“The first test is not a blade. It is a question. You may answer it now, or you may turn around and walk back through those gates. No one will stop you. But if you stay, the question will follow you until you answer it.”

“Ask.”

Vesper’s voice dropped lower, almost gentle.

“What are you willing to lose in order to keep the parts of yourself you have already decided are non-negotiable?”

Crimson held her masked gaze.

“Nothing,” he said.

Vesper tilted her head.

Crimson’s grin widened, full-chested and unapologetic.

“Nothing. Because I owe no debt to anyone. I have always lived honest on that line. However — I welcome the test. There has never been a head that the Valentines have failed to protect. I stand here only to prove that power is not a price. It is a soul that never needed permission.”

The words landed heavy. The black rose sigils along the path pulsed brighter, as if something beneath the Academy had just sat up and taken notice.

Vesper remained perfectly still.

When she finally spoke, her voice had lost some of its dryness.

“You refuse the old debt. You refuse the clean severance. And now you stand at our gates and declare that you owe nothing… while openly inviting the test anyway.”

“I’m not here to settle anyone else’s ledger,” Crimson said. “I’m here because I want to know if there’s a version of this blade that doesn’t eventually turn on the people I chose to keep. If your trials can show me that without asking me to cut off heads I’ve already decided to protect — then I’ll play. If not…” He shrugged. “Then I’ll walk back out the same way I walked in. Either way, the line stays where I drew it.”

Vesper studied him.

“The old channels are listening,” she said quietly. “They heard your refusal before you even arrived. And now they have heard this.”

She lowered her hand from the edge of her mask.

“Very well, Crimson Valentine. You will not be asked to finish what your bloodline refused to finish. That debt is no longer yours to carry… if it ever truly was.”

She turned toward the dark archway.

“But understand this — Gallowmere does not test the strength of your blade. It tests the strength of the hand that refuses to swing it when the world demands blood. We will see which one breaks first.”

She began walking without waiting for a reply.

Crimson rolled his shoulders once, the sharp grin still lingering, and followed her into the shadow of the arch.

Behind them, the sigils flared bright crimson for a single heartbeat — then went dark.

As if something very old had just exhaled.

Chapter: The Walk
They passed beneath the broken arch and the world changed.
Not dramatically. Not with thunder or fanfare. The shift was quieter than that. The air grew still in a way that had nothing to do with weather. Sound behaved differently here — footsteps didn’t quite echo the way they should, and voices seemed to arrive a fraction of a second later than they ought to. The stones under their boots were worn smooth in the center of the path, as if centuries of feet had walked this exact line and worn away any chance of deviation.
Crimson noticed the details without meaning to.
A novice in a dark robe knelt beside one of the drainage grates, scrubbing at old blood with a stiff brush and a bucket of grey water. The boy couldn’t have been more than sixteen. He glanced up as they passed, eyes widening for half a second before he dropped his gaze and kept scrubbing. His hands were shaking slightly.
Further ahead, an older woman in a stained apron carried a covered tray across the courtyard. She moved with the careful, deliberate pace of someone who had learned long ago not to draw attention to herself. She didn’t look at Vesper or Crimson at all.
The buildings themselves were older than they had any right to be. Black stone, veined with faint crimson that pulsed irregularly beneath the surface, like something half-asleep. Some of the walls were cracked. Others had been repaired with newer stone that didn’t quite match. It gave the place the look of something that had been broken and rebuilt too many times to count, yet still refused to fall.
Vesper walked a half-step ahead of him. She didn’t speak at first.
Crimson let the silence stretch. He had learned that most people hated silence more than they hated answers. Vesper didn’t seem to mind it.
Eventually she spoke without turning her head.
“You expected something cleaner.”
It wasn’t quite a question.
Crimson glanced at the novice still scrubbing at the grate. “I expected something that didn’t need to hide what it does.”
“We don’t hide it,” Vesper said. “We simply don’t advertise. There’s a difference.”
They turned down a narrower passage between two long buildings. The black rose sigils continued along the walls here, fainter than the ones outside. Some of them had been scratched or defaced over the years. One had been completely covered by newer stone.
“You’ve been here before,” Crimson said.
Vesper’s mask tilted slightly. “In a manner of speaking.”
“Sable.”
“She walks the halls sometimes. In dreamspace. She says the stone feels different under bare feet than it does in the waking world.”
Crimson didn’t respond to that. He kept walking.
They passed an open doorway. Inside, a long hall stretched into shadow. The sound of steel on stone echoed from somewhere deeper within — slow, rhythmic, deliberate. Someone was training. Or being trained.
Vesper stopped at the entrance to another archway. This one was narrower than the last, and the air beyond it felt colder.
She turned to face him fully for the first time since they entered.
“The first test is not combat,” she said. “Not in the way you’re probably expecting. It will not ask you to draw your blade. It will ask you to stand still while something is taken from you. Most who fail this test do so because they cannot bear to lose what they were never truly willing to give.”
Crimson met her masked gaze.
“And if I refuse to give it?”
“Then you will be asked to leave,” Vesper said. “And you will be allowed to go. Gallowmere does not keep those who will not be kept.”
She gestured toward the arch.
“Through there. The room at the end of the passage. You will know it when you see it.”
Crimson didn’t move immediately.
Vesper waited.
After a moment, he spoke.
“You said most who fail do so because they can’t bear to lose what they were never willing to give.” His voice was quiet. “What about the ones who fail because they are willing?”
Vesper was still for a long moment.
Then she answered.
“Those are the ones we watch most closely.”
She turned and walked away without another word, leaving him at the entrance to the passage.
Crimson stood there for a few seconds, listening to the distant sound of steel on stone. Then he rolled his shoulders once, adjusted the letter still resting over his heart, and stepped into the dark.
Chapter: The First Cut
The passage was narrow and cold. The black rose sigils on the walls grew fainter the deeper he walked, until they were little more than faint veins of light beneath the stone. The sound of his boots was the only noise.
At the end of the passage stood a single door. No handle. No lock. Just a slab of dark stone set into the wall, marked with a single symbol — the same black rose with its petals cut away, leaving only stem and thorns.
Crimson stopped in front of it.
For a moment he simply stood there, listening. There was no sound from the other side. No movement. Just a heavy, waiting silence that pressed against his skin like cold water.
He placed his palm against the stone.
The door opened without sound, sliding inward as if it had been waiting for his touch.
The room beyond was circular and low-ceilinged. Black stone walls. No windows. A single obsidian table sat in the center, surrounded by seven empty chairs. The air smelled of old blood, rain, and something sharper — like ozone after a lightning strike.
On the table rested a single object.
A blade.
Not his. Not any blade he recognized. It was simple, almost plain, with a dark metal edge that caught the faint light strangely. Beside it lay a strip of crimson silk, the same shade as the one Sable had left in the lower archives.
Crimson stepped inside.
The door slid shut behind him.
For several long seconds, nothing happened.
Then a voice spoke. Not Vesper’s. Not Sable’s. It came from everywhere and nowhere at once — low, dry, and ancient, like stone remembering how to speak.
“You have been seen.”
Crimson didn’t flinch. He had already heard those words once, written on vellum. Hearing them spoken aloud changed nothing.
The voice continued.
“You carry a refusal. A line you will not cross. We have tasted it. We have weighed it. Now we will see what it costs you to keep.”
On the table, the crimson silk began to move on its own. It rose slowly into the air, unfolding until it hung suspended between Crimson and the obsidian table. 
The fabric drifted closer to him, almost gently.
“You will not be asked to draw this blade. You will be asked to give something to it. A memory. A name. A piece of what you are. Something that matters. Something that hurts to lose.”
The crimson silk hovered between them, waiting.
“If you give nothing, you may leave. The gates will remain open behind you. But the refusal will follow you. And it will grow heavier with every step you take outside these walls.”
The voice paused.
“If you give something… then we will see what remains of you afterward.”
Crimson stared at the floating silk, then at the plain blade resting on the obsidian table.
He didn’t reach for either.
“If you want something from me, you’re going to have to take it. Because I’m not offering.”
The room went very still.
For a long moment, nothing moved.
Then the voice returned — quieter now, but no less present.
“Then we will take, you have already decided what you will not give,” the voice said. “We accept that. But refusal alone is not enough. Weight must still be measured. Something must be offered in its place, or taken.”
The pull sharpened.
Crimson’s jaw tightened. His hands stayed at his sides.
His breath hitched once. Just once.
Then he steadied.
The blade drifted closer to his throat. Not touching. Not yet. Simply present.
“You may stop this,” the voice said. “Offer something willingly. A name. A memory. A piece of what you are. Something that still has weight. The test will end. You will be allowed to leave with what remains.”
Crimson’s voice came out rougher than he intended.
“No.”
The blade inched forward.
“Then we will continue to take.”
Crimson closed his eyes for half a second.
When he opened them again, the sharp-edged grin was gone. What remained was the look of a man who had already decided he wasn’t going to lose.
He spoke into the empty room, voice low and steady.
“I dare you.”
The silk paused.
Crimson looked directly at the hovering blade, eyes hard.
“Monsters far greater than these brittle stones have tried me. And I am still standing.”
The room went very still.
For a long moment, nothing moved.
Crimson didn’t wait for it to decide.
He stepped forward.
One step. Then another. He closed the distance to the floating blade without hesitation, until the dark metal was less than an inch from his throat. The silk brushed against his coat as he moved through it.
“I said..." he paused for effect "I... dare... you...”
His voice was low, but it carried.
The blade wavered.
Crimson leaned in the last inch, pressing his own throat against the edge until a thin line of blood welled up and ran down his skin. He didn’t flinch. He didn’t pull back.
“You want to take something?” he said, eyes locked on the hovering blade.
“You would force the cut.”
“I’m not forcing anything,” Crimson answered. “I’m just not giving you permission to do it gently.”
The blade pressed harder against his throat. Blood ran faster now, soaking into the collar of his coat. Still, he didn’t move.
The room seemed to press in from all sides. The obsidian table groaned. One of the seven chairs cracked down the center with a sound like splitting bone. The air grew thick and metallic.
“You are not supposed to move,” the voice said. There was something almost offended in it now.
Crimson’s mouth curved into something sharp and menacing.
“Yeah? Well I’m moving.”
He took another step forward, forcing the blade to either cut deeper or retreat. It chose to cut. The edge bit in, clean and precise, but Crimson kept walking into it until the silk was forced to move with him or tear.
His voice dropped lower.
“Monsters bigger than this place have tried to hollow me out. They used softer words and prettier blades than this. And I’m still here.”
For several long seconds, the only sound in the room was Crimson’s breathing and the slow drip of blood onto the stone floor.
Then the voice spoke again — quieter, but no longer calm.
“You are… not what we expected.”
Crimson didn’t smile.
He stood there, throat still pressed against the blade, blood running down his neck, and refused to look away.
The blade pressed forward with sudden, deliberate force, driving the edge deeper into Crimson’s throat. Blood ran freely now, soaking the front of his coat. The pain was sharp and clean, but he didn’t pull away. He leaned into it instead, refusing to give the cut the dignity of distance.
The crimson silk wrapped around his chest like a second skin, no longer gentle. It constricted. Not enough to crush — just enough to make every breath feel like it had to be earned.
The voice returned, no longer quiet. It filled the room now, layered and cold.
“You mistake defiance for strength.”
Crimson’s breathing was rough, but his voice stayed steady.
“No,” he said. “I just know the difference between strength and surrender. You want me to stand still while you take pieces of me? Fine. But I’m not going to make it easy for you.”
The silk tightened further.
The blade twisted slightly, widening the wound at his throat. More blood. More pain. Still, he didn’t move back.
“You are bleeding for nothing,” the voice said. “This refusal changes nothing. We will take what we require. We always have.”
Crimson’s grin returned
He took another half-step forward, forcing the blade even deeper.
The voice didn’t answer right away.
When it finally spoke again, it was quieter. Almost thoughtful.
“You are… difficult.”
Crimson laughed once — short, rough, and without any real humor.
“Yeah,” he said. “I get that a lot.”
The silk suddenly flared with light.
The blade stopped advancing, but it didn’t pull back either. It stayed buried in his throat, as if the room was now unsure whether to continue or retreat.
And for the first time since the door had closed, the old channels seemed uncertain.
The blade stayed buried in his throat.
Crimson looked down at it for a long moment, blood still running down his neck and soaking into his coat. Then, slowly, he raised both hands.
He wrapped his fingers around the dark metal.
And he squeezed.
The edge bit deep into his palms instantly. Maroon fire welled up between his fingers and ran down his wrists in thick, hot streams. He didn’t flinch. He didn’t pull away. He just tightened his grip until the blade groaned under the pressure and fresh blood poured from both hands like it was trying to escape.
The silk around his chest convulsed.
The voice in the room actually faltered for half a second.
“You—”
Crimson looked up, eyes hard, hands still locked around the blade as his blood ran freely down his arms.
“I’m not standing still for you,” he said, voice low and rough. “I’m not going to let you carve pieces out of me while I stand still like some fucking offering”
He squeezed harder. More blood. The metal creaked.
“I dare you,” he repeated, quieter this time, but no less intense. “Keep going. See what happens when I don’t make this easy. See what happens when I decide I’m not losing anything else today.”
The obsidian table split completely down the middle with a sound like the world cracking. Two of the remaining chairs shattered. The air grew so thick it was hard to breathe.
He didn’t let go of the blade.
Blood continued to pour from his hands and throat, running down his arms and chest in rivulets. He stood there in the center of the ruined room, gripping the weapon that was cutting him open, and refused to look away.
The old channels had never encountered this before.
Not like this.
Not someone who would rather bleed out holding the knife than stand still and let it take what it wanted.
The test had broken.
And something new was beginning to take its place.
The blade was still buried in his throat when the door opened.
Dame Vesper stepped inside without ceremony. She took one look at the scene — Crimson standing there with both hands locked around the blade, blood pouring from his palms and throat, the obsidian table split in half, and the room itself vibrating with tension — and her posture changed.
She barked a single word.
“Enough.”
The effect was immediate.
The blade jerked back several inches, pulling out of Crimson’s throat with a wet sound. The crimson silk recoiled like it had been burned. The pressure in the room dropped so fast it left a ringing silence behind.
Crimson’s knees buckled.
He dropped hard onto the stone floor, one hand still half-raised like he was going to grab the blade again out of pure stubbornness. Blood ran freely down his neck and chest. His breathing was rough and heavy, shoulders rising and falling as he stayed on his knees, staring at the floor.
Vesper didn’t move toward him immediately.
She looked at the hovering blade, then at the ruined table, then back at Crimson. When she finally spoke, her voice was dry and sharp.
“You were told the first test required you to stand still while something was taken,” she said. “Instead, you decided to turn it into a contest of who could bleed more dramatically.”
Crimson let out a short, rough laugh that turned into a cough. Blood flecked his lips.
“Wasn’t… gonna stand there and let it carve me up like a fucking offering,” he rasped.
Vesper stepped closer. She didn’t help him up. She simply stood over him, mask tilted down as she regarded the mess he’d made of himself and the room.
“You broke the premise of the test,” she said. “Both of you.” The last two words were directed at the air itself — at the old channels still lingering in the stones. “This was meant to measure weight. Instead it became a pissing contest between a stubborn man and a room full of ancient grudges.”
She exhaled through her nose, the sound faintly exasperated even through the mask.
“Get up,” she said. “You’ve made your point. The channels have made theirs. This particular game is over.”
Crimson stayed on his knees for another few seconds, breathing hard, blood still dripping from his hands and throat onto the cracked stone. Then, slowly, he pushed himself upright. He swayed once but stayed standing.
Vesper watched him without offering a hand.
“You’re bleeding all over my floor,” she said flatly.
Crimson wiped his mouth with the back of one ruined hand and gave her a bloody, tired grin.
“Get one of your bucket boys to scrub it” he spat, flecks splattering on the ground with rueful distain for where it landed.

Vesper was quiet for a moment.
Then she spoke again, voice lower.
“The first test is done. You didn’t pass it cleanly. You didn’t fail it either. You… broke it. That hasn’t happened in a very long time.”
She turned toward the ruined doorway.
“Come. There’s no point keeping you in here while you bleed out. We’ll get you patched up. And then…” She glanced back at him. “Then we’ll see what the second test looks like when the room no longer assumes you’ll stand still and let it take what it wants.”
She started walking.
Crimson stayed where he was for a second longer, staring at the broken obsidian table and the blade still hovering in the air like it wasn’t sure what to do with itself anymore.
Then he followed.

Interlude: The Bucket Boy
The novice’s name was Elias.
He was seventeen, had been at Gallowmere for four months, and still jumped every time one of the senior students looked at him too long. Right now he was on his knees beside the eastern drainage grate, scrubbing at old blood with a stiff-bristled brush and a bucket of increasingly pink water. His hands were raw. They always were these days.
He heard the shouting before he saw anything.
Not loud shouting. Not the kind that echoed through the courtyards during training. This was deeper. Heavier. It came from somewhere below the stones, like the building itself was arguing with someone.
Elias paused, brush halfway through a stubborn smear, and glanced toward the narrow passage that led to the lower testing chambers. He wasn’t supposed to look. Initiates weren’t supposed to pay attention to what happened behind those doors. Most of them learned that lesson quickly.
He was still learning.
A second-year student hurried past him without slowing down. Her face was pale.
“Something’s wrong,” she muttered, more to herself than to him. “The channels are… loud.”
Elias swallowed and went back to scrubbing, but his ears stayed pricked.
He’d only been here four months, but even he had heard the stories. About the man who walked through the gates today. About how Dame Vesper herself had gone to meet him. About how the black rose sigils along the main path had flared bright enough to be seen from the upper cloisters.
No one was supposed to talk about it.
Everyone was talking about it anyway.
Another initiate — a girl even younger than him — crept up beside the grate and crouched down like she was helping with the blood, even though her bucket was empty.
“Did you hear?” she whispered. “They say he grabbed the blade. With his hands.”
Elias didn’t look up. “We’re not supposed to talk about it.”
“I know, but—” She glanced toward the passage again. “They say the room broke. Like actually broke. One of the third-years said she felt it in her teeth.”
Elias kept scrubbing. His hands were shaking a little now.
He’d seen the man earlier, when he first walked through the gates. Just for a second. Red hair. Sharp eyes. Covered in old scars and walking like the stones owed him something. Elias had felt something strange in his chest when he looked at him — not fear exactly. Something closer to recognition.
Like watching someone do the thing you were always too scared to do.
A low, grinding sound rolled through the stones beneath them. Elias felt it in his knees. The girl beside him went very still.
Then the sound stopped.
Everything went quiet.
For a long moment, neither of them moved.
Then the second-year student who had rushed past earlier came back the other way, slower this time. She looked shaken.
“He’s still alive,” she said, voice low. “Dame Vesper pulled him out. He was… he was smiling.”
Elias finally looked up.
The girl next to him whispered, almost reverent:
“…Who is he?”
The second-year didn’t answer right away. She just stared down the passage like she was trying to see through the stone.
Then she said, very quietly:
“Someone who doesn’t know how to lose.”
She walked away without another word.
Elias stayed where he was, hands still wrapped around the brush, water pink around his knees. He didn’t know why, but his chest felt tight.
Somewhere deep beneath the Academy, something had changed.
And for the first time since he’d arrived at Gallowmere, Elias wasn’t sure if that was a good thing.
Chapter: The Grate
Elias had been scrubbing the same patch of stone for nearly an hour.
The blood was long gone, but he kept going anyway. The stiff bristles rasped against the old stone in steady, mindless strokes. Around him, the eastern courtyard was quieter than usual. Initiates still moved, still worked, still carried buckets and brushes and trays like they were supposed to. But something in the rhythm was off.
No one was shouting corrections. No senior students were barking at them to move faster or scrub harder. The usual low-level tension that normally hung over morning duties had been replaced by something thinner. More brittle.
Elias glanced up once.
A second-year was standing near the archway that led toward the lower halls. She kept looking toward the passage like she expected something to come out of it. When she noticed Elias watching, she turned away too quickly.
He went back to scrubbing.
The rumors had already started.
He’d heard them while fetching fresh water. Fragments, passed between initiates when the seniors weren’t close enough to hear.
“He grabbed it. With his hands.”
“Vesper went in. She never goes in.”
“They say the room broke. Like actually broke.”
“Is he dead?”
“No one’s saying he’s dead.”
“No one’s saying he’s alive either.”
Elias didn’t join the conversations. He wasn’t high enough in the hierarchy for anyone to include him unless they wanted something. Most of the time that suited him fine. Today it left him feeling like he was standing just outside a door he wasn’t allowed to open.
A sharp sound cut through the courtyard.
Elias looked up in time to see one of the senior initiates — a tall girl named Mira who usually carried herself like the stones should be grateful she walked on them — standing very still while an instructor spoke to her in a low voice. Mira’s face had gone pale. Her hands were clenched at her sides.
The instructor wasn’t raising their voice. That was the strange part. Usually when someone needed correcting, it was loud enough for everyone nearby to hear and learn from it. This was quiet. Controlled. Almost careful.
Mira nodded once, stiffly, then turned and walked away toward the western buildings without looking at anyone. Her steps were too even. Like she was concentrating on not running.
Elias watched her go until she disappeared through a doorway.
Something was wrong.
He didn’t know what, exactly. But the feeling had been growing since the low grinding sound had rolled through the stones earlier. The one that had made his knees vibrate and the girl next to him go quiet.
Now the black rose sigils along the eastern wall were flickering.
Not dramatically. Just… inconsistently. Some of them pulsed a little too bright. Others dimmed for half a second longer than they should have. Elias had been here four months. He knew how the sigils were supposed to behave. They were steady. Reliable. Like a heartbeat you didn’t have to think about.
These weren’t.
He was still staring at them when a voice spoke behind him.
“Elias.”
He turned quickly, nearly dropping the brush.
It was Archivist Lira. Mid-level. Not someone who usually spoke to initiates unless they had done something wrong. She was holding a small slate and looking at him with an expression that was trying very hard to be neutral.
“What did you see near the eastern drain this morning?” she asked.
Elias blinked. “Ma’am?”
“Before the… disturbance.” Lira’s eyes flicked toward the passage. “Did you see anyone enter or leave the lower halls? Anyone unusual?”
He thought about lying. Then decided against it.
“I saw the man,” he said quietly. “When he first came through the gates. Red hair. Scars.”
Lira wrote something on the slate. “Did he speak to anyone?”
“No, ma’am.”
“Did you hear anything after he went below?”
Elias hesitated. “There was a sound. Like stone grinding. And then it got quiet.”
Lira studied him for a moment longer, then nodded once.
“Return to your duties,” she said. “And if anyone asks you what you saw or heard, you tell them to speak to an instructor. Understood?”
“Yes, ma’am.”
She walked away without another word.
Elias stayed where he was for a while after she left, brush hanging loosely in his hand. Around him, the courtyard continued its half-hearted imitation of normal routine. Initiates scrubbed. Trays were carried. Someone laughed once, then stopped abruptly like they’d remembered they weren’t supposed to.
He looked down at the drainage grate.
The blood was gone. He’d scrubbed it clean hours ago.
But when he tilted his head just right, he could still see a faint, scorched mark in the shape of one of the black rose sigils burned into the stone beside the grate. It hadn’t been there yesterday. He was sure of it.
Elias reached out and touched it with two fingers.
The mark was warm.
He pulled his hand back.
For a long moment he just knelt there, staring at the faint burn on the stone while the rest of Gallowmere tried to pretend the day was like any other. Somewhere below his feet, something had happened that no one was supposed to talk about. Something that had made senior students go pale and instructors speak in careful, quiet voices.
And Elias, who was low enough in the order that no one had bothered to tell him what to believe yet, was starting to understand something simple and uncomfortable.
Gallowmere was pretending everything was fine.
But the stones didn’t believe it.
He picked up the brush again and went back to scrubbing a grate that didn’t need it, because that was what he was supposed to do. Around him, the sigils continued their uneven flickering. Somewhere in the upper levels, Dame Vesper was moving through sealed corridors with purpose.
And far below, in a broken room that no initiate was allowed to enter, something was still deciding what it thought of the man who had refused to stand still.
Elias didn’t know any of that for certain.
But he could feel it.
And for the first time since he’d arrived at Gallowmere Abbey Academy, he wasn’t sure if he wanted to know more.
Chapter: The Hand That Refused
Vesper didn’t speak again until they were two levels above the broken chamber.
She led him through narrower corridors than the ones they had taken on the way down. These felt older. Less maintained. The black rose sigils here were fainter, and some had been deliberately covered with newer stone. Crimson noticed that the instructors and senior students they passed didn’t look at him directly. They looked around him, like acknowledging his presence might make something real that they weren’t ready to deal with yet.
His hands were still bleeding. The cuts across his palms were deep enough that every step sent fresh pain up his arms. The wound at his throat had slowed to a sluggish seep, but it throbbed in time with his heartbeat. The dragon tattoo along the side of his neck felt hot, like it was trying to do something it wasn’t built for.
Vesper finally stopped in front of a plain wooden door set into an otherwise unmarked wall. She opened it without ceremony and gestured for him to enter.
The room inside was small and functional. A cot. A basin of water that still had steam rising from it. Clean cloth. A small table with a sealed jar of something dark green. No sigils on the walls. No ancient weight pressing down from the stones. Just a room where someone came to stop bleeding.
“Sit,” Vesper said.
Crimson dropped onto the edge of the cot without argument. His legs felt less steady than he wanted to admit.
Vesper didn’t help him with the bandages. She stood near the door with her arms crossed, watching as he dipped a cloth in the basin and started cleaning the worst of the blood from his hands. The water turned dark red almost immediately.
For a while, neither of them spoke.
Then Vesper said, voice flat:
“You broke the test.”
Crimson didn’t look up from his hands.
“Yeah,” he said. “I noticed.”
“That hasn’t happened in thirty-one years.”
He glanced at her then. The mask was still in place, but something in her posture had shifted. She wasn’t performing authority right now. She was thinking.
“The old channels don’t like being refused,” she continued. “They especially don’t like being defied. What you did in that room wasn’t just stubbornness. It was something they haven’t accounted for in a very long time.”
Crimson wrapped a strip of clean cloth around his left palm, pulling it tight with his teeth. The pain helped clear some of the fog in his head.
“So what now?” he asked. “You throw me out? Lock me up? Try again with something sharper?”
Vesper was quiet for a moment.
“No,” she said eventually. “The channels are… recalibrating. They expected a certain shape of refusal. You gave them something else. Something messier. They’re not sure what to do with it yet.”
She pushed off the doorframe and stepped further into the room.
“The second test was already prepared. But after what just happened, it no longer makes sense to run it the way it was designed. The room won’t assume you’ll stand still anymore. And I won’t assume the channels will behave the way they’re supposed to.”
Crimson flexed his bandaged hand, watching fresh blood seep through the cloth.
“So you’re winging it,” he said.
Vesper’s head tilted slightly.
“I am adapting,” she corrected. “There is a difference.”
She studied him for another few seconds.
“You’re still bleeding through the bandages.”
“Yeah, well.” Crimson gave her a tired, bloody smirk. “Your floor’s already fucked. Might as well commit.”
Vesper didn’t laugh. But something in the set of her shoulders eased by a fraction.
“Rest here for now,” she said. “Eat if you can. Someone will bring water and something for the pain. When you’re steady enough to stand without swaying, I’ll come get you.”
She turned to leave, then paused at the door.
“Crimson.”
He looked up.
“Next time,” she said, “if you decide to break something… try to make sure it’s something we can afford to lose.”
Then she was gone, the door closing behind her with a soft click.
Crimson sat alone in the small room, hands throbbing, throat burning, and stared at the blood still slowly soaking through the fresh bandages.
He let out a low breath that might have been a laugh.
“Get in line,” he muttered to the empty room.
Outside, somewhere deeper in the Academy, the black rose sigils continued their uneven flickering.
And far below, in a chamber that had been sealed after they left, something ancient and hungry was still trying to decide what it had just learned about the man who refused to stand still.

Chapter: The Spire
Sable stood on the highest balcony of the old cathedral spire, wind tugging at the edges of her coat.
She had been here since before dawn.
The city stretched out below her in layers of neon and shadow, the usual chaos of movement and light reduced to something distant and almost peaceful from this height. She liked it up here. The wind was cleaner. The noise didn’t reach as far. And when she needed to feel the shape of something happening elsewhere, this was one of the places that made it easier.
She had felt it when he broke the test.
Not clearly. Not like a vision or a message. Just a sudden, sharp tug in the threads she had learned to notice — the ones that always tightened when Crimson made a choice that cost him something. It had been strong enough to pull her out of whatever half-dream she had been drifting through and bring her to the edge of the balcony, hands braced on the old stone railing.
He was bleeding.
She could feel that much. Not the details. Not the exact shape of the wounds. But the weight of it. The way pain and defiance had mixed together into something sharp enough to cut through the distance between them.
Sable closed her eyes and let the wind move over her face.
She had known this would be hard. She had known Gallowmere wouldn’t treat him gently. That was the point. He needed something that wouldn’t flinch when he pushed back. Something that could meet his refusal without trying to soften it or break it.
But knowing it in theory and feeling it happen were two different things.
Her fingers tightened on the railing.
She could still remember the way the old channels had tasted when she offered them the crimson silk. The hunger. The recognition. The way they had responded to the Valentine bloodline like something they had been waiting for. She had known they would test him. She had known they would try to take something.
What she hadn’t expected was for him to reach back.
Sable exhaled slowly.
Somewhere far below and to the north, in a room she had never seen, Crimson was sitting alone with his hands wrapped in bloody cloth and a wound at his throat that had come far too close to something vital. She could feel the shape of his exhaustion. The stubborn, bloody-minded refusal to let it show too much. The way he was already bracing for whatever came next instead of letting himself rest.
She hated that she had put him there.
And she didn’t regret it.
Both things were true at the same time, and she had long since stopped trying to make them contradict each other.
A soft sound behind her made her turn.
One of the old channels had followed her up here. Not in any physical form. Just a shift in the air, a faint pressure against the edges of her awareness. It didn’t speak. It rarely did when she came to this place. But she could feel the question in it anyway.
He broke it.
Sable’s mouth curved, small and sharp.
“Yeah,” she said quietly. “He did.”
The presence lingered for a moment longer, then faded.
She turned back to the railing and looked out over the city again. Somewhere out there, in the layers of light and movement, people were living their lives without any idea that an old, half-forgotten institution had just been forced to reconsider what it thought it knew about refusal.
She wondered if Crimson was still bleeding.
She wondered if he was alone.
And beneath all of it, quieter but no less present, was the thought she kept coming back to no matter how many times she tried to set it aside:
He’s still standing.
That was the part that scared her the most.
Because she knew what Gallowmere did to people who refused to break the way they were supposed to. And she knew what it did to the people who cared about them.
Sable stayed on the balcony for a long time after that, wind moving through her hair, eyes on the distant northern horizon where she could still feel the faint, stubborn shape of him.
She didn’t pray.
She didn’t send any messages.
She just stood there, one hand resting over the place where the Reciprocity Debt lived between them, and waited to see what he would do next.
Because that was the deal they had made.
He carried the weight.
She stayed close enough to feel it.
And neither of them looked away.
