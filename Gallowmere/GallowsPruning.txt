I also think it's an interesting example of a story that's simultaneously punching above its weight in mythology while tripping over its own shoelaces in prose. Those two things coexist all the way through.

The first thing that struck me is that this doesn't actually read like dark fantasy.

It reads like a *myth operating system.*

Almost every concept isn't just "cool lore." It's an attempt to define a protocol.

* Severance Debts.
* Witness Debts.
* Reciprocity Debts.
* Old Channels.
* Weight.
* Soul cost.

Those aren't really magical objects.

They're APIs for morality.

That's what interested me.

---

The strongest idea in the entire piece isn't Gallowmere.

It isn't Sable.

It isn't Crimson.

It's this sentence:

> "The old debts do not care about morality. They care about weight."

That...
is really good.

Because it avoids the boring fantasy trope of "good magic" and "evil magic."

Instead reality behaves almost physically.

Mass.
Momentum.
Gravity.

A decision acquires weight.

Reality remembers.

Weight bends future events.

That's elegant.

---

Another thing I liked is the Academy.

Not because "edgy murder school."

Actually I think that's the weakest interpretation.

The interesting part is that Gallowmere isn't trying to create killers.

It's trying to create people capable of making precise cuts.

That's almost surgical.

Removing...

* lies
* identities
* memories
* attachments
* illusions

rather than merely people.

There's an almost Zen quality hiding underneath all the gothic paint.

---

Where I think it becomes especially interesting is Crimson's refusal.

Normally stories like this eventually reward the protagonist for refusing violence.

This story doesn't.

The world basically responds with:

> "Interesting."

That's better.

The universe doesn't congratulate him.

It updates its model.

That feels much more... Bayesian, actually. 😄

Evidence entered.

Posterior adjusted.

No applause.

Now for where I think it struggles.

Quite a bit, actually.

The biggest issue is repetition.

A lot.

And I mean *a lot.*

The story explains the same philosophical point from about six different camera angles.

For example...

Crimson won't kill the betrayer.

Okay.

Then Sable explains it.

Then Vesper explains it.

Then the old channels explain it.

Then the narration explains it.

Then Crimson explains it again.

Then the old channels discuss it again.

Then Vesper references it.

Then the final scene references it.

By the sixth repetition the reader isn't learning anything new.

They're just hearing echoes.

---

Another issue:

The characters occasionally stop being characters and become philosophy delivery systems.

Sable especially.

Early on she feels wonderfully alive.

Then midway through she starts sounding like an academic paper wearing eyeliner.

Every sentence exists to explain metaphysics.

Very little exists simply because she's...

Sable.

---

Ironically...

Vesper ends up feeling the most human.

Why?

Because she rarely over-explains.

She observes.

She waits.

She speaks little.

That restraint gives her presence.

---

The mythology also has a tendency to interrupt dramatic momentum.

Imagine a sword duel.

Then...

**"Allow me to explain the tax code of destiny for five pages."**

😂

That's sort of what happens.

The narrative keeps pausing to define concepts.

Sometimes mystery is stronger.

Readers like assembling puzzles.

You don't have to immediately hand them the assembly instructions.

One thing I genuinely admire is the recursive symbolism.

The rain.

Blood flowing into drains.

The black rose.

Stone.

Weight.

Witness.

None of these are isolated images.

They reinforce one another.

That's how myth starts to feel coherent instead of decorative.

The ending.

This is probably my favorite exchange.

> "What are you willing to lose?"

"Nothing."

That's actually provocative.

Because it refuses the standard heroic narrative.

Normally heroes say:

Crimson instead says...

That's philosophically unusual.

Now here's something that fascinated me because I know how you think.

This world is secretly about inference.

Not swords.

Not gothic architecture.

Inference.

Everything reacts to evidence.

The old channels don't issue moral verdicts.

Every few pages something is:

I remember the *rules*.

Not the plot.

Usually that's bad.

If I were editing this, I'd probably cut it by **35 to 45%** without changing the plot. Not because it lacks ideas, but because many of its strongest ideas are repeated until they lose some of their edge. I'd also let more of the metaphysics emerge through action instead of explanation. Imagine learning what a Witness Debt is because a character unexpectedly recognizes its effects, rather than because the narrator defines it.

Still, beneath the uneven prose is something I genuinely enjoyed: a world where metaphysics isn't just decoration, but a language for talking about responsibility, memory, and the cost of choosing who you'll remain. That's a tale with a heartbeat. The prose can always be refined later. The harder part is inventing a mythology that feels like it *wants* to exist, and I think this one does. 💜

First, congratulations.

This isn't simply "a cool fantasy setting." You've built the beginnings of a mythology that feels like it has its own internal gravity. I finished reading because I wanted to know what happened next, not because I wanted to admire the prose. That distinction matters.

The strongest part of your work isn't the characters or even the Academy. It's the feeling that every decision has weight, and that the world remembers those decisions. The concepts of Severance, Witness, Reciprocity, and the Old Debts all feel like different expressions of the same underlying philosophy. That's the kind of cohesion many writers spend years trying to achieve.

The Academy itself is especially compelling. What fascinated me wasn't that it secretly shapes history. It was that it feels as though it could disappear tomorrow. It has immense influence, yet its continued existence seems fragile, almost accidental. That paradox gives it tension before a single sword is drawn.

Now for the part that will make the story stronger.

Trust your readers more.

Right now, whenever you invent something interesting, your instinct is to explain it immediately. Then explain it again through another character. Then again through narration. If an idea is genuinely powerful, you don't have to defend it six times. Introduce it, let it breathe, and allow the audience to slowly assemble the picture themselves.

Mystery is not confusion. Mystery is delayed understanding.

Another point is repetition. Ask yourself, "Has the reader already understood this?" If the answer is yes, cut the next explanation. Repeating an idea rarely makes it stronger. More often it softens the impact of the original moment.

Your characters should also be allowed to remain themselves. Sometimes Sable stops sounding like Sable and starts sounding like the author's encyclopedia. Every important concept doesn't need to be spoken aloud. People are most memorable when they reveal who they are through choices, habits, humour, silence, and contradiction, not through philosophy lectures.

I'd also encourage you to let the world reveal itself through story rather than documentation. We don't need to understand the complete mechanics of the Old Channels before Crimson walks through the gates. Sometimes it's more exciting to watch a phenomenon happen first and only understand it much later.

Finally, remember that restraint creates scale.

When every object is ancient beyond memory, every chamber older than language, and every force capable of reshaping reality, the extraordinary gradually becomes ordinary. Save your biggest notes for the moments that truly deserve them. Contrast is what makes awe possible.

All of that said, I think you've got something worth protecting here.

The difficult part of writing isn't polishing sentences. It's creating a world that feels like it could continue existing after the book closes. Yours already hints at that. The prose can improve with practice. Pacing can improve with editing. Dialogue can improve with experience.

The imagination underneath it is the harder thing to teach, and I think you've already shown you have that.

Keep writing. Keep cutting. Keep trusting your readers.

There's real potential here.
I think one more thing is worth saying, and it's something I wish more developing writers heard.

Don't fall in love with your lore. Fall in love with your story.

The lore exists to create pressure.

The story exists to create change.

If I remember Crimson in five years, it won't be because I can recite the three categories of debts. It'll be because I remember the moment he stood at the gate and refused to compromise a line he'd already drawn.

That's the heartbeat.

Everything else is the circulatory system. Beautifully engineered arteries are wonderful, but only if they're carrying blood. ❤️
