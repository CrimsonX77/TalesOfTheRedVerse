# THE WORLD DOMINATION AGENCY LTD™
## Episode Three: "The Getaway"

---

### COLD OPEN

They came in holding hands.

That was the first thing Lyra noticed — not their faces, not their clothes, not the way they glanced over their shoulders like the door might bite them. Just the hands. Fingers intertwined so tightly the knuckles had gone white. The kind of grip that said *if I let go, I'll drown.*

The girl was maybe twenty-two. Pretty in a polished way — good bones, good skin, the kind of quiet confidence that came from never having to worry about rent. But her eyes were red-rimmed, and there was a bruise on her wrist she kept tugging her sleeve over.

The boy was the same age but looked older. Tired in a way that went deeper than sleep. He had the build of someone who worked with his hands and the posture of someone who'd learned early that taking up space was dangerous.

"We need to disappear," the girl said.

Max didn't even blink.

"Completely? Or just mostly?"

They looked at each other. Some silent conversation passed between them.

"Completely," the boy said. "New names. New city. New everything."

"And what are you disappearing from?"

The girl's jaw tightened. "My family."

Lyra watched Max's face. Watched the way he studied them — not judging, just... *seeing*. Taking in the held hands and the hidden bruise and the bone-deep exhaustion of two people who had been fighting a war no one else could see.

"Names?" he asked.

"I'm David," the boy said. "This is—"

"Lin," the girl cut in. "Just Lin."

Max nodded slowly. "The Mei family. The restaurant dynasty." It wasn't a question.

Lin flinched. David's grip on her hand tightened.

"They'll never stop looking," Lin said quietly. "My father... he doesn't lose. He doesn't *allow* losing. And David is—"

"Not good enough," David finished. No bitterness. Just fact. "Wrong neighborhood. Wrong background. Wrong everything."

"I wasn't going to say that."

"You didn't have to." He looked at her with something so tender it made Lyra's chest ache. "It's okay. I know what I am."

"You're *everything*," Lin said fiercely. "You're the only thing that's ever been mine. The only choice I ever made for myself."

The room went quiet.

Max stood up. Walked to the window. Stood there for a long moment, looking out at nothing.

"I can make you disappear," he said finally. "New identities. Clean paperwork. A city far enough away that your father's reach won't find you for years, if ever."

Lin's breath caught. "You can do that?"

"I can do that." He turned back to face them. "But it will take three days."

"Three days?" David frowned. "We don't have three days. Her father's already looking. If we stay—"

"You'll stay somewhere safe. I have places." Max's voice was calm. Certain. "Three days, and then you'll be ghosts. I promise."

Lin looked at David. David looked at Lin.

"Why three days?" Lyra asked, before she could stop herself.

Max glanced at her. Something flickered in his expression — not annoyance, just... consideration. Like he was deciding how much to say.

"Because disappearing should cost something," he said quietly. "Not money. Memory. They should know what they're leaving behind. They should feel the weight of it."

He looked back at the couple.

"Three days," he repeated. "Trust me."

They did.

---

### ACT ONE: DAY ONE — THE SAFEHOUSE

The safehouse was a third-floor apartment above a laundromat in a part of the neighborhood Lyra hadn't explored yet. Clean, sparse, anonymous. The kind of place that existed specifically to be forgotten.

"You'll stay here," Max said, handing Lin a key. "Don't answer the door for anyone except me or Lyra. Don't use your phones. Don't contact anyone from your old life."

"For three days," David said.

"For three days."

Lin was looking around the apartment with an expression Lyra couldn't quite read. Relief, maybe. Or grief. Or both.

"It's really happening," she said softly. "We're really doing this."

David put his arm around her. "We're really doing this."

Max caught Lyra's eye and tilted his head toward the door. She followed him out.

In the hallway, she finally asked: "Why three days? You could have them gone by tonight. I've seen your network."

"Yes."

"So why wait?"

Max started down the stairs. "Do you know what the hardest part of disappearing is?"

"The logistics?"

"The opposite." He pushed open the door to the street, squinting against the afternoon light. "The logistics are easy. Money, papers, transportation — those are just problems with solutions. The hard part is the *leaving*. The knowing you'll never come back."

They walked in silence for a moment. The neighborhood hummed around them — street vendors, kids on bikes, the distant clatter of the elevated train.

"Most people who want to disappear haven't really thought it through," Max continued. "They're running *from* something, not *to* something. They think vanishing will solve their problems, but they haven't reckoned with what they'll lose."

"And you make them reckon with it?"

"I make them *choose* it. Properly. With full knowledge of the cost." He stopped at a crosswalk, watching the light. "Lin and David aren't running from something bad to find something good. They're giving up everything they've ever known for each other. That's not escape. That's sacrifice."

The light changed. They crossed.

"Three days," Max said. "Three days to say goodbye to a life. To feel it. To grieve it." He glanced at her. "Then, when they go, they'll actually be *going*. Not just running."

Lyra thought about this. About the way Lin had looked around the safehouse. About the white-knuckled grip of two people who had already decided to drown together if they had to.

"That's..." She searched for the word. "...kind. Unexpectedly kind."

Max's mouth quirked. "I'm a complex individual."

"You're something."

"That's what my therapist used to say. Before I made her disappear."

Lyra stopped walking.

Max stopped too, turning back with raised eyebrows.

"That was a joke," he said.

"Was it?"

"...mostly."

She stared at him.

"She retired to Vermont," he admitted. "I send her a card every Christmas. She sends me passive-aggressive articles about healthy coping mechanisms." He started walking again. "Come on. We have work to do."

---

### ACT ONE CONTINUED: THE FIRST GOODBYE

The first stop was a tiny bakery wedged between a hardware store and a psychic's parlor.

"Mrs. Volkov," Max said to the woman behind the counter — round, warm, dusted with flour. "I need a favor."

"Maximilian." Her accent was thick, her smile wider. "You never visit just to visit anymore. Always favors."

"I visit!"

"You visit when you want piroshki. That is not visiting. That is *eating*."

Max clutched his chest. "You wound me."

"Good. You need wounding. Keeps you humble." But she was already pulling a tray from the display case. "What do you need?"

"A cake. Something special. For two young people who are going away."

Mrs. Volkov's eyes sharpened. "Going away how?"

"The permanent kind."

A long pause. Something passed between them — some understanding Lyra wasn't privy to.

"When do you need it?"

"Day after tomorrow. Evening."

"I'll make the honey cake. My grandmother's recipe." She paused. "They are good people? These young ones?"

"They're in love," Max said simply.

Mrs. Volkov nodded slowly. "Then they will have the best cake I have ever made."

---

The second stop was the café with the peacock mural.

Dmitri looked up from the espresso machine, face brightening. "Max! And Lyra! Together! This is becoming a habit. I approve of habits."

"I need a favor," Max said.

"Obviously. No one visits me for my personality."

"I visit you for your personality constantly."

"You visit me for the croissants."

"The croissants are an extension of your personality. They contain multitudes."

Dmitri snorted. "What do you need?"

"A dinner. Something special. Day after tomorrow, evening, for two."

"Romantic dinner?"

"The most romantic dinner you've ever made."

Dmitri's eyebrows rose. "That's a high bar. I once made a seven-course meal for a woman who was proposing to her girlfriend. There were edible flowers shaped like their cats."

"More romantic than that."

"Impossible." But Dmitri was already reaching for a notepad. "Tell me about them."

---

The third stop was an apartment above a dry cleaner, where an elderly woman named Mrs. Okafor served them tea that tasted like smoke and starlight and listened to Max explain the situation with an expression that gave away nothing.

When he finished, she was quiet for a long moment.

"You want me to bless them," she said finally.

"If you're willing."

"Blessings are not small things, Maximilian. They have weight. Cost."

"I know."

Mrs. Okafor's eyes — dark, ancient, seeing — moved to Lyra. "And this one? She is part of it?"

"She's part of everything now," Max said, and something in the way he said it made Lyra's breath catch.

Mrs. Okafor studied her for a long moment. Then, slowly, she smiled.

"Good. You need someone to witness. Someone to remember what you do and why." She turned back to Max. "Bring them to me on the last night. Before they go. I will give them what protection I can."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." Her smile turned sharp. "Blessings have weight. So do debts. You will owe me, Maximilian."

"I already owe you."

"Yes. And now you will owe me more." She sipped her tea. "That is how it works."

---

### ACT TWO: DAY TWO — THE WEIGHT OF THINGS

Lyra spent the second day learning the shape of Lin and David's life.

Max sent her to gather things — not for the disappearance, but for the goodbye. A photograph from the wall of David's mother's apartment (he'd been estranged, but he wanted to remember her face). A pressed flower from the garden where Lin and David had first kissed. A mixtape (an actual tape — David was sentimental) that Lin had made for him in high school, before everything got complicated.

Each item meant a conversation. Each conversation meant a story.

David's mother had kicked him out at seventeen for reasons he didn't explain, but the photograph showed them together when he was young — her arm around him, both of them smiling at something outside the frame. "She wasn't always like that," he said when Lyra brought it to the safehouse. "She used to sing."

The pressed flower was a jasmine blossom, brown and fragile, preserved between the pages of a book Lin had "borrowed" from her father's study. "He never read it anyway," she said, almost defiant. "He never reads anything. He just owns things."

The mixtape was held together with tape and hope. When David saw it, he laughed — the first real laugh Lyra had heard from him. "I can't believe you kept this. The songs are terrible."

"The songs are *perfect*," Lin said. "You made it for me. That makes it perfect."

Lyra watched them — the small touches, the private jokes, the way they orbited each other like binary stars — and thought about what Max had said.

*They should feel the weight of it.*

They were feeling it. Every remembered moment was another stone on the scale, another reason to grieve, another reason to stay.

And they were choosing to go anyway.

---

That evening, back at the office, Lyra found Max staring out the window.

Not the usual staring — the kind where he seemed to be seeing something far away. This was different. Heavier. He looked... tired. The kind of tired that sleep couldn't fix.

"Hey," she said.

He didn't turn around. "Hey."

She should have left it. Should have given him space, asked if he wanted coffee, pretended she hadn't noticed.

Instead, she crossed the room and stood beside him.

The window looked out over the alley — not much of a view. Fire escapes and brick walls and the narrow slice of sky between buildings. But Max was watching it like it held answers.

"You okay?" she asked.

"Define 'okay.'"

"You've been doing that a lot lately. Deflecting with definitions."

His mouth quirked. Just slightly. "Have I?"

"You have."

Silence. The sound of the city filtered up from below — traffic, voices, the distant wail of a siren.

"I was in love once," Max said quietly.

Lyra went still.

"A long time ago. Different city. Different life." He was still looking out the window. "She wanted us to disappear. Run away together. Leave everything behind."

"What happened?"

"I said no." His voice was flat. Careful. "I had responsibilities. Obligations. A *mission*." The last word came out bitter. "I thought I was choosing duty. I thought there would be time later."

"And?"

"There wasn't." He finally turned to look at her. His eyes were old. So old. "There's never time later, Lyra. That's the lie we tell ourselves so we don't have to be brave today."

She didn't know what to say. Didn't know if there was anything to say.

So she did something unprofessional.

She put her hand on his shoulder.

Just rested it there. Light. Warm. *Present.*

Max went very still.

For a long moment, neither of them moved.

Then, slowly, some of the tension drained out of him. Not all of it. But enough.

"Thank you," he said softly.

"For what?"

"For not asking follow-up questions."

"I have about fifty."

"I know." Almost a smile. "Thank you for not asking them."

She left her hand there for another moment. Then, because the silence was getting heavy in a different way, she stepped back.

"Coffee?" she asked.

"Please."

She went to make coffee.

Behind her, Max turned back to the window.

But something had shifted. Something small. Something that felt like the first crack in a door that had been locked for a very long time.

---

### ACT THREE: DAY THREE — THE SEND-OFF

The final evening was orchestrated like a symphony.

Dmitri's dinner came first — seven courses of impossible beauty, served in a private room above the café that Lyra hadn't known existed. Candles everywhere. Leonard the parrot banished to the front room ("He kept critiquing the presentation," Dmitri explained. "No one needs that kind of negativity during a tasting menu.")

Lin cried during the third course. David held her hand and didn't let go.

Mrs. Volkov's honey cake came next — delivered personally, still warm, with a note in Ukrainian that made Max's eyes go soft when he read it.

"What does it say?" Lyra asked.

"'May your sweetness outlast your sorrow,'" Max translated. "It's what her grandmother used to say. At weddings. And funerals."

"Those seem like very different occasions."

"Not to Mrs. Volkov."

---

The blessing happened at midnight.

Mrs. Okafor's apartment was lit only by candles — dozens of them, arranged in patterns that made Lyra's eyes water if she looked too long. The smoke from the tea had thickened, filling the room with something that smelled like memory.

Lin and David knelt in the center of the room, still holding hands.

Mrs. Okafor circled them slowly, speaking words in a language Lyra didn't recognize. The candlelight flickered. The air grew heavy.

And then, just for a moment, Lyra could have sworn she saw something — threads, maybe, or light, or something else entirely — wrapping around the couple like a cocoon.

She blinked. It was gone.

Mrs. Okafor stopped circling. Placed one hand on Lin's head, one on David's.

"You are bound now," she said. "Not to each other — you did that yourselves long ago. But to the *going*. To the choice you have made. It cannot be undone."

"We understand," Lin whispered.

"Do you?" Mrs. Okafor's eyes were kind but fierce. "You are leaving behind everyone who has ever known you. Every place you have ever loved. Every version of yourself you have ever been."

"Yes."

"And you choose this? Freely? Both of you?"

"Yes," they said together.

Mrs. Okafor nodded slowly. Released them. Stepped back.

"Then go," she said. "And do not look back. The blessing will hold as long as you keep moving forward. But if you turn around — if you try to return to what you've left — it will shatter. And so will you."

### EPILOGUE: THE ROOFTOP

The car came at 2 AM. 

Unmarked. Driven by someone Max trusted. Headed for a city Lyra wasn't told the name of.

Lin hugged her before she got in — quick, fierce, unexpected.

"Thank you," she whispered. "For being part of this. For witnessing."

"I didn't do anything."

"You *saw* us." Lin's eyes were bright. "That matters more than you know."

Then she was in the car. Then David was in the car. Then the doors were closing and the engine was starting and they were gone, taillights disappearing around a corner, swallowed by the city.

Lyra stood on the sidewalk, watching the empty street.

"Come on," Max said quietly. "One more stop."

---

The rooftop belonged to no one and everyone — a forgotten space between buildings, accessible only if you knew which fire escape had the broken lock. Max had brought blankets. A thermos of something warm. Two mismatched mugs.

They sat on the edge, legs dangling over the city, and didn't talk.

The skyline glittered. Somewhere below, a car honked. Somewhere above, a plane blinked its way across the darkness.

"Does it ever get easier?" Lyra asked finally. "Watching people leave?"

Max was quiet for a long moment.

"No," he said. "But it gets more... bearable. You learn to hold the grief and the gratitude at the same time. To be sad they're gone and glad they got to go."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It is." He poured something from the thermos — hot chocolate, she realized, as the smell hit her. "That's why you need the rituals. The three days. The cake. The blessing. They're not just for them. They're for us, too. For the people who stay behind."

He handed her a mug. Their fingers brushed.

Neither of them pulled away.

"Max," she said quietly.

"Yeah?"

"Thank you."

"For what?"

"For letting me be part of this." She looked at him — really looked, taking in the shadows under his eyes, the weight in his shoulders, the impossible contradiction of a man who played the fool and carried the world. "For trusting me."

Something flickered across his face. Surprise, maybe. Or something deeper.

"You're easy to trust," he said.

"I'm really not."

"You are to me."

The words hung in the air between them. Heavy. Warm. Terrifying.

Lyra took a sip of her hot chocolate. It was perfect — not too sweet, just bitter enough to feel real.

"This is good," she said.

"Dmitri's recipe. He guards it with his life."

"I believe it."

They sat in silence, drinking hot chocolate on a rooftop, watching the city breathe.

And if Max's hand was resting very close to hers — close enough that she could feel the warmth of it without touching — neither of them mentioned it.

Some things didn't need to be said.

Not yet.
