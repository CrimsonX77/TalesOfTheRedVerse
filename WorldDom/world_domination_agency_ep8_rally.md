The Rally Sequence

The stage was a flatbed truck.

Someone had painted it overnight — deep greens and golds, roots and circuits intertwined, the kind of image that didn't resolve into a single meaning until you stood back far enough. A sound system borrowed from three different sources, cables gaffer-taped along the gutter. Speakers zip-tied to lamp posts. A crowd of maybe two hundred that had become three hundred while Lyra was watching and was still growing.

Remi had built something real.

Lyra stood at the edge of it, coffee going cold in her hand, watching the girl work the crowd from the stage with the instinctive precision of someone who'd been studying pressure and release her whole life. Not a speech — a conversation, just loud enough for everyone to be part of it. She asked questions. She waited for the answers. She made three hundred strangers feel like a room.

She reminds me of someone.

Through her earpiece: Max, somewhere in the crowd, voice unhurried.

"Northeast lamp post. The one with the junction box. Man in the grey jacket has been standing in the same spot for forty minutes and hasn't looked at the stage once."

Lyra found him without moving her head. "I see him. He's watching the north exit."

"There's another one mirroring him at the south. And two more I'm less certain about near the merch table, but the way they're standing—"

"Weight forward," Lyra said.

"Ready to move. Yes." A pause. "Where's Crimson?"

She didn't answer immediately. Because the honest answer was: I don't know, which means he's exactly where he wants to be.

Twenty feet behind the south lamp post, in the shadow between a food truck and a generator unit, Crimson watched the man in the grey jacket's mirror image check his phone for the third time in four minutes.

Waiting for a signal.

He'd had this argument with Max twice in the last eighteen hours and he was going to have it again the moment they were in the same room, but right now the math was simple: you could pull these four quietly, before the rally reached its peak, and nobody would know the threat had existed. Clean. Safe. Max's preference.

Or you could let the crowd see what happened when people tried to disrupt their event. Let them watch it get handled. Let the evidence walk itself into plain sight in front of three hundred witnesses and seventeen phone cameras.

Max thought the risk wasn't worth it.

Max wasn't wrong.

Crimson just disagreed.

He moved along the wall, unhurried, hands in his pockets, the particular quality of stillness that made people's eyes slide past him without landing. The plant near the south exit was already tracking the stage, fingers curled around something in his jacket pocket.

Crimson stepped up beside him like an old friend.

"Don't," he said quietly.

The man turned. Took in the size of the person standing next to him. Made a calculation. Reached anyway.

Crimson's hand closed around his wrist before the grenade cleared the pocket.

The sound was minimal. The man folded in a way that suggested several things had been politely but firmly rearranged. Crimson caught him, guided him down to sitting against the wall, removed the grenade, the phone, and the secondary device he found taped to the man's ankle, and walked away.

Twelve seconds.

Nobody looked.

Into the earpiece: "South plant is down. Northeast is yours if you want him."

Max's voice, dry as concrete: "You started early."

"He was about to move."

"He was waiting for a signal. They move together or not at all. If you've pulled one—"

And then the smoke grenade went off at the north exit.

Not one grenade. Four, near-simultaneous, blooming grey-white across the crowd's edges. Professional spacing — designed to create confusion without a clear direction to run. And then the pushing started, targeted, surgical, three large men wading into the crowd's northeast quadrant and throwing elbows with the calibrated brutality of people who'd been paid to create an incident rather than win a fight.

The crowd lurched. Three hundred people becoming a single panicked organism, looking for exits, finding smoke.

On stage, Remi's voice cut through it — clear, measured, the kind of voice that had learned to be a fixed point.

"Nobody move. Stand where you are. This is manufactured. Don't give them the footage they want."

Half the crowd listened. Enough.

Lyra was already moving toward the northeast quadrant. In her ear, Max, no longer unhurried:

"I need the junction box at the north lamp post in the next forty seconds or their communications stay live."

"On it."

"Crimson—"

"Already moving," Crimson said. Because he was.

The three men working the northeast quadrant were professionals, which meant they were effective and predictable. Same training, same muscle memory, same gap between what they were ready for and what arrived.

Crimson came in from behind the crowd's edge and took the first one out of the picture so cleanly that the man to his left didn't register it had happened for a full three seconds. By that point the second one was down, and the third turned to find the source of the problem and found Crimson already inside his reach.

It wasn't fast exactly. It was efficient in a way that made fast look like it was working too hard. Every movement used exactly as much energy as required and no more, the way water finds level — not because it's trying to, but because it's the natural state of things.

The third man went down.

Lyra, at the junction box, working Max's instructions through the earpiece, caught the last four seconds of it peripherally. Her hands kept moving on the wires. Her eyes stayed on the junction box for approximately three seconds longer than the task required.

From somewhere in the middle distance, Max's voice: "The box, Lyra."

"Done." She reconnected her attention to her hands. "Their comms are cut."

"Good. Now I need you to move to—"

"I see them."

What Max had seen — had been tracking for eleven minutes while Crimson handled the crowd — was the secondary layer.

Not the plants. The enforcers. Four of them, proper hired weight, holding at the block's south entrance waiting for the smoke to clear the crowd enough to move in. The kind of personnel you sent when the message wasn't just cause chaos but make sure someone doesn't walk away.

They were looking for Remi.

Max was already between them and the stage.

This was not because Max was confident he could handle four professional enforcers — he was realistic about numbers. It was because he'd spent six minutes identifying the three chokepoints in the block's layout and had positioned himself at the one that made the other two irrelevant.

The lead enforcer saw him. Assessed. Dismissed.

"Move."

"I don't think I will," Max said pleasantly. "But I'm curious — who's signing the paycheque today? The LLC, or is someone new involved? Because the operational signature here is slightly different from the last two incidents and I've been trying to—"

The enforcer moved.

Max was not there.

He was — and this was the thing that people consistently underestimated, because the suit didn't fit right and he talked too much — good. Not Crimson-good, not that quality of movement that made physics feel optional. Different. Max fought like someone who'd read the last page of the book already and was just walking through the required chapters to get there. Every engagement steered somewhere. Every contact created information.

He was also, currently, losing.

The numbers were wrong and he knew it before the third man reached him.

Then Crimson was there.

They'd never fought side by side. Had no shared language for it, no agreed geometry. And for approximately eight seconds it was chaos — two people with completely incompatible combat philosophies occupying the same space, nearly taking each other out twice, until something clicked.

Not an agreement. More like — a recogntion of each other's rhythm. Crimson stopped filling the whole space and left gaps. Max started using them. Crimson moved big and final. Max moved small and redirecting. Together they were something that didn't have a name.

"I told you," Crimson said, not particularly out of breath, "that taking them before the rally was the better—"

"You told me nothing, you went early and triggered the—"

"The timeline moved, the south plant was—"

"The south plant was waiting for a signal that I had already—"

The fourth enforcer came in hard and they both handled it simultaneously from different angles, which worked better than it had any right to.

"—intercepted," Max finished. "As I was saying."

"Yeah yeah." Crimson rolled his neck. "Shut up and fight, wise guy."

"I'm not — this is talking, I can do both, it's called—"

"There's another one."

"I see him."

What stopped it wasn't either of them.

It was the neighbourhood.

Mrs. Volkov came out of her bakery with her largest wooden rolling pin and a Ukrainian expression that needed no translation. Dmitri appeared from somewhere with Leonard the parrot on one shoulder and a look on his face that suggested he'd been waiting for something like this. Mr. Hassan had, apparently, a cricket bat under the cart that he produced with the calm of a man who kept it specifically for occasions like this.

And then the rest of the block, because word moved the way it always moved here — fast, wordless, certain — and three hundred rally attendees became three hundred and forty, and the people from the buildings came down, and the woman from the dry cleaner brought her brother, and the art student from the bodega showed up with four friends, and Mrs. Okafor walked through it all like it was parting for her which, largely, it was.

The remaining enforcers looked at this.

Did the math.

Left.

The smoke was clearing. Someone had cut the planted speakers. Remi's voice found the crowd again from the stage, steadier than it had any right to be, pulling the energy back toward something that could be held.

Lyra appeared at Max's elbow. She handed him a handkerchief. He looked at it, looked at her, pressed it to the cut above his eyebrow without comment.

Crimson stood a few feet away, looking at the crowd. Not at Remi specifically. Just — looking. Something in his expression that wasn't satisfaction exactly. More like the particular stillness of someone watching a thing they'd known was possible become real.

Max watched Crimson watch the crowd.

Lyra watched Max watch Crimson.

"Well," Max said finally.

Crimson glanced at him.

"Your way worked."

"Yeah." Crimson turned back to the stage. "So did yours."

A pause.

"The junction box," Max said. "I wouldn't have had comms cut in time without—"

"The south entrance," Crimson said. "I didn't see the secondary layer until you were already there."

They stood in silence for a moment. Two people acknowledging something without performing it.

"The official incident report," Lyra said, looking at her phone, "says there was a minor sound equipment malfunction and brief crowd disturbance attributed to a faulty smoke machine rented for the event. Three individuals suffered minor injuries consistent with tripping. Police attended, found no evidence of criminal activity, departed at 16:42."

Max looked at her.

"I've been emailing for the last four minutes," she said. "Mrs. Kim knows someone at the precinct. Dmitri's connection at the permits office. Mr. Hassan apparently has a nephew who—"

"Of course he does," Max said.

From the stage, Remi's rally was becoming something else. Something that hadn't been planned or managed or orchestrated by anyone. Just — people, in a street, feeling the specific electricity of a thing that had tried to be stopped and hadn't been.

Crimson picked up his jacket from the ground. Shrugged it on.

"We're square," he said. It wasn't a question.

"We're square," Max agreed.

Crimson nodded once. Started to leave.

"Crimson."

He stopped. Didn't turn.

"She's going to figure out you were the client," Max said. "Probably today."

A long pause.

"Yeah," Crimson said. "I know."

He walked away through the crowd. People made space without knowing why.

Lyra watched him go.

Max watched Lyra watch him go.

Said nothing. But the small line between his eyebrows — the one that appeared when he filed something for later — was there.

On the stage, Remi had stopped speaking.

She was looking at the back of the crowd. At a particular broad-shouldered silhouette moving toward the block's edge.

Something on her face that she would have furiously denied was anything at all.

She looked away. Back to her crowd. Back to the work.
