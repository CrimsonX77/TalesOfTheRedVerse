# THE WORLD DOMINATION AGENCY LTD™
## Episode Three: "The Network"

---

### COLD OPEN

The dreams started on Friday.

Lyra woke at 3:47 AM, gasping, heart hammering, with the absolute certainty that she had just lived an entire lifetime in a world where she'd never found the alley. Never seen the sign. Never met Max.

In that life, she'd gone back to Ohio. Moved in with her mother. Taken a job at a insurance company and spent forty-three years filing claims and eating lunch alone and wondering why everything felt like a movie she'd wandered into by accident.

She'd died at sixty-seven. Heart attack. Alone in an apartment that smelled like microwaved dinners and regret.

And the last thing she'd thought, in that other life, was: *I should have laughed.*

Lyra sat in her tiny sublet, shaking, and tried to convince herself it was just a dream.

Just stress. Just the conversation with Ellis, the threats, the filing cabinet humming in her memory.

Just a dream.

But she could still taste the microwave lasagna. Could still feel the weight of forty-three years of *nothing*.

And somewhere, in the part of her mind that was learning to pay attention, she wondered:

*What's in the PROMETHEUS file?*

*And why does it feel like it's already inside me?*

---

### ACT ONE: THE COLLECTION

"You look terrible," Max said cheerfully when she arrived at the office. "Did you sleep at all?"

"Did *you*?"

He considered this. "Depends on your definition of sleep. I achieved a state of horizontal unconsciousness for approximately four hours, during which I may or may not have solved three outstanding problems and composed a strongly-worded letter to the concept of linear time."

"That's not an answer."

"Welcome to working with me." He slid a coffee across the desk. "Drink. We have a busy day. I'm calling in the network."

"The network?"

Max smiled — that real smile, the one that made him look almost young. "Time you met the neighborhood, Ms. Chen."

---

They walked.

Not drove — walked. Through alleys Lyra had never noticed, up fire escapes she would have sworn were decorative, across rooftops connected by bridges made of shipping pallets and hope.

"The city has layers," Max explained as they navigated a path between water towers. "The official layer — streets, addresses, the map everyone agrees on. And then there's this." He gestured at the rooftop garden they were passing through, where an elderly man in a sun hat was tending tomatoes that had no business growing in January. "The *real* city. The one that exists because people decided it should."

The man in the sun hat looked up. Nodded at Max. Said something in a language Lyra didn't recognize.

Max responded in the same language, fluid and easy.

"Mr. Volkov," he said as they passed. "Ukrainian. Came here in '94. The official story says he's a retired plumber living on social security. The real story is he ran the largest smuggling operation in the Eastern Bloc for thirty years and retired when his wife told him she wanted grandchildren."

"And he grows tomatoes now?"

"Everyone needs a hobby." Max hopped over a gap between buildings. "I helped him with a passport situation last year. Completely legal passport, mind you — just needed to exist more... quietly than the normal channels allow."

"You forged documents for a former smuggler?"

"I *facilitated* his transition to legitimate retirement. There's a difference." He paused at a door marked MAINTENANCE — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. "And now he owes me a favor. Just like Mrs. Okafor owes me a favor. And Dmitri. And the Hendersons. And approximately three hundred and forty-seven other people in a six-block radius."

He opened the door.

Inside: a stairwell that descended into darkness.

"Welcome to the collection, Lyra. Everyone I've ever helped. Everyone who owes me something. Everyone who's decided that the weird guy in the back-alley office is worth protecting."

"That's... a lot of favors."

"That's the point." He started down the stairs. "This is how you take over the world. Not with armies. Not with money. With *kindness that compounds*. You solve enough small problems for enough small people, and eventually..." He glanced back, eyes glinting in the dim light. "Eventually, you're not small anymore."

---

The basement was a speakeasy.

Not the trendy kind with craft cocktails and ironic Edison bulbs. An actual speakeasy — the real thing, preserved in amber since Prohibition, complete with a bar made from a single slab of oak and booths upholstered in leather that had seen things.

It was also packed.

Lyra counted at least forty people — no, fifty — no, more kept arriving, filtering in through doors she couldn't see, filling the space with low conversation and the clink of glasses. A United Nations of the unofficial city: old and young, every ethnicity, every language, connected only by the fact that they were all looking at Max.

"Ladies, gentlemen, and those who have transcended such limiting categories," Max announced, hopping onto the bar with the ease of someone who'd done it many times before. "Thank you for coming on short notice. I know you're all very busy with your various enterprises, vendettas, and victory gardens."

Scattered laughter. The sound of a community that shared jokes Lyra didn't understand yet.

"As some of you have heard," Max continued, "we have a situation. The people I've been hiding from have found me. They're sending someone — probably multiple someones — and they're not interested in negotiation."

The room went quiet.

"I'm not asking you to fight." Max's voice was steady. Serious. "That's not what we do. But I am asking you to *watch*. To *listen*. To be the eyes and ears that no amount of money can buy." He paused. "They think they're looking for one man in a city of eight million. They don't know they're looking for one man protected by three hundred."

An older woman near the front — elegant, silver-haired, with the bearing of someone who'd once been very important — raised her hand.

"What do we get in return?"

"What you've always gotten, Mrs. Ashworth. My help when you need it. My discretion when you don't. And the knowledge that you're part of something bigger than any of us." Max smiled. "Also, I'm told there will be an open bar after the meeting. Dmitri's experimenting with infused vodkas."

"The peacock one was a mistake," someone called out. "My tongue was blue for a week."

"Gerald was offended you didn't appreciate his essence!"

More laughter. The tension broke, just slightly.

Max hopped down from the bar and made his way to Lyra, accepting handshakes and shoulder-claps and murmured words she couldn't hear. These people *loved* him. Not feared — loved. The way you love the person who saw you when no one else was looking.

"What do you think?" he asked quietly.

Lyra looked around the room. At the faces. The connections. The invisible web of obligation and affection that Max had spent years weaving.

"I think," she said slowly, "that you might actually pull this off."

"The world domination?"

"The survival."

His smile flickered. Something sad underneath.

"One day at a time, Ms. Chen. One day at a time."

### INTERLUDE: THE ASHWORTH CONVERSATION

After the meeting, while Dmitri poured questionable vodka and the network mingled like the strangest cocktail party in history, the silver-haired woman approached.

"Mrs. Ashworth," Max said. "May I introduce Lyra Chen, my—"

"I know who she is." Mrs. Ashworth's eyes were sharp, assessing. "I know everything about everyone who enters Max's orbit. It's a habit."

"Former CIA," Max murmured to Lyra. "Station chief in three countries. Retired when she realized she was working for idiots."

"Five countries," Mrs. Ashworth corrected. "Two were classified." She turned her full attention to Lyra. "He trusts you. That's rare. He doesn't trust easily — not since what happened."

"What *did* happen?"

Mrs. Ashworth glanced at Max. A silent question.

He nodded, almost imperceptibly.

"He invented something," Mrs. Ashworth said. "Something that shouldn't be possible. A technology that fundamentally alters human consciousness. When his family discovered what he'd created, they tried to take it. When they couldn't, they tried to take *him*."

"I know that much."

"Do you?" Mrs. Ashworth's voice dropped. "Do you know that he used it on himself? That he's not... entirely... the same person he was before?"

Lyra thought about the dreams. The lifetime in Ohio. The taste of microwave lasagna.

"I'm beginning to understand," she said.

Mrs. Ashworth nodded slowly. "He sees things now. Possibilities. Branches. The mathematics of choice spreading out in every direction." She paused. "It's why he's so good at what he does. Why he always knows which favor to call in, which string to pull. He's not guessing, Ms. Chen. He's *seeing*."

"Seeing what?"

"Everything." Mrs. Ashworth's eyes were very old. "Every version of how this could go. Every path. Every outcome." She leaned in. "The question is — which one is he steering us toward?"

### ACT TWO: THE FIRST STRIKE

They came that night.

Not Ellis. Not Constantine's thugs. Something worse.

Professionals.

Lyra was walking home from the speakeasy — alone, despite Max's objections, because she needed time to think — when she noticed the van.

White. Unmarked. Idling at the corner with its lights off.

She kept walking. Didn't change her pace. Didn't look directly at it.

Three blocks later, there was another one.

*Shit.*

Her phone buzzed. A text from a number she didn't recognize:

**Turn left. Now. Don't run.**

She turned left.

The alley was narrow, garbage-strewn, the kind of place you'd expect to find trouble. Instead, she found a door that hadn't been there yesterday — she was SURE it hadn't been there yesterday — and Mr. Volkov standing in front of it, holding a watering can like it was the most natural thing in the world.

"Inside," he said. "Quickly."

She went inside.

The door led to a bakery. The bakery led to a dry cleaner. The dry cleaner led to a basement that connected to a subway tunnel that hadn't been used since 1987.

By the time she emerged, three blocks from her apartment, the vans were gone.

Her phone buzzed again.

**You're clear. Get home. Stay there. - M**

She texted back: **How did you know?**

The response came immediately:

**I know everything. Also Mrs. Ashworth has a drone. Several drones. Possibly too many drones. We're working on it.**

Then:

**They'll try again. We need to move faster.**

Then:

**Get some sleep. Tomorrow, we go on offense.**

Lyra stared at her phone. At the impossible escape route. At the network that had swallowed her whole and spit her out safely on the other side.

*What have I gotten myself into?*

But even as she thought it, she was smiling.

### ACT THREE: THE FAVOR

The next morning, Max was waiting outside her building.

Not inside — outside. On the street. In a suit that actually fit, for once. Looking like someone who belonged in boardrooms instead of back alleys.

"Change of plans," he said. "We're visiting someone."

"Who?"

"My sister."

---

The St. Claire-Pemberton family compound was in Connecticut.

Of course it was in Connecticut. Old money always lived in Connecticut, in houses that looked like they'd been there since before the concept of taxes was invented.

They drove in a borrowed car — borrowed from the network, naturally — through miles of manicured grounds to a manor house that made Lyra's entire life feel like a rounding error.

"I thought your family wanted you dead," she said.

"My family is complicated." Max parked in the circular drive. "My father's side wants me dead. My mother's side wants me controlled. And my sister..." He paused. "My sister just wants to understand."

"Understand what?"

He didn't answer.

The door opened before they could knock. A woman stood there — late twenties, Max's eyes, Max's cheekbones, but none of his chaos. She was precision where he was entropy. Control where he was hurricane.

"Maximilian," she said. "You look terrible."

"Evelyn," he replied. "You look exactly the same."

"Stability is a virtue." Her gaze shifted to Lyra. "And who is this?"

"Lyra Chen. My associate."

"Associate." Evelyn's lips twitched. "You've never had an 'associate' before. You barely have friends."

"I'm expanding my social circle. Personal growth."

"You're in danger and you need something from me." Evelyn stepped aside. "Come in. I assume this is about PROMETHEUS."

Max flinched. Actually flinched. 

Lyra had never seen him do that before.

"You know about—"

"I know about everything, brother." Evelyn led them into a sitting room that cost more than most countries. "I've known since you first started the project. Did you think Mother's side of the family wasn't watching? That we wouldn't notice when the golden child started playing with things that could remake reality?"

"I was careful."

"You were *arrogant*." She poured tea — actual tea, from an actual tea service, like they were in a period drama. "You thought you were the only one smart enough to understand what you'd built. You thought you could control the implications." She handed him a cup. "And then you used it on yourself."

"I had to know if it worked."

"You had to know what it *felt* like." Evelyn's voice was sharp. "Don't lie to me, Max. I know you. You didn't test PROMETHEUS because you wanted data. You tested it because you couldn't resist. Because the idea of experiencing every version of yourself was too tempting to refuse."

The room went very quiet.

Lyra watched Max's face. Watched the walls come down, just for a moment, showing something raw underneath.

"It's not what you think," he said softly. "It's not power. It's not enlightenment. It's..." He closed his eyes. "It's *grief*, Evelyn. Infinite grief. Every life you could have lived. Every person you could have been. Every choice you didn't make." He opened his eyes. "I've died a trillion times. I've loved and lost in ways you can't imagine. I've been the hero and the monster and everything in between."

He set down the tea cup. His hands were shaking.

"I used it twice," he said. "Because after the first time, I thought I understood. I thought I'd integrated the experience. But I hadn't. I'd just... skimmed the surface. So I went back in. Deeper. Longer." He laughed — a broken sound. "Do you know what you find at the bottom of infinity, Evelyn? At the end of every possible self?"

"What?"

"*This*." He gestured at himself. "Just this. Just the person who comes out the other side. All those lives, all those possibilities, and you still end up exactly where you were always going to end up." His voice cracked. "You just know more about why."

The silence stretched.

Evelyn set down her own cup. Something had shifted in her face — the first crack in the porcelain.

"What do you need, Max?"

"Access to the family archives. The real ones. Mother's side." He leaned forward. "They're coming for me. Father's people. And I need to know what I'm fighting. What resources they have. What they're willing to do."

"That information is—"

"Evelyn." His voice was quiet. Intense. "Please."

She stared at him for a long moment. 

Then she stood.

"Wait here."

She left the room.

Lyra looked at Max. At the cracks in his armor. At the man underneath who had lived a trillion lives and was still just *tired*.

"Are you okay?" she asked.

He laughed weakly. "Define 'okay.'"

"Do you need... I don't know. A hug? A drink? A detailed spreadsheet of all the ways this could go wrong?"

That surprised a real laugh out of him. "The spreadsheet might help. I do love a good spreadsheet."

"Max."

"I'm fine." He wasn't. They both knew it. "I'm just... remembering things I usually try to forget. That's the cost of PROMETHEUS. It never really leaves you. Every life I lived is still *there*, waiting for me to remember it."

"Like dreams?"

His eyes snapped to hers. Sharp. Searching.

"Why do you ask?"

Lyra thought about Ohio. About forty-three years of insurance claims. About dying alone in an apartment that smelled like regret.

"Just curious," she lied.

Max watched her for a long moment. Something shifted in his expression — recognition, maybe. Or concern.

But before he could speak, Evelyn returned.

She was carrying a folder.

"This is everything Mother's side has on Father's current operations. Finances, personnel, ongoing projects." She handed it to Max. "And this—" She produced a smaller envelope, sealed with wax. "—is a personal favor from me. Use it wisely."

Max took both. "Thank you, Evie."

"Don't call me that." But she was almost smiling. "And Max? Be careful. You're not the only one who cares about you. Even if we show it badly."

He nodded once.

They left the way they came.

In the car, driving back to the city, Max finally spoke.

"She knows about you."

"What?"

"The dreams." He didn't look at her. "PROMETHEUS has... side effects. Proximity to someone who's been exposed can cause... echoes. Bleed-through from other timelines."

Lyra's stomach dropped.

"You're telling me I'm dreaming about other versions of my life because I *work* for you?"

"I'm telling you it's possible." He finally glanced at her. "I'm sorry. I should have warned you. But honestly, I didn't know you'd be susceptible. Most people aren't."

"And what does it mean that I am?"

He was quiet for a long moment.

"It means you might be important," he said finally. "It means the universe is paying attention to you."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be."

---

### TAG

That night, Lyra dreamed of Ohio again.

But this time, something was different.

This time, in the dream, she was sitting in her insurance company cubicle, staring at the same claim she'd been processing for forty years — and she *remembered*.

Remembered the alley. The sign. The office. Max.

In the dream, she stood up.

In the dream, she walked out.

In the dream, she found the door that shouldn't exist, the path that only appeared if you knew where to look, and she followed it all the way back to where she'd started.

She woke up in her apartment, heart pounding, with two absolute certainties:

First: The dreams weren't random. They were showing her something. Teaching her something.

Second: Whatever Max had built, whatever PROMETHEUS actually was — it wasn't just a weapon.

It was an *invitation*.

And somehow, without ever having touched it, she was starting to accept.
