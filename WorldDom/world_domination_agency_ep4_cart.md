# THE WORLD DOMINATION AGENCY LTD™
## Episode Four: "The Cart"

---

### COLD OPEN

The falafel was perfect.

Lyra hadn't meant to become a regular. It just... happened. The cart was on her route from the subway to the office, and Mr. Hassan had a way of making you feel like you'd been coming there for years even on your first visit.

"Ah, the assistant!" he called out when he saw her, already reaching for the pita. "Extra tahini, yes? And the pickled turnips you pretend you don't like but always eat?"

"I don't *pretend* —"

"You make a face." He demonstrated — an exaggerated grimace of disgust. "And then you eat them all. Every time."

Lyra felt her cheeks warm. "They grow on you."

"Everything good grows on you." He assembled her sandwich with the practiced efficiency of fifteen years, hands moving in patterns so familiar they'd become art. "That is the secret of life. The things you resist become the things you need."

He handed her the wrapped falafel, waving away her attempt to pay.

"Mr. Hassan—"

"You work for the strange one in the building with no sign. He helped my nephew with immigration papers last year. Your money is no good here." His eyes crinkled. "Besides. You will need your strength today, I think."

"Why?"

But he just smiled and turned to the next customer.

Lyra walked the remaining blocks to the office with the falafel warm in her hands and the strange feeling that she'd just been given a message she didn't know how to read.

---

### ACT ONE: THE PROBLEM

Max was already in the office when she arrived, which was unusual. He was sitting at his desk — actually sitting, not pacing or juggling or doing a handstand against the filing cabinet like that one time she still didn't have an explanation for.

He was reading a letter.

"Morning," she said, setting down her bag.

No response.

"Max?"

He looked up. His eyes were doing the thing — the distant thing, like he was seeing something behind reality's curtain. Then he blinked, and he was back.

"Lyra! Excellent. You brought falafel. Is it from Hassan?"

"How did you—" She stopped herself. "Yes. He wouldn't let me pay."

"He never does." Max held up the letter. "He also never asks for help. Which is why this is concerning."

Lyra crossed the room and took the letter. Handwritten, careful script, the kind of penmanship that came from a generation that still believed in fountain pens.

> *Dear Mr. Max,*
>
> *I am sorry to trouble you. You have done so much for my family already. But there is a situation with my cart, and I do not know who else to ask.*
>
> *Three health inspections in two weeks. Violations I did not make. My permit is "under review" for reasons no one will explain. Yesterday, my supplier said he cannot deliver to me anymore. He would not say why, but he was afraid.*
>
> *I have been on that corner for fifteen years. I am not a stupid man. I know when I am being pushed.*
>
> *If you can help, I would be grateful. If you cannot, I understand. Some fights are too big for small people.*
>
> *Your friend,*
> *Yusuf Hassan*

Lyra read it twice. The second time, something cold settled in her stomach.

"Someone's trying to force him out," she said.

"Yes."

"But why? It's a falafel cart. What could anyone possibly want with—"

"The corner." Max was at the window now, looking out at nothing. "Not the cart. The corner. 47th and Vine."

He was quiet for a moment. Lyra had learned to wait when he got like this.

"Do you know how many pedestrians pass that corner every day?" he asked.

"No."

"Three thousand, four hundred and twelve. Give or take seasonal variation." He turned to look at her. "Do you know who owns the buildings on all four sides of that intersection?"

"No."

"Three weeks ago, I would have said four different people. But I've been watching the property transfers." He walked to the filing cabinet — the one that hummed, the one with the military-grade lock — and pulled open a different drawer. A normal drawer. Full of folders.

He extracted one and handed it to her.

"Now the answer is: one person owns three of them. The fourth is a rent-controlled apartment building with seventeen tenants, twelve of whom have lived there for over a decade."

Lyra flipped through the folder. Property records. Transfer documents. A name that appeared again and again: **Meridian Holdings LLC.**

"Who's behind Meridian Holdings?"

"That's the question, isn't it?" Max's smile didn't reach his eyes. "Shell company behind a shell company behind a trust registered in Delaware. The kind of structure you build when you don't want anyone to know what you're buying or why."

"And the cart?"

"The cart is in the way. Can't build on a corner if there's a beloved local institution parked on it. Can't displace a vendor with fifteen years of community goodwill without looking like the villain." He took the folder back, tucked it under his arm. "So instead, you make his life impossible. Death by a thousand paper cuts. Health violations. Permit issues. Supply chain disruption. You don't *evict* him. You make him *choose* to leave."

"That's—"

"Elegant. In a soulless, capitalism-as-entropy kind of way." Max grabbed his coat. "Come on. We're going to go sit near a falafel cart for a while."

"Sit?"

"Sit. Watch. Count." He was already heading for the door. "Bring your falafel. It's going to be a long morning."

---

### ACT ONE CONTINUED: THE WATCHING

They sat on a bench across the street from Hassan's cart.

For an hour.

Max didn't explain what he was doing. He just... watched. His eyes tracked pedestrians, lingered on storefronts, occasionally unfocused in that way that made Lyra's skin prickle.

She ate her falafel. Watched him watch the corner. Tried to figure out what she was supposed to be learning.

"Seventeen," Max said suddenly.

"What?"

"Seventeen people have walked past the cart in the last four minutes and not bought anything. Three months ago, the conversion rate on this corner was one in eight. Now it's one in twenty-three."

Lyra frowned. "How do you know what it was three months ago?"

"I count things."

"You count *everything*?"

"Not everything." He was still watching the corner. "Just the things that matter. Foot traffic patterns. Conversion rates. The rhythm of a neighborhood." A pause. "You can feel when something's wrong, if you pay attention. The numbers change before the reality does."

She didn't know what to say to that. So she said nothing.

After another few minutes, Max stood up.

"The health inspector will be here in about twenty minutes," he said. "Fourth visit this month. Let's go introduce ourselves."

"How do you know—"

But he was already walking.

Lyra hurried to follow, tossing her falafel wrapper in a trash can. She didn't ask how he knew about the inspector. She was starting to learn that some questions didn't have answers she'd understand.

*Trust the outcome*, she told herself. *Even when the method feels wrong.*

She wasn't sure when she'd started thinking that way.

---

### ACT TWO: THE INSPECTOR

The health inspector was a nervous man named Gerald Huang who sweated through his shirt before he'd even reached the cart.

Max intercepted him halfway across the street.

"Mr. Huang! What a pleasure. I don't think we've met — I'm Max, I run the consulting firm in the building with no sign. You know the one. Third floor. We handle... special situations."

Gerald Huang went pale.

"I'm just here to—"

"Conduct your fourth inspection of Mr. Hassan's cart this month, yes. Remarkable dedication to public health. The city is lucky to have you." Max's smile was warm, friendly, and somehow terrifying. "I'm curious, though. In your professional opinion, does a cart that's passed every health inspection for fifteen years suddenly develop critical violations? Or does someone with access to the right forms decide it should?"

"I don't know what you're—"

"Of course you don't." Max patted his shoulder gently. "You're just doing your job. Following orders. Someone puts a file on your desk, you process it. That's how bureaucracy works. No one's ever *responsible* because everyone's just following procedure."

Gerald Huang was sweating more now.

"The thing is," Max continued, his voice still pleasant, "I've been looking into who's been putting those files on your desk. And I've been looking into *you*, Gerald. Your mortgage. Your daughter's tuition. The gambling debts you thought no one knew about."

All the color drained from Gerald's face.

"I'm not threatening you," Max said quickly. "I want to be very clear about that. I'm just... observing. The way someone's been observing Mr. Hassan. The way someone decided his cart was a problem that needed solving."

He leaned in closer.

"Here's what's going to happen. You're going to conduct your inspection. You're going to find that everything is in perfect order — because it is. And then you're going to go back to your office and lose whatever file someone's been using to target this cart. Permanently."

"I can't just—"

"You can. You will." Max's smile widened. "Because the alternative is that I start making phone calls. Not to your boss — your boss is probably in on it. To the *other* people who might be interested in knowing about the pressure being applied to small business owners in this neighborhood. Journalists. City councilors. That one investigator in the AG's office who's been looking for exactly this kind of case."

He stepped back.

"Or you can lose a file and we all go on with our lives. Your choice, Gerald. I believe in free will."

---

Lyra watched the whole thing from ten feet away.

She watched Gerald Huang conduct the fastest, most cursory health inspection she'd ever seen. She watched him sign off on everything with shaking hands. She watched him practically run back to his car.

She watched Max wave cheerfully as he drove away.

"That was..." She searched for the word.

"Unethical?" Max suggested. "Manipulative? Arguably a form of blackmail?"

"I was going to say *effective*."

He looked at her. Something shifted in his expression — surprise, maybe, or something softer.

"That's not the reaction most people have."

"Most people haven't watched you work." She fell into step beside him as they walked back toward Mr. Hassan's cart. "You didn't actually threaten him. You just... showed him the shape of what could happen."

"The map is not the territory. But sometimes showing someone the map is enough."

"Where did you learn to do that?"

He was quiet for a moment. Too long.

"Experience," he said finally. "The expensive kind."

She didn't push.

---

### INTERLUDE: THE NETWORK MOVES

Over the next two days, Lyra watched the network do what it did.

Mrs. Volkov knew a woman whose brother worked at the permit office. The "under review" status on Hassan's permit mysteriously resolved itself.

Dmitri had a connection at the distribution company Hassan's supplier worked for. A single phone call, and deliveries resumed.

Mr. Chen from the dry cleaner happened to mention to Mrs. Okafor that he'd seen men in suits photographing the buildings around 47th and Vine. Mrs. Okafor mentioned it to Max. Max added it to a file Lyra wasn't allowed to see.

None of it was dramatic. None of it made headlines. It was just... people helping people, in a web so intricate she was only starting to see the edges.

"How long did it take you to build this?" she asked Max on the second day, as they walked back from checking on Hassan.

"Build what?"

"The network. The favors. The... whatever this is."

He considered the question.

"I didn't build it," he said finally. "I just... started helping people. One at a time. And then they helped other people. And then those people helped me. It's not a structure. It's an ecosystem."

"That takes time."

"It takes *presence*." He stopped walking, turning to look at her. "Most people move through neighborhoods like ghosts. They don't see the dry cleaner or the baker or the old woman who feeds the pigeons. They don't learn names. They don't remember faces. They're too busy going somewhere else to notice where they are."

"And you notice."

"I notice." He started walking again. "I notice that Mrs. Kim's hip hurts more in cold weather, so I shovel her steps before she wakes up. I notice that the teenager at the bodega is saving for art school, so I buy his sketches and hang them in my office. I notice that the street musician on the corner of 45th plays better when someone's actually listening, so I sit on the bench for twenty minutes every Tuesday."

He shrugged.

"It's not altruism. It's investment. Every small kindness is a thread. Enough threads, and you have a net. Enough nets, and you have a safety system that doesn't need institutions or authorities. Just people who know each other. People who *see* each other."

Lyra thought about this.

"That's either beautiful or terrifying," she said.

"Why not both?"

---

### ACT THREE: THE BUYER

On the third day, the buyer made a mistake.

A man in an expensive suit showed up at Hassan's cart. Not to buy falafel — to make an offer. A generous offer, by his telling. Enough money to retire on. All Hassan had to do was give up the corner.

Hassan said no.

The man in the suit smiled and said he should think about it. Think about his family. Think about how difficult running a business could be when the city decided to pay attention.

Hassan said no again.

The man in the suit stopped smiling.

Max was there before Lyra even realized he'd moved.

"Marcus Webb," Max said pleasantly, appearing at the man's elbow like he'd materialized from nothing. "Senior acquisitions manager for Meridian Holdings. Yale Law, class of 2015. Divorced, no kids, apartment in Tribeca that costs more per month than most people make in a year."

Marcus Webb went very still.

"Impressive memory," he said carefully.

"I have a gift." Max's smile was bright, friendly, and utterly devoid of warmth. "I also have a file. On you. On Meridian Holdings. On the very interesting property acquisitions you've been making in this neighborhood over the past eighteen months."

"That's all public record."

"The acquisitions are. The methods aren't." Max tilted his head slightly. "Seventeen small business owners pressured to sell in the past year. Three of them lost their livelihoods entirely. One had a heart attack from the stress. He's fine now, but his daughter had to drop out of college to help pay the medical bills."

Marcus Webb's jaw tightened.

"All legal."

"Legal isn't the same as *right*." Max stepped closer. "Here's what I know: Meridian Holdings is a front for someone who wants to control this intersection. Not for the real estate — the buildings are worth less than the hassle of acquiring them. For something else. Something about *position*."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No. You probably don't. You're a middle manager. You follow orders, just like Gerald Huang. Just like everyone in the machine." Max's voice softened, almost gentle. "But here's what I want you to tell whoever's giving those orders: this corner is protected. This *neighborhood* is protected. And if anyone tries again — the inspections, the permits, the 'generous offers' — I'll stop being polite."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's a forecast." Max smiled. "I'm very good at forecasting. Ask anyone."

Marcus Webb looked at him for a long moment. Something flickered in his expression — not fear, exactly, but reassessment. The realization that he was dealing with someone who didn't operate by normal rules.

"I'll pass along the message," he said finally.

"Please do."

Marcus Webb turned and walked away. He didn't look back.

Max watched him go, eyes tracking his path down the street with that distant intensity that made Lyra's skin prickle.

"It won't stop them," he said quietly. "Whoever's behind this. They'll try again."

"So what do we do?"

"We wait. We watch. We make sure Hassan knows he's not alone." He turned to look at her. "And we find out who's really pulling the strings. Because *this*—" he gestured at the retreating figure of Marcus Webb "—this is just a symptom. Someone wanted this specific corner, this specific intersection, badly enough to spend two years and substantial resources acquiring it piece by piece."

"Why?"

Max was quiet for a moment.

"I have a theory," he said. "But I need more information before I say it out loud."

"Why?"

"Because saying things out loud makes them real. And this theory..." He shook his head. "Let's just say I hope I'm wrong."

### EPILOGUE: THE COFFEE

The next morning, Lyra got to the office early.

She didn't think about why. She just did. Stopped at the café on the way — the good one, three blocks out of her way, where Dmitri had shown her how to order the thing Max liked but never asked for.

She was halfway up the stairs before she realized what she'd done.

*I bought him coffee without being asked.*

She stood on the landing, holding the cup, feeling suddenly very aware of her own hands.

*When did I start doing that?*

*Why did I start doing that?*

The door at the top of the stairs opened. Max leaned out, hair disheveled, clearly having slept on the office couch again.

"Is that coffee?"

"I—" She looked down at the cup. "Yes."

"Is it the thing from Dmitri's? The one with the cardamom and the—"

"Yes."

His face did something complicated. Surprise, then something softer, then the usual mask of cheerful chaos sliding back into place.

"You didn't have to do that."

"I know."

A pause. Longer than it should have been.

"Thank you," he said. And for once, there was no joke underneath it. No deflection. Just the words, simple and real.

"You're welcome."

She climbed the rest of the stairs. Handed him the coffee. Walked past him into the office.

The filing cabinet hummed in its corner. Low. Patient. Waiting.

She didn't look at it.

---

That evening, after Hassan had closed his cart and the neighborhood had settled into its nighttime rhythms, Lyra found Max on the roof.

He was sitting on the edge, legs dangling over the city, watching the intersection of 47th and Vine with that distant intensity.

She sat down beside him. Didn't say anything. Just... sat.

After a while, Max spoke.

"Thank you. For the coffee."

"You already thanked me."

"I know. I'm thanking you again." He was still looking at the corner. "It's been a long time since someone... noticed. What I might want. Without being asked."

Lyra felt something twist in her chest.

"You notice things about everyone else," she said quietly. "Mrs. Kim's hip. The art student's sketches. The musician on 45th. Someone should notice things about you, too."

He turned to look at her.

The city glittered below them. Somewhere in the distance, a siren wailed.

"Lyra," he said.

"Yeah?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Shook his head.

"Never mind. It's not—" He stopped. Started again. "Thank you. For staying. Most people don't stay."

"I'm not most people."

"No." That complicated expression again. The one she couldn't quite read. "No, you're really not."

They sat in silence, watching the city breathe.

And if his hand was resting very close to hers on the rooftop edge — close enough that she could feel the warmth of it without touching — neither of them mentioned it.

Some things were still too new to name.
