EPISODE 7 — "THE RALLY BEFORE THE RALLY"

COLD OPEN

The permit office smelled like fluorescent lighting and defeated ambition.

Remi had been here four times. The woman at window three knew her by name now, which would have been flattering under different circumstances.

"Still under review," the woman said, not unkindly. She had the expression of someone who'd been delivering bad news long enough that it had become weather — impersonal, ambient, something that just happened to people.

"It's been eleven days," Remi said.

"These things take time."

"The Meridian Tech installation on the same block got permits in forty-eight hours."

The woman's expression didn't change. Didn't need to change. The answer was in its stillness.

Remi picked up her folder. Thanked her, because her mother had raised her right even if the system hadn't, and walked out into the grey afternoon.

She stood on the steps of the permit office and did the thing she never let anyone see her do — she calculated. Laid out the board. Counted what she had, what was gone, what she'd built and what had been quietly removed while she was looking the other way.

Three coalition partners. Two supply contacts. The venue they'd booked through a friend of a friend that had suddenly become unavailable. The social posts that kept getting flagged. The email to the city councilor that had gone unanswered for nine days.

One by one. Quietly. The kind of pressure you couldn't point to because every individual failure had a plausible explanation.

Remi was twenty-three years old and already very tired of plausible explanations.

She pulled out her phone. Found the forwarded message from a contact she trusted — these people solve problems, go see them, don't ask me how I know about them — and looked at the address.

An alley she knew. A building she'd walked past.

She'd thought it was abandoned.

ACT ONE: THE INTAKE

Lyra opened the door before Remi knocked.

"I heard you on the stairs," Lyra said, stepping back to let her in. "You walk like someone who's annoyed but managing it."

Remi blinked. Walked in anyway.

She was shorter than Lyra had expected from Crimson's description — not small, just compact, the way certain people are, built for efficiency. Her hair was natural, pulled back practically, a single paint-stained clip holding it. She was wearing the kind of clothes that had been good once and were now working clothes — kept clean, not precious about it. Her eyes moved around the room with the same systematic speed Lyra was beginning to recognize as a specific kind of intelligence.

She clocked Max. Reassessed. Clocked the filing cabinet. Clocked Lyra.

"You're the agency," she said. Statement, not question.

"We are."

"You solve problems."

"When they're interesting," Max said from behind his desk. He hadn't looked up from whatever he was reading. "Sit down, Ms.—"

"Remi."

"Remi. What brings you to The World Domination Agency?"

A beat. "Your name is insane."

"Our results aren't." He looked up. His eyes did the thing — the full-attention thing, the seeing-several-things-at-once thing. "Sit down."

She sat.

She talked for twenty minutes without stopping.

Not because she was nervous — Lyra had interviewed enough clients by now to know the difference between nervous talking and efficient talking. Remi had clearly done this before, laid her situation out for people who might or might not be able to help, and she'd learned to give them everything they needed without wasting their time.

The rally. The cause. The practical politics of the anti-chrome movement in this specific district at this specific moment — not abstract ideology, but concrete: three families on her block whose primary earner had taken chrome-work augmentation packages under predatory financing terms and were now in debt structures they didn't understand and couldn't exit. A teenager she knew who'd been recruited by a Chrome-head adjacent crew with promises of augmentation access and a sense of belonging. The medical debt industry that had quietly begun partnering with augmentation companies to offer body modification as debt restructuring.

"They make it sound like a solution," she said. "You're in debt, you can't pay, but you can work off the interest if you take the package. And then suddenly you're contracted labor with a body that belongs to someone else's payment plan."

Max had stopped pretending to read. His elbows were on the desk.

"That's not in any of the coverage," Lyra said quietly.

"Because the coverage is written by people who think the chrome debate is philosophical." Remi's voice was even. Not angry — past anger, into something colder and more useful. "It's not. It's economic. It's the same story it's always been, just with new hardware."

Silence.

Then Max: "Tell me about Meridian."

Her expression shifted. "How do you know about Meridian?"

"Tell me what you know."

She knew more than she should have been able to, which Lyra was beginning to recognize as a pattern with the people Crimson apparently noticed. Not inside information — assembled information. She'd been mapping the property transfers. She'd cross-referenced the LLC registrations. She'd identified three shell layers without legal access to any of the underlying documents.

"I can't prove it yet," she said. "I know the shape of it but not the mechanism."

"We can help with the mechanism," Max said.

"In exchange for?"

"Nothing you'd find objectionable." He leaned back. "We have a particular interest in Meridian's activities in this area. Your rally is useful to us strategically. Your intelligence on their financial structure is useful to us operationally." He was quiet for a moment. "Also I think what you're doing matters, but I find people trust me more when I'm honest about my motivations than when I pretend to be purely altruistic."

Remi studied him.

"You're strange," she said.

"Frequently."

"I'll take the help." She stood, extending her hand. "But I run the rally. My decisions."

"Naturally."

"And if I find out you've been managing me—"

"You'd know," Max said simply. "You're not someone who gets managed without noticing."

Something crossed her face. Not quite surprise. The particular expression of someone who'd been underestimated so consistently that being accurately assessed landed differently than it should.

She shook his hand.

INTERLUDE: THE MEMORY

The memory came the way it always did — not invited, just there, slotted into a quiet moment like something her brain refused to let stay filed.

The shop.

Her uncle's repair shop, which had smelled like solder and machine oil and the particular tea he drank that nobody else liked. She'd grown up in the back room of it, doing homework on a workbench while he dismantled things and put them back together better than they'd started.

He was the one who'd taught her that machines had logic. That you could understand anything if you were willing to be patient with it. That the problem was never the machine — the problem was always the decision about the machine, who built it and why and who benefited from it and who didn't.

He'd taken an augmentation package at fifty-one. Knee joint, nothing dramatic, he'd been in pain for years and the surgery waiting list was eighteen months and the package was available now.

She hadn't argued with him. She'd understood the math.

What she hadn't understood, until it was too late, was the contract language. The ongoing maintenance requirements. The proprietary components that only one service provider could touch. The way a single joint had become a dependency, and the dependency had become leverage, and the leverage had been used — quietly, plausibly, with every individual step documented and legal — to extract three years of contracted consulting work from a fifty-four year old man who'd only wanted his knee to stop hurting.

She'd read the contract after. Every page.

She'd understood every mechanism.

She'd built her first protest sign the week after she finished reading it.

ACT TWO: THE PLANNING

They worked late.

Lyra noticed the moment Remi stopped treating the office like a foreign country she was visiting. It was subtle — she started reaching for things without asking, the spare chair, the better marker, the city map on the wall that she'd been trying not to look too interested in. She started finishing sentences when she and Lyra were talking through logistics, the way people do when they've established that the other person is keeping up.

Max watched this without commenting.

Around nine PM, Remi was cross-legged on the floor with three overlapping maps and Lyra's borrowed laptop and what appeared to be a hand-drawn crowd-flow model that she'd produced from memory in about four minutes. She was explaining ingress patterns to Lyra, who was taking notes, and Lyra was pointing out a variable Remi hadn't accounted for, and Remi was frowning at it and then nodding and incorporating it.

Max, from his desk, said: "The lamp posts at the north and south exits. Are you planning signage?"

"Yes. Why?"

"The junction boxes at both of them are accessible. If someone wanted to interfere with your sound system externally—"

Remi looked up. "They could do it from the lamp posts." She was already picking up her pen. "I didn't think about that. I was thinking about the stage system not the—" She stopped. Looked at the map. Looked at Max. "How do you know where the junction boxes are?"

"I know where everything is," Max said pleasantly. "It's a gift. Or a curse. The jury's still out."

Remi looked at him for a long moment.

"You're not what I expected," she said.

"What did you expect?"

"I don't know. Someone more..." She gestured vaguely.

"Legitimate?"

"Normal."

"Normalcy is deeply overrated." He turned back to his papers. "The junction boxes. I'd recommend having someone you trust positioned near both during the rally."

"I'll add it to the list."

She went back to the maps.

Lyra caught Max's eye over Remi's head. He made a small, minimal expression. See?

Lyra made a smaller, more minimal expression back. Yes, I see. Stop being smug about it.

TAG: REMI ALONE

The walk home was forty minutes. She always walked — it was cheap and she thought better moving.

She went over the rally structure. The ingress changes. The junction box problem and its solution. The way the Agency woman — Lyra — had spotted the flow variable she'd missed without making it a thing.

The Agency itself, strange and small and operating from some logic she hadn't fully mapped yet.

The man behind the desk who said things with complete seriousness that should have been absurd and somehow weren't.

She turned a corner. Passed a block she knew.

Chrome-Headz territory.

She didn't change her pace. She never changed her pace on this block. It was important not to.

The thing she didn't like to examine: she'd never actually had a problem on this block. Five years of crossing it regularly, sometimes late, sometimes carrying equipment, sometimes with coalition members who were visibly nervous, and nothing. Not once.

The Chrome-Headz were visible. They were there, present, the way a neighborhood organization was present. But there was no tax on passing through. No territorial friction. No incidents.

She'd asked someone about it once. A woman who'd lived in the area for twenty years.

That's Crimson's block, the woman had said, as if that explained everything.

And? Remi had said.

The woman had looked at her like she'd asked why the sky was up. And nothing happens to people on Crimson's block.

Remi had wanted to argue. She'd been building an argument about protection rackets and community capture and the way certain kinds of power disguised themselves as neighborhood solidarity.

She hadn't argued.

She didn't know why.

She walked faster.

The rally was in four days. She had a list of things that needed solving. She had, now, people who might help solve them. She had a plan and the plan was good and she was going to focus on that.

She was not going to think about the gang leader she'd seen once at the edge of a protest she'd organized — big, still, watching with the kind of attention that had made her skin prickle in a way she'd diagnosed immediately as political discomfort and had been almost, almost convincing herself of ever since.

She turned onto her street.

"She's going to be something," she'd overheard someone say once, a man's low voice in a conversation she wasn't part of.

She didn't know who'd said it. She'd told herself it wasn't about her. She'd been telling herself that for three weeks.

She went inside.

She went back to work.

The map didn't solve itself.

EPISODE 7.5 — "FREEFALL"

COLD OPEN

The shortcut through Chrome-Head territory took four minutes off her route.

Remi took it anyway, every time, deliberately. It was a small thing. A decision she made with her feet that her brain could interpret as practical rather than what it actually was, which was a refusal to let geography become political surrender.

She had a list. She always had a list. This one was in her head — three things she needed to sort before tomorrow, two calls she needed to make, one supply contact she was going to try again even though he'd gone quiet twice already. She was running the list while she walked, which was why she was almost past the alley mouth before she registered what she was seeing.

She stopped.

Looked.

Later she would try to reconstruct the sequence of images. Her brain, trying to be responsible, kept presenting them in order, each one arriving before she had time to process the previous one:

The boy. Fifteen, maybe sixteen. Slight, the kind of slight that came from years of not quite enough of things. Standing on the edge of the building's roof access platform, which was seven stories up, which Remi registered in the flat, immediate way you registered important numbers.

Crimson.

Standing behind the boy with the absolute stillness that Remi had decided she was not going to think about and had therefore been thinking about constantly. One hand on the boy's shoulder.

And then — without ceremony, without warning, with the shoulder-roll casualness of someone discarding something they were done with —

He threw the boy off the roof.

One-handed.

With a shrug.

The boy fell.

Seven stories of falling, which took longer than Remi's brain wanted it to.

She was already moving toward the building entrance, phone out, because there were things you did when you witnessed something and calling it in was one of them, and she was calculating the angle of impact and whether there was any conceivable way that someone survived a seven-story —

The boy fell past the street level.

Through the service grate. Down into the maintenance tunnel that ran under the block, the one that connected to the old infrastructure, and Remi knew that tunnel, she'd mapped this block, she'd researched this block, and that tunnel led to —

She lost him.

She stood at the edge of the grate and looked down into the dark and heard nothing.

Her hands were shaking.

She looked up at the roof.

Crimson was still standing there. Not looking down. Not alarmed. Not anything. He had the expression of someone who had just set something down in a place they expected it to land and were giving it time to get settled.

And then — this was the part Remi would return to, again and again, the detail that embedded itself like glass — he smiled.

Small. Private. The smile of someone watching something they'd been waiting for.

He turned and walked away from the edge.

Remi stood on the street with her phone in her hand and her list completely gone from her head and something cold and solid taking up the space where her careful policy arguments usually lived.

She told herself: you have a rally to organize.

She told herself: you have three calls to make.

She told herself: this is not your block, you are not responsible for what happens here, you have a documented legal case to build and a permit application to—

From below. From the maintenance tunnel.

A sound.

She'd never heard anything like it. It took her three full seconds to understand what she was hearing — the compressed rush of displaced air, rhythmic, powerful, joyful, building from the tunnel system like something that had been held down finally releasing upward —

The boy came out of the service grate like a rocket.

Not falling. Flying.

Shooting straight up past the street level, past the second floor, the third, the fourth, his arms out, his whole body arranged around this new thing happening to him, this impossible thing, laughing — she could hear him laughing — as he cleared the roof line and kept going, the compressed air system cycling and releasing in controlled bursts, and he wheeled in the air above the building's edge with the specific abandon of someone who has just discovered that the world is larger than they were told.

He came back down in a controlled arc.

Landed on the roof next to where Crimson was standing.

She couldn't hear what they said. She could see the boy's face — the full-body incandescence of it, the way he grabbed Crimson's arm with both hands, the way Crimson put a hand briefly on top of his head in a gesture so unperformed it landed like a fact.

Neither of them looked down.

They didn't know she was there.

Remi stood on the street.

Put her phone away.

Walked to her next appointment, because she had three calls to make and a rally to organize and she knew what she'd seen and she knew what it meant and the timing of it, the ease, the smile — she'd seen his face and she knew what she'd seen.

She knew what she'd seen.

ACT ONE: WHAT SHE DOES WITH IT

The coalition meeting was in Dara's kitchen, which fit eight people comfortably and currently held fourteen.

Remi was the last one in. She'd been the last one in at the last three meetings because she was always coming from somewhere else, always mid-task, always already working before she arrived.

She put her bag down. Looked at the room. Fourteen people who had each found their own path to caring about the same things, which was the miracle of organizing — you never knew what door someone had walked through to get there, just that they'd arrived.

"Update on the permits," she said, because that was always first.

She walked them through it. The stall, the new contacts through the Agency, the revised timeline that was now technically viable if three things broke right. She watched their faces do the math. Some of them needed hope. Some of them needed competence. She gave them both at once when she could.

Then Dara asked: "Are we worried about interference? On the day?"

Remi had been going to say we have contingencies. She'd been going to be measured and tactical and reference the work she'd been doing with the Agency on exactly this question.

What came out instead:

"I saw Crimson today."

The room changed texture. The name did that — not fear exactly, but the particular alertness of people who had opinions and weren't sure if this was the moment to voice them.

"He threw a kid off a roof," Remi said. "Seven stories. Didn't check. Didn't hesitate." She could hear herself. She knew she was speaking too evenly, the tone she used when something was sitting in her chest and she was processing it into language before it was fully processed. "The kid had new legs. New lungs. Chrome augmentation. Crimson just—" she made a gesture. "Like it was nothing. Like the kid was a test. Like his safety was—"

She stopped herself.

The room was very quiet.

"That's why," she said. "That's the thing I keep trying to explain when people ask me why this matters. It's not about the technology. It's never just about the technology. It's about who gets to decide what another person's body is for. Who gets to throw someone off a roof and call it a gift."

She picked up her marker.

"The rally," she said. "Let's talk about the rally."

ACT TWO: WHAT CRIMSON DOESN'T KNOW

Three blocks away, at approximately the same time, the boy — whose name was Theo and who was sixteen and had been in a chair for four years following an accident that the responsible party had settled out of court for an amount that covered the chair but not the living in it — was telling his friend Marcus everything.

He was telling it in the particular breathless mode of people who have just experienced something that language wasn't built to cover.

"He just — you don't get it — he said jump and I said what and he said jump and I looked down and I—" Theo made a sound. "Marcus. Marcus, I flew."

Marcus, seventeen, deeply skeptical of most things and especially things that sounded like they required this level of wide-eyed delivery, said: "You flew."

"I flew. The system, it's — he built something into the — Marcus, I came back up. I went up."

Marcus looked at him for a long time.

"Crimson did this."

"He said it took him three months to get the compression ratios right. Three months." Theo laughed, which was a sound Marcus hadn't heard in four years. "He said I'd need to calibrate for another week before I could go full altitude. He said don't go too high until you know the system. Like I've got ceilings now. Like there are places I shouldn't go yet."

Marcus was quiet.

"He didn't charge you."

"He didn't charge me."

"He didn't—" Marcus stopped. Tried again. "He just did this."

"He said—" Theo's voice shifted, something in it that was trying to be casual and wasn't. "He said he'd been working on it for a while. He said he had the materials. He said—" Pause. "He said a person should be able to go where they want to go."

They sat with that for a moment.

Neither of them was going to cry about it. They were seventeen and sixteen and there were reputations to consider.

"I gotta go back and thank him again," Theo said.

"You already thanked him."

"I know, but—"

"He walked away while you were still talking."

"Yeah." Theo looked at the sky. The specific part of it he'd just come back from. "That's kind of his thing."

ACT THREE: THE AGENCY DOESN'T KNOW EITHER

Lyra found out by accident.

She was on the block the next morning, following up on something unrelated to the rally, and she stopped at Hassan's cart, and Hassan mentioned it the way he mentioned most things — quietly, sideways, as if the information was just part of the air.

"You heard about the Reyes boy?"

"No."

"Theo Reyes. He was on the Díaz block, the one in the chair. His mother used to come past, sometimes I gave her extra pita, she looked tired." He wrapped something, handed it across. "He came by this morning. Walking. Well — not quite walking." Something complicated crossed his face. "Flying, they say."

Lyra put down her coffee.

"He flew?"

"The new system. Air compression, the lungs, I don't know the technical terms." Hassan shook his head slowly. "He came to show his mother. He went up from the street, right outside her window. She was on the third floor." He stopped. "She hasn't been up to the third floor in four years. She always had to send him up."

Lyra stood with this for a moment.

"Who did the augmentation?"

Hassan looked at her.

"Hassan."

"Some things," he said carefully, "you don't say out loud. The person who did it didn't do it for the credit." He turned to the next customer. Then, almost as an aside: "Tell your man Max. Tell him sometimes the neighbourhood does things that don't need managing."

Lyra told Max.

Max sat with it for longer than he usually sat with anything.

"He built a flight system," Max said finally. "Custom. For a specific kid. Three months of development."

"That's what Hassan said."

"Crimson. Who presented himself as a client with a logistical problem." Max turned to look at the window. "Who said nothing about—"

"No."

"Who didn't use it as context or justification or—"

"Nothing."

The window didn't offer anything useful. Max turned back.

"The augmentation Remi's uncle went through," he said slowly. "The package that trapped him. The mechanism she built her entire politics around." He paused. "Crimson knows about that. He knows about this neighborhood. He hears things."

"You think he knows about her uncle specifically."

"I think," Max said carefully, "that a man who spends three months building a flight system for a kid he doesn't charge, in private, with no audience, and then walks away while the kid is still thanking him—" He stopped. "I think he has a very precise understanding of the difference between what augmentation is and what it gets used for."

Lyra thought about the wall in Max's storage room. The floor-to-ceiling trophies nobody was supposed to know about.

"People are more than what they've chosen to become," she said.

Max looked at her.

"You said that," she said. "Two weeks ago. About someone else."

"Did I."

"You did."

He turned back to the files.

"Remi doesn't know about Theo," he said.

"No."

"She's going to go into the rally with whatever she thinks she saw. Whatever interpretation she built."

"Yes."

"We don't tell her."

Lyra frowned. "Why not?"

"Because it's not ours to tell. And because—" Max paused. "Because what she saw wasn't wrong. She looked at a man and read something in him and acted on it. The interpretation was wrong. The instinct wasn't." He picked up a pen. "She's going to have to get there herself. When she's ready."

Lyra thought about a roof, and a boy, and a smile that she hadn't seen but could somehow picture — small, private, the smile of someone watching something fall and knowing it was going to fly.

"He knew she was there," she said.

Max looked up.

"Crimson. The way he hears things — the way he knows things he shouldn't." She met Max's eyes. "He knew she was watching."

Silence.

"Yes," Max said quietly. "I think he probably did."

"Then why didn't he—"

"Because you don't explain yourself to someone who isn't ready to understand the explanation." Max went back to his files. "You just do the work. And you wait."

Lyra sat down at her desk.

Thought about a boy going up through seven stories of air, laughing.

Thought about a woman three floors above her ordinary life, watching her son come back to her from a direction she hadn't known was possible.

Thought about a man who walked away while the thanking was still happening.

She picked up her pen.

She had a rally to help plan.
