EPISODE 6 — "THE CHROME-HEAD"

COLD OPEN

The neighborhood had a language for trouble.

Not words — texture. The way Hassan's cart suddenly had no queue even though the smell of falafel was perfect. The way Dmitri found reasons to wipe the same section of counter three times. The way Mrs. Volkov appeared briefly in her doorway, assessed the street, and went back inside without buying anything from anyone.

Lyra had been learning this language for five months. She was getting conversational.

She looked up from her intake forms.

Two women had come around the corner first.

Women was doing some work as a descriptor. They moved with the particular fluid precision of something engineered rather than grown — not robotic, nothing so crude, but the way they occupied space was too considered. Too perfect. One was dark-haired, the other lighter, both dressed practically in a way that still managed to look like a statement. Their eyes tracked the street in overlapping arcs. Not threatening. Just — comprehensive.

Between them, and slightly behind, was the largest man Lyra had ever seen in a suit that actually fit.

He wasn't rushing. He wasn't performing. He walked like someone who had long since stopped needing to establish anything about himself and was mildly bored by the fact that other people hadn't caught up yet.

He read the alley sign without stopping.

Came inside.

ACT ONE: THE MEETING

The two women took up positions flanking the door with the naturalness of people who had done this ten thousand times and found it neither interesting nor tedious — simply correct. Their eyes moved constantly. Their posture didn't.

The man sat in the client chair.

He looked at Max.

Max looked back.

This lasted four seconds, which was three seconds longer than most people lasted under Max's full attention without filling the silence.

"Coffee?" Max said.

"No."

"We have the good kind. Dmitri's batch, cardamom, the whole thing."

"I'm fine."

Lyra, at her desk, was not taking notes. She was watching the two women by the door in her peripheral vision and thinking about the specific quality of stillness they had — like a blade at rest, like potential energy that had never once been wasted.

"Crimson," Max said. Not a greeting. A confirmation.

"Max." The same register.

Lyra's eyes moved between them. They've met before. Neither of them is going to say how.

"The Agency's services," Crimson said. "I have a situation."

"Most people who find us do." Max leaned back, fingers laced. "You're not most people."

"No."

"Which raises an interesting question about why you're here instead of handling it through your own channels."

Crimson looked at him with the expression of someone watching a chess piece they'd already anticipated move. "Because my channels are the problem. Me being involved makes it worse. I need people who aren't me."

"Tell me about the situation."

He told it plainly. No performance, no framing, no strategic omission that Max could immediately detect — which was itself information. Either Crimson was completely honest by nature or he was good enough at this that the difference didn't matter.

There was a girl. He'd noticed her maybe six weeks ago — a rally outside a chrome-tech distributor, small crowd, hand-painted signs, the kind of protest that got three minutes on a local news feed and was forgotten by morning. He'd been there for entirely unrelated reasons he didn't specify and Max didn't ask about.

"She wasn't the loudest one," Crimson said. "She was the one the loud ones kept turning to when they didn't know what to do next."

"Organizer."

"More than that." He paused. Something almost careful in the pause. "She's running the logistics, the messaging, the permit applications, the coalition outreach. All of it. On her own. With no money and no institutional support."

"Anti-chrome activism," Max said. "Which given your—" he gestured briefly at Crimson's general existence "—is an interesting cause to be invested in."

"I didn't say I agreed with her politics."

"No. You said you noticed her." Max tilted his head. "Keep going."

The situation: the girl — Remi, he said her name once and didn't repeat it — had been planning a rally for three weeks. A real one, proper permits applied for, community support, the kind of thing that had a chance of becoming something. And in the last ten days, the permits had stalled. Her supplier contacts had gone quiet. Two of her coalition partners had pulled out citing vague concerns they couldn't or wouldn't specify.

"Someone's strangling it quietly," Lyra said.

Crimson glanced at her. The first time he'd fully looked at her. Something assessed, concluded, filed. "Yes."

"Corporate?" Max asked.

"Meridian-adjacent." He said it like he was setting something down on the table. Watching where it landed.

Max went very still for exactly one second. "You know about Meridian."

"I know about a lot of things."

"How?"

The first hint of something that might have been dry humor. "Same way I know you had a visitor three weeks ago who left in a hurry. Same way I know the permits on this building got quietly extended six months ago without an application. I hear things."

"People tell you things."

"People tell me things they don't tell other people." He didn't elaborate. He didn't need to. The Chrome-Headz ran four blocks of this city and somehow had no criminal record attached to them as an organization — not because they were careful, Lyra was starting to understand, but because nobody in those four blocks wanted them gone. "The rally isn't the target. Remi isn't the target. The neighborhood is the target. They want an incident. Something messy, something attributable, something that gives them grounds to move in and call it cleanup."

"And they'll attribute the incident to your crew," Max said.

"It's a clean story." No emotion in it. Just the geometry of the thing. "Chrome-head gang disrupts anti-chrome rally. People get hurt. Authorities have documented reason to displace an undesirable element and install private security infrastructure. Meridian gets the foothold they've been trying to buy for eighteen months."

Silence.

Lyra watched Max think. She'd gotten good at reading it — not the visible parts, the stillness underneath. The way his eyes went slightly distant when he was three moves ahead.

"The girl," Max said. "She won't accept your help directly."

"She'd burn the offer and mail me the ashes."

"But she needs help."

"Yes."

"And you want us to provide it without her knowing the client is you."

"I want her protected. I want the rally to happen. I want Meridian's play exposed in public, in front of witnesses, in a way they can't quietly paperwork into nothing." Crimson's voice was level. "How you do that is your business."

Max studied him for a long moment.

"Why?" he said. "The real answer."

Crimson met his eyes. "Because she's going to be something. And I won't let someone else's politics erase that before she gets the chance."

The room was quiet. The two women by the door hadn't moved.

"The ladies," Lyra said, before she could stop herself.

Crimson glanced back. "Sable. Luna."

The dark-haired one — Luna — angled her chin slightly. The other didn't move at all.

"They go where I go," Crimson said simply.

"And if I need to reach you?" Max asked.

"You won't need to reach me. I'll know if something's moving." He stood. Straightened his jacket. "One other thing."

"Yes?"

"The last time our paths crossed." He looked at Max with the particular directness of someone who didn't bother with indirection because they'd never found it necessary. "I didn't like how it ended."

"Agreed."

"This arrangement doesn't change that."

"Also agreed." Max's mouth curved slightly. "It just means we're professionals."

Crimson considered this. Nodded once.

He left. Sable and Luna flowed out after him with that same engineered grace, and the room felt suddenly larger than it had.

Lyra let out a breath she'd been holding.

"What happened. Last time."

"Unrelated to this."

"That's not what I asked."

Max looked at her. Looked away. Picked up his coffee. "He was right and I was wrong about something and it cost us both time we didn't have. The details aren't relevant."

"You said agreed when he said he didn't like how it ended. You never agree with people that quickly."

"I agree with people when they're correct."

"You argue with people when they're correct. It's your primary mode." Lyra turned back to her desk. "What are those two?"

"Excuse me?"

"Sable. Luna. What are they?"

A pause. "Automatons. Combat grade, from the look of the movement patterns. Someone built them very carefully." Another pause. "Someone built them to appear very carefully."

"Appear?"

"They weren't just standing there. They were cataloguing. The room, the exits, the filing cabinet—" Max stopped.

"They looked at the filing cabinet?"

"Sable did. For exactly 1.3 seconds."

Lyra absorbed this.

"So Crimson walks in here with two highly sophisticated combat AIs flanking him, claims he wants to protect a girl who hates everything he represents, knows about Meridian, knows about your building's permits, and knows about your visitor three weeks ago." She paused. "And you took the case."

"Of course."

"Why?"

Max picked up the new file. Began writing. "Because he told the truth. All of it. Including the parts that made him look interested in someone he has no obvious reason to be interested in." He looked up. "Dishonest people edit. They leave out the things that reveal them." He paused. "Crimson didn't leave anything out."

Lyra thought about the way he'd said she's going to be something. The precise, unembellished certainty of it.

"No," she said. "He didn't."
