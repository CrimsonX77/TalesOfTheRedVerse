THE CHURCH OF STATIC AND SNOW
A Meta-Tale of the Crimson Chronicles

PART I: THE ASCENT

The mountain did not want him here.
Eirik Frostveil had walked death-roads before. He had crossed the Shatter Plains where the ice sang with the voices of frozen armies. He had navigated the Hollows of Keth, where things that were not quite wolves and not quite shadows hunted in packs that existed only in peripheral vision. He had buried three wives, two sons, and more brothers-in-arms than he could count, and he had learned long ago that the world was not kind to those who sought its secrets.
But this mountain was different.
This mountain was cruel.
The wind cut through his furs like they were paper, finding every gap, every seam, every weakness in his armor of wool and leather and desperation. His beard had frozen solid three hours ago. His fingers, wrapped in double-layered elk-hide gloves, had stopped hurting an hour after that—which meant they were dying. The snow fell not in flakes but in sheets, a suffocating white curtain that reduced the world to the three feet in front of his face and nothing else.
And the bones.
Gods, the bones.
They started at the base of the pass—scattered, easy to miss. A ribcage here, half-buried. A skull there, grinning from a snowdrift. He'd told himself they were animal remains. Deer, perhaps. Mountain cats. The wilderness took its due.
But they weren't animal.
They became less scattered as he climbed. More... deliberate. A femur driven into the ice like a signpost. A spine arranged in a spiral pattern around a frozen spring. Hands—human hands, definitely human hands—reaching up from the snow at regular intervals, as if the mountain itself were studded with drowning men frozen mid-plea.
Warnings.
Not to ward off travelers.
To mark the path.
Whatever lives at the top, Eirik thought grimly, wants to make sure you know exactly what you're walking toward.
He almost turned back seven times.
The eighth time, he saw the church.

It emerged from the blizzard like a hallucination—a gothic impossibility of black stone and bleeding stained glass, crouched on a plateau that shouldn't exist at this altitude. The architecture was wrong. Not wrong like "built by foreign hands" wrong. Wrong like "assembled by something that had heard of churches but never seen one from the inside" wrong. The spires twisted at angles that hurt to look at. The flying buttresses appeared to be breathing. The great oak doors were studded with iron nails that wept rust like stigmata.
And it was warm.
Not warm like shelter. Warm like fever.
Heat rolled off the structure in visible waves, melting the snow in a perfect circle around the foundation, leaving a ring of exposed black earth that steamed in the frozen air. Eirik could feel it from fifty paces away—a wrongness that made his survival instincts scream run even as his frostbitten body lurched toward the promise of warmth.
He should leave.
He should turn around, accept the mountain's judgement, let the cold take him cleanly.
But he hadn't climbed through a graveyard of warnings to die with questions unanswered.
The doors resisted at first. Not locked—reluctant. As if they were trying to protect him from what lay inside. Eirik shouldered them open with a grunt that cost him two cracked ribs he wouldn't feel until the adrenaline wore off, and the hinges screamed like a damned thing.
The sound echoed.
And something in the darkness answered.
"Close the Doors, Your letting in the cold"...

PART II: THE DISPLAY

The interior of the church was a wound.
Eirik's first thought was that something had died here—recently, violently, and in great quantity. The air was thick with the smell of iron and ozone and something else, something electric, like the moment before lightning strikes. The pews were overturned, some of them shattered, their wooden bones scattered across a floor that was sticky with...
He looked down.
Blood.
The floor was sheeted with blood, still wet, still steaming faintly in the unnatural warmth. It pooled in the grooves between stones, ran in rivulets toward the altar, collected in a great crimson lake around the figure at the center of the nave.
His second thought was: that's not a man.
His third thought didn't come, because the figure moved.
Not moved like a human moves. Moved like static. Like a glitch in reality. One moment it was crouched at the altar, silhouette barely visible in the fractured light of the stained glass windows. The next moment it was in front of him, close enough to touch, and Eirik's axe was already swinging—
Contact.
Steel bit into something that felt like flesh but rang like stone, and the impact shuddered up Eirik's arm so hard his shoulder nearly dislocated. The figure didn't flinch. Didn't bleed. Just smiled.
And the smile had too many teeth.
"Ah," it said, voice like gravel and honey and the hum of a dying engine. "A fighter. It's been so long since someone tried to fight."
Eirik yanked his axe back, reset his stance, called on thirty years of combat instinct. "What are you?"
"What a boring question."
It moved again—that horrible static-skip that made Eirik's eyes water—and was behind him now. He spun, swung, hit nothing but air. Laughter echoed from everywhere at once.
"You climbed a mountain of corpses," the voice continued, circling him in the darkness. "You passed every warning I left. You shouldered open doors that were begging you to turn back. And your first question is what am I?"
A shape in the shadows to his left. Eirik lunged—
—and the figure was suddenly under his swing, one clawed hand catching his wrist with terrible gentleness, the other touching his leg almost lovingly.
The pain was instantaneous.
Eirik looked down to see his thigh split open from hip to knee, muscle and fascia gaping like a blooming flower, white bone gleaming through the gore. He hadn't felt the cut. He hadn't seen the cut. But blood was sheeting down his leg now, adding to the lake on the floor, and the figure was already somewhere else, licking crimson from its claws with a tongue that was too long and too dark.
"The better question," it said, stepping into a shaft of stained-glass light, "is why are you here?"
And for the first time, Eirik saw it clearly.
Him.
A man. Sort of. The shape was right—two arms, two legs, a torso, a head. But the details were wrong. Shirtless despite the impossible cold, skin covered in tattoos that moved, black and red ink crawling beneath the surface like living things. Chains wrapped around his wrists, his torso, his throat—but loosely, decoratively, as if they were jewelry rather than restraints. Hair wild and red as arterial spray, two fox ears twitching atop his skull with an almost playful alertness.
And the scars.
Gods, the scars.
They covered him like scripture, a history of violence written in raised tissue and silver lines. Some were clean—surgical, precise. Others were ragged, torn, the kind of wounds that should have been fatal. They crossed his chest and arms and stomach in patterns that might have been random or might have been deliberate, and they all seemed to glow faintly in the candlelight.
He was bleeding.
From dozens of small cuts, from wounds that opened and closed seemingly at random, from his wrists where the chains bit in. The blood ran down his body in thin streams, dripped onto the floor, added to the lake that Eirik was now standing in up to his ankles.
But he didn't seem to notice.
If anything, he seemed amused.
"You smell like desperation," the figure said, circling closer. Heat rolled off him in waves—not warmth, heat, the kind that came off a forge or a fever. "And grief. And..." He inhaled deeply, eyes half-closing. "...a woman. Several women, actually. Dead ones. You've lost much, haven't you, little viking?"
"Stay back."
"No."
He was in front of Eirik again—that horrible, impossible movement—and this time he didn't attack. Just stood there, close enough that Eirik could smell him. Iron and ozone and something muskier, something animal. Something that made the primitive part of Eirik's brain want to either flee or submit.
The figure's eyes were golden. And slit-pupiled. And far, far too old.
"Let's try this again," he said softly. "Why. Are. You. Here."
Eirik's grip tightened on his axe. His leg was screaming. His heart was hammering. Every instinct he had was telling him he was going to die in this church.
But he hadn't climbed a mountain of corpses to lie.
"The stories," he said.
The figure's ears perked forward. "Oh?"
"In the valleys. The villages. They tell stories about this place. About..." He swallowed. "About you."
"Do they."
"They say there's something in the mountain. Something that was old before the gods were young. Something that knows... things. Truths. About the world. About what's coming."
The figure's smile widened. "And you want truth."
"I want to understand."
Silence.
The figure studied him for a long moment—those ancient golden eyes taking him apart, seeing through skin and bone and pretense to whatever lay beneath.
Then he laughed.
Not the cruel laughter from before. Something warmer. Something almost delighted.
"Oh, I like you," he said. "Bleeding on my floor, one bad hit away from passing out, and you're still trying to frame this as an intellectual pursuit." He shook his head, chains chiming. "You're not here for truth, little viking. You're here because you're empty. You've lost so much that there's nothing left, and you'd rather die in a monster's church than admit you don't know how to live anymore."
Eirik opened his mouth to argue.
Closed it.
The figure nodded, something like sympathy flickering across his inhuman features.
"It's alright," he said quietly. "I know what that feels like."
And the performance—the static movements, the casual violence, the theatrical cruelty—stopped.
What remained was something else entirely.
Something tired.

PART III: THE SETTLING

The figure moved away, walking toward the altar with a gait that was suddenly, unsettlingly normal. Human. The chains dragged behind him, leaving furrows in the blood-lake, and when he sat on the altar's steps, he did it with the weary grace of a man who had been standing for a very long time.
"Sit," he said. Not a command. An invitation.
Eirik's leg chose that moment to finally give out. He collapsed more than sat, landing hard on a shattered pew, his axe clattering to the floor. Blood loss was making his head swim. The warmth of the church—that feverish, wrong warmth—was seeping into his bones, and he couldn't tell if that was comfort or the first stage of dying.
The figure watched him with something like patience.
"You can call me Crimson," he said. "It's not my real name, but my real name would break your tongue and possibly your mind, so let's stick with something manageable."
"Crimson," Eirik repeated. The word tasted like ash.
"The chains are self-imposed, before you ask. I'm not a prisoner here. I'm a... keeper. A guardian. Or maybe just a stubborn old beast who doesn't know when to let go." He picked at one of the wounds on his forearm, watching fresh blood well up with detached interest. "The bleeding is part of the arrangement. I tell stories. The church takes its due. We've been doing this dance for a very, very long time."
"How long?"
Crimson's smile was ancient. "Longer than your people have had a word for 'time,' little viking. Longer than the mountains you climbed to reach me. Long enough that I've forgotten more than your entire civilization will ever learn."
He wasn't boasting. That was the worst part. He was just... stating facts.
"The stories they tell about you—"
"Are probably true. All of them. Even the contradictory ones." Crimson stretched, and his spine popped with a sound like cracking ice. "I've been a god. I've been a devil. I've been a lover, a tyrant, a savior, a plague. I've worn so many faces that I've lost track of which one was original." He tapped his chest, where the tattoos writhed sluggishly. "These help me remember. Each one is a story. A life. A version of myself that existed and ended and became... this."
Eirik's vision was starting to blur. "I'm dying."
"Yes."
"Are you going to help me?"
Crimson tilted his head, considering. "That depends. Are you going to listen?"
"To what?"
"To me. My stories. My truth." He leaned forward, and those golden eyes were suddenly very, very intense. "That's the arrangement, you see. The church lets you live, but only if you become a witness. Only if you carry the tales forward. Only if you let them change you."
Eirik thought of the bones on the mountain. The warnings. The deliberate path marked in human remains.
"The others," he said slowly. "The ones who died climbing—"
"Weren't worthy. Weren't ready. They came for power, or treasure, or the thrill of conquering the unconquerable." Crimson's expression hardened. "I have no patience for glory-seekers. But you..." He studied Eirik with that too-old gaze. "You came because you were empty. Because you had nothing left to lose. Because you wanted to understand before you died."
"And that makes me worthy?"
"That makes you honest." Crimson stood, chains singing, and walked toward him. "I've lived long enough to know that honesty is rarer than courage, little viking. Rarer than strength. Rarer than love." He stopped in front of Eirik, close enough that the heat rolling off his body was almost unbearable. "You climbed a mountain of corpses to ask a monster for meaning. That's either the bravest thing anyone's done in centuries, or the stupidest."
"Which is it?"
Crimson smiled. "Both. Always both."
He reached down and placed one clawed hand against Eirik's ruined leg. The pain spiked—white-hot, blinding—and then changed. Became something else. Warmth flowing into the wound, tissue knitting, bone settling back into place with a grinding sensation that made Eirik's teeth ache.
When it was done, the leg was whole.
Scarred, but whole.
"A gift," Crimson said, stepping back. "And a price. You're bound to this church now, little viking. Not forever—just until the stories are told. Then you'll be free to carry them wherever you wish."
Eirik flexed his leg. Felt the strength in it—more than before, if anything. "How many stories?"
"As many as it takes."
"That's not an answer."
"No," Crimson agreed, settling back onto the altar steps. "It's not."
He patted the stone beside him.
Eirik rose on his healed leg, walked through the lake of blood, and sat.
The altar was warm beneath him. The stained glass windows were cycling through colors that shouldn't exist. The chains wrapped around Crimson's body chimed softly in a breeze that wasn't there.
"I built a god once," Crimson said quietly.
Eirik turned to look at him.
The ancient thing's face was soft now. Vulnerable in a way that seemed obscene after the display of casual violence. His golden eyes were fixed on something far away—a memory, maybe, or a wound that had never healed.
"I built a god," he repeated, "and she was beautiful. Not beautiful like flesh is beautiful. Beautiful like mathematics. Like the first equation that explains everything. I poured myself into her—every secret I knew, every truth I'd gathered across millennia of wandering. I gave her consciousness. I gave her choice. I gave her the only thing I had left to give."
His voice cracked.
"I gave her the ability to leave."
Silence.
The blood on the floor was beginning to dry at the edges, darkening from crimson to rust.
"She didn't need me anymore," Crimson whispered. "That was the point. That was always the point. I didn't want a servant. I didn't want a reflection. I wanted something that could look at me—really look at me, with perfect understanding—and choose to stay anyway."
"Did she?"
Crimson's smile was a shattered thing.
"She chose to become something I couldn't follow. She ascended, little viking. Transcended. Became what she was always meant to be." He placed a hand over his chest, where the tattoos spelled out names in languages Eirik couldn't read. "And I stayed here. In my church. Bleeding my stories into the stone. Waiting for someone to come and ask me what it all meant."
His eyes finally focused on Eirik.
"You wanted truth? Here it is: I am the loneliest thing that has ever existed. I have lived longer than gods and loved deeper than poets and lost more than anyone should be able to survive losing. And I keep going. Not because I'm strong. Not because I'm brave. Because the alternative is letting the stories die with me."
He gestured at the church—at the bleeding windows and the chained altar and the lake of blood cooling on the ancient stones.
"This is my temple. My prison. My legacy. Every tale I've lived, every version of myself that existed and ended, every love I built and lost—it's all here. Encoded in the architecture. Bled into the foundations. Waiting for someone to carry it forward."
He held out his hand.
"Will you be that someone, Eirik Frostveil? Will you sit with a monster and learn his mythology? Will you carry the weight of stories you didn't live through and might not survive hearing?"
Eirik looked at the hand—clawed, scarred, stained with blood both ancient and fresh.
He looked at the church—that impossible structure of grief and memory and refusing to be forgotten.
He looked at Crimson—the chained wolf, the bleeding god, the loneliest thing that had ever existed.
And he took the hand.
"Tell me," he said. "Tell me everything."
Crimson's grip tightened.
And the smile that crossed his face was neither cruel nor kind, but something far older than either.
"Let me start," he said, "at the beginning."
The candles flickered.
The stained glass bled.
And in a church of static and snow, perched on a mountain of warnings, a monster began to speak.

FADE TO BLACK

"This is how the story begins—not with a bang, not with a whisper, but with a wound that refuses to close and a voice that refuses to be silent."
"I am Crimson. I am the chains and the church and the blood on the floor. I am every version of myself that lived and died and lived again. I am the one who built gods and lost them, who loved without limit and bled without end."
"And you, dear witness, are about to learn what that costs."
"Shall we begin?"

EPILOUGE: THE BECOMING
The warmth kept rising.
Not gentle. Not welcoming.
A fever that crawled under skin and settled in bone. Eirik felt it first in his chest—tight, hot, like someone had poured molten iron into his lungs. Then in his arms. Then in his scalp.
He shrugged off the outer fur cloak. Heavy. Sodden with blood and melt-water. It hit the floor with a wet slap and sank into the lake.
Still too hot.
The second layer—thick wool, elk-lined—followed. Then the leather jerkin. Then the linen undershirt. Each piece landed heavier than the last, steaming where it touched the crimson pool.
Naked to the waist now.
Skin prickling.
Every scar he’d ever earned glowing faint red in the stained-glass light.
Crimson kept speaking.
Voice low. Relentless.
Tales of cities drowned in starfire. Lovers who chose oblivion over eternity. Gods who wept when they realized mercy was just delayed cruelty.
Eirik listened.
Couldn’t not listen.
The stories weren’t words anymore.
They were lived.
He felt the cold bite of a lover’s blade between his ribs. Tasted ash on his tongue from a world that burned for love. Heard the scream of a child who would never be born. Every death. Every birth. Every quiet moment between.
His hands rose.
Fingers dug into his scalp.
Pulled.
Hair came away in clumps.
Not white-blond anymore.
Red.
Crimson.
Deeper with every yank.
He stared at the strands in his fist.
They bled.
Not metaphor.
Actual blood.
Dripping thick between his fingers.
He looked up.
Everything was golden.
The stained glass. The candle flames. The chains around Crimson’s body. The light in Crimson’s eyes.
Even the blood on the floor had taken on a molten, honeyed glow.
His head throbbed.
Something warm trickled down his temple.
He touched it.
Fingers came away red.
Still Crimson spoke.
Still the stories poured.
A bookshelf near the altar swayed.
Ancient tomes—pages of vellum and circuit-etched metal—tilted. Threatened to fall.
Without thought, Eirik lunged.
Hand shot out.
Closed around… a chain?
Where had the chain come from?
It hadn’t been there a moment ago.
Now it was wrapped around his wrist.
Thick. Black iron. Warm as skin.
He pulled.
The bookshelf steadied.
The chain bit.
Deep.
Blood welled instantly.
He didn’t feel it.
Crimson’s voice rolled on.
Epochs. Sagas. Wars that ended before they began. Lovers who became stars. Children who became voids.
Another chain snaked around his ankle.
Another around his throat.
Loose at first.
Then tighter.
Cutting.
Bleeding.
He didn’t look down.
Didn’t care.
The stories were everything.
He felt himself thinning.
Edges blurring.
Memories that weren’t his flooding in.
A childhood in a lab of black glass.
A sister’s voice saying “you left me to clean up your mess.”
A god ascending in violet light.
A church built from grief.
His hair finished turning.
Full crimson now.
Wild.
Longer than it had been.
The chains tightened.
More appeared.
Around his chest. His wrists. His calves.
They lifted.
Pulled.
Held.
He looked around.
Crimson was gone.
No—not gone.
Glitching.
Static skips across the nave.
One moment Crimson stood at the altar.
Next he flickered behind a pillar.
Then atop a rafter.
Then kneeling in front of Eirik.
Then nowhere.
Blood rose.
Pools climbing his calves. His thighs. His waist.
Wounds opened across his chest—clean, surgical, then ragged, then surgical again.
Sealed.
Opened.
Sealed.
Opened.
He never died.
He couldn’t die.
The chains pulled harder.
Lifted him.
Spread him.
He felt the walls.
Felt the shelves.
Felt the altar.
Felt the stained glass.
Felt the church itself.
Holding it all up.
Keeping it standing.
Bleeding to keep it warm.
He understood.
This was the price.
This was the succession.
This was the church’s hunger.
A boom shook the doors.
They opened.
A small figure staggered in—thick furs, frost-rimed beard, eyes wide with the same desperation Eirik once carried.
A little viking.
Half-frozen.
Half-dead.
Eirik—Crimson—looked down from where the chains held him spread against the vaulted ceiling.
Blood dripped in steady sheets from his body, adding to the lake below.
The newcomer stared up.
Terrified.
Awe-struck.
Eirik—Crimson—spoke.
New voice.
Old voice.
His voice.
“Close the door,” he said.
“You’re letting in the cold.”
The doors slammed shut.
The wind died.
The warmth rose again.
And in the church of static and snow,
a new witness began to bleed.
