Chapter One: Before Morning

She woke to the boom.

Not gradually — one moment nothing, the next her body already sitting upright on the mattress, heart already running, the sound still reverberating in the walls of the small room like it couldn't find a way out. Outside the window the sky was the wrong colour. Orange where it should have been black.

She was pulling on her boots before she was fully awake.

The street outside was chaos in the specific way that chaos always starts — not everyone running yet, just the ones who already knew. Mrs. Yelen from the corner unit standing in her doorway with both hands over her mouth looking east. Two kids from the lower block sprinting past without shoes. The smell of the composite burning reaching her before the sound did, which meant the wind was coming from the north and the fire was big enough to have its own weather.

Mara found her at the junction.

Or Mara ran into her. Hard to say which. She came around the corner fast with her hair still half-braided and grabbed Lyra's arm with both hands and her eyes were doing the thing they did — too wide, showing white at the edges, the expression she got when her brain was running faster than her mouth could keep up with.

"East block," she managed. "They came in through the east block."

"How many."

"I don't— a lot. Lyra they're—"

Another boom. Closer. They both flinched toward each other without deciding to.

"Come on," Lyra said.

They moved west, away from the orange, which was the right direction until it wasn't — until they came around the long curve of the old maintenance road and saw the group of them maybe two hundred metres ahead and had to pull up hard against the shadow of the wall.

Seven. Eight. Hard to count because they weren't standing still.

Raiders was the word her brain supplied and it was accurate enough. Not military — nothing that organised. No matching kit, no formation discipline. They moved in a loose churning mass through the street, and the sound they made was the thing that lodged itself somewhere uncomfortable in her chest — not battle sounds exactly, more like celebration. Whooping. Laughter that had too much edge on it. One of them discharged a weapon at nothing in particular and the others thought this was genuinely funny.

Whatever they were running on had burned the caution out of them entirely.

Mara pressed close. "We have to go back."

"We can't go back."

The east was orange. The west was them. North was the old quarter, the stone buildings, the storage blocks that nobody used anymore because the access road had collapsed two winters ago and nobody had fixed it because nobody fixed anything anymore.

"North," Lyra said.

They ran low and close to the walls the way you learn to when running is the only option and being seen is the worse problem. Past the burnt-out shell of the charging station. Through the gap in the collapsed fence that everyone used as a shortcut. Across the open ground of the old yard which was thirty metres of pure exposure and felt like three hundred.

That was where she saw him.

Or saw his back.

A man coming the other way — from the north, moving fast but not panicked, the controlled movement of someone who'd already made calculations she hadn't caught up to yet. He cut across the yard at an angle that brought him briefly close, close enough that she registered the width of his shoulders, the fact that he wasn't wearing a shirt despite everything, and the ink — tattoos that covered his back in patterns she didn't have time to read, dark and elaborate against skin that was sheened with sweat and orange light.

He didn't look at them.

They didn't look at him.

Everyone had somewhere to be.

He was gone before she'd fully registered him, swallowed by a side alley heading east, which was the wrong direction, which was his problem.

"There," Mara said, pointing.

The storage block. Old stone. Door hanging open, dark inside.

They ran.

Twelve of them ended up in the room.

She didn't know all their names. Petr she knew — old, bad knee, had lived in the settlement longer than anyone and had opinions about it. The two boys from the lower block who'd been running without shoes, who turned out to be brothers and whose names she didn't catch because they gave them at the same time. A woman with a baby who hadn't made a sound yet and who everyone in the room was quietly praying would continue not making a sound.

They pulled the door closed. Let the dark settle.

For a while nobody said anything because there was nothing useful to say and everyone understood this instinctively.

Then Petr, very quietly: "We wait."

Someone else: "For what?"

Petr considered this with the seriousness it deserved. "Morning," he said finally. "Things are always different in the morning."

Nobody argued. It was the kind of logic that didn't hold up to examination so nobody examined it.

Mara found Lyra's hand in the dark. She let her keep it.

The sounds came and went outside. The raider noise — the whooping, the occasional discharge — moving through the settlement in waves, getting louder then fading then louder again as they swept through the streets in their loose chemical momentum. Once, close enough to make the baby stir, a burst of laughter that went on too long and then stopped too suddenly.

The woman with the baby did something with her hand and the baby made a small sound and then didn't make another one.

Torchlight under the door. Sweeping past. Gone.

Then again. Slower.

Voices outside that Lyra couldn't make out — the words lost through old stone but the tone readable. The administrative tone of men checking boxes. A question. An answer. Footsteps moving away.

She let out a breath she'd been holding since the torchlight.

Then the fighting started.

It was around the corner — close enough to hear clearly, far enough that she couldn't see it through the gap at the bottom of the door. She pressed her eye there anyway from instinct and got a narrow slice of orange-lit gravel and nothing else.

The sounds were specific. Impact sounds. The kind that have weight behind them.

What was strange was that it kept going.

One man against several — she could tell from the distribution of sound, the way the impacts came from multiple angles — and the smart move, the survival move, was to go down early and stay there. Let them think they'd won. Most people understood this.

Whoever was out there didn't seem to.

He kept getting up. She could tell from the rhythm of it. Down, then the scrape of boots on gravel that meant upright again, then the sound of him making them regret it briefly before the numbers caught up.

The raiders loved it. The whooping had taken on a different quality — appreciation, almost. The sound of men watching something that surprised them.

Then gravel. Silence.

"—the hell is wrong with you." Raider voice. Genuinely entertained.

A pause. Then him, breathing audibly even through the stone: "Noticed that."

Laughter. The unhinged kind.

"Seven guns on you and you're smiling. You're actually smiling."

"Hurts less than it looks."

More laughter.

Lyra shifted to find a better angle on the gap. Found one. A narrow slice of the alley came into view — gravel, boots, the edge of someone kneeling.

The tattoos.

The same ones. Same back, same ink, now with blood tracking down between the shoulder blades from somewhere above. He was on his knees in the gravel with his hands relaxed at his sides — not surrendered exactly, more like a man taking a brief rest before deciding what to do next — and the smirk she could see from the angle of his jaw was the most ill-advised expression she'd ever seen on someone in his position.

A man stepped into her narrow view. Bigger than the others. The way he moved through the group made the hierarchy obvious without needing any other signal.

He looked down at the kneeling man for a moment.

Then he started laughing. Properly. Something about the whole situation apparently delighting him on a level the others hadn't reached yet.

"I want him," he said, to no one in particular.

"He bit Farro," someone offered.

"I want him more." He crouched down to eye level. "You. You're either the bravest man in this settlement or the stupidest one."

"Haven't decided yet," the kneeling man said.

The leader studied him. Then stood. Extended a hand — not helping him up, just indicating the option existed.

The kneeling man looked at the hand.

Then he moved — fast and wrong, not taking the hand but going for something else, a last calculation that didn't quite land, and then he was back down in the gravel and the laughter was louder this time and the leader was shaking his head with the expression of a man whose opinion had been confirmed rather than changed.

"Done?" the leader said.

A beat.

"Yeah," he said into the gravel. "Done."

"Good." The leader straightened. Looked around at his group with satisfaction. "He's coming with us."

Boots. Movement. The sound of him being hauled upright — he didn't help but he didn't resist either, the specific cooperation of a man conserving what he had left.

The leader's voice again, almost as an afterthought, bright with the chemical cheerfulness that hadn't left him all night:

"Anyone here you want to save? Anyone at all? Last chance to be a hero."

The tone of it sat wrong. A trap wearing the shape of generosity.

The tattooed man turned.

Not toward the leader. Not toward the group. He turned slowly, still held by two of them, and his eyes moved across the settlement — the burning east, the dark west, the old stone north — with the unhurried patience of a man who was looking for something specific.

His eyes found the door.

Found the gap.

Found her eye in it.

She didn't breathe.

"Nah," he said. Easy. Like the question had been genuinely considered and found uninteresting. "Fuck it. Let's go."

And he winked.

The same way — she understood this later, sitting with it while the sound of boots faded east and the orange sky began its slow shift toward grey — the same way you wink at someone when you're telling them I got this. Stay down. The same way you wink when the gun is at your head and you've already made the decision and you just need them to know it's okay.

Like it was nothing.

Like she was worth protecting for no reason at all.

The boots faded. The orange faded. The sounds of the settlement settling into the specific quiet of aftermath — things still burning but no longer being fed, the chemistry of the raiders wearing off somewhere to the east, the occasional distant sound that she stopped trying to interpret.

Petr exhaled beside her. Long and slow and ancient.

The baby made a small sound. Then stopped.

Nobody spoke.

They waited for morning.

Petr was right, as it turned out.

Things were different in the morning. Not better — the east block was gone, and three people from the settlement weren't in the gathering at the junction, and Mrs. Yelen had taken a burn to her arm that someone wrapped with material torn from a shirt while she held very still and looked at nothing. The access road was worse than it had been. The charging station was a shell.

Different, though. The orange was gone. The sky was the colour it was supposed to be.

Lyra stood at the edge of the gathering and looked east for a while.

She didn't know his name. Didn't know where he'd come from or what he'd been doing in the settlement or what working for the raiders actually meant for someone who'd just agreed to it at gunpoint with blood on his back and a smirk that had no business being there.

She knew the tattoos. She knew the wink.

She knew that when the question had been asked — anyone you want to save — he hadn't hesitated the way a man hesitates when he's calculating. He'd hesitated the way a man hesitates when he's making sure the answer lands right.

She filed it away in the place where things go that don't have a category yet.

Then she went to help with Mrs. Yelen's arm.

End of Chapter One.

Chapter Two: Reasonable Rates

The client had been talking for eleven minutes before Crimson had stopped listening.

This wasn't unprofessional. This was adaptive. There was a version of listening that processed information and a version that maintained the appearance of processing information while the actual cognitive resources went somewhere more useful, and after four years of this work Crimson had developed the second version into something close to an art form.

He drove. The client talked.

"—because the east approach is obviously compromised, I told my contact specifically, I said specifically, that the east approach—"

The east approach was fine. Crimson had checked it forty minutes before picking the client up, which was forty minutes before the client had apparently decided the east approach was compromised, which meant the client's intelligence was either forty minutes old or completely fabricated by a nervous system currently running on what Crimson conservatively estimated was three hits of Klaxons on top of whatever the man's baseline anxiety looked like without chemical assistance.

The Klaxons explained the hands. Both of them moving constantly, independent of each other, like they were having a separate conversation the rest of the client's body wasn't invited to.

"—so I'm thinking we go north first, loop around, come in from the industrial side which they won't be watching because nobody uses the industrial—"

"Nobody uses the industrial access because the road collapsed," Crimson said. "Two winters ago."

A pause. The hands kept moving.

"Right," the client said. "Right, okay, so then south—"

"We're going east."

"But I just said—"

"East is clear," Crimson said. "I checked."

"When?"

"This morning."

"That was hours ago, things change, you have to understand that things—"

"East," Crimson said.

The client made a sound that wasn't quite agreement and wasn't quite argument and contained elements of both, which was apparently what Klaxons did to the part of the brain responsible for completing thoughts. He'd had clients on it before. The military variant — the real compound, the one that had come out of the border conflicts fifteen years ago — that stuff turned ordinary soldiers into something that didn't have a clean name yet. Fast and decisive and operating on a threat-assessment bandwidth that normal human nervous systems simply didn't have access to.

The street variant was a different proposition entirely.

The street variant took whatever was already running in a person's head and turned the volume up without improving the signal. Paranoid people became extremely paranoid. Anxious people became comprehensively anxious. People who were already convinced that every variable in a situation required equal urgent personal attention became — he glanced at the client, who was now checking the wing mirror for the fourth time in two minutes — this.

"The contact confirmed noon," the client said. "Did I tell you noon? It's noon, it has to be noon, if it's not noon—"

"It'll be noon," Crimson said.

"How can you be sure—"

"Because it's eleven forty-three and we're four minutes away."

The hands moved. "Right. Right okay. And you've done this before."

"Yes."

"This specific exchange."

"Similar ones."

"But not this one."

Crimson looked at the road. "Not this exact one, no."

"Okay," the client said, in the tone of a man filing information he found deeply unsatisfying. "Okay. And the arm — does the arm — I mean I'm not trying to be—"

"The arm works fine."

"Right. Right of course. I just meant—"

"It works fine."

The client nodded several times, which seemed to use up whatever he'd been about to say next. Outside the window the city moved past in its usual state of managed deterioration — the factory line everything, grey and identical and functional in the way that things are functional when nobody has thought about them beyond the minimum required for basic operation. A charging station with two of its four ports dead. A block of housing units where someone had painted something on the end wall in red that the maintenance crews hadn't gotten to yet. A man selling something from a crate at a junction who watched the vehicle pass with the professional disinterest of someone who'd learned not to clock faces.

The arm rested on the door.

People always asked about the arm eventually. They tried not to, which meant they thought about it more than people who would have just asked directly. The client had lasted eleven minutes and forty-odd seconds of pre-exchange briefing plus four minutes of driving before the compulsion had beaten the politeness, which was actually above average.

The arm was custom. He knew what custom meant in this city, this era, this supply-chain-fractured present tense where factory line was what you got if you were lucky and you got what you were given and you were grateful for it. Custom meant someone had sourced components that weren't on the standard allocation. Custom meant connections or money or the specific kind of leverage that didn't get talked about in polite company.

He was not obviously rich. He was not obviously connected. He was a man who drove clients to exchanges for reasonable rates and made sure they came back alive and didn't ask what was in the box.

The arm attracted attention he had stopped explaining years ago.

"Here," he said, and pulled over.

The exchange was in a loading bay off a service road — the kind of location that appeared on no official map and existed in the city's memory as a place things happened that didn't require documentation. Three vehicles already there. Six men visible, probably two more he couldn't see because there were always two more he couldn't see.

His contact was there. The client's contact was there. The thing being exchanged was presumably there in one of the cases sitting on the composite floor of the loading bay, equidistant between both parties in the way that things are placed when nobody trusts anybody but everybody wants the thing badly enough to show up anyway.

Crimson parked. Assessed. Ran the geometry of exit options in the background of his attention the way he always did now, the way breathing worked, without having to think about it.

"Okay," the client said. "Okay so I go in and I—"

"You let them talk first," Crimson said. "You don't introduce the timeline. You don't mention the east approach. You don't tell them about the industrial road."

"I wasn't going to mention the—"

"You let them set the pace and you confirm and you come back to the vehicle."

"And if they—"

"Come back to the vehicle."

The client looked at him. The Klaxons were doing something to his pupils that made direct eye contact an interesting experience. "You make it sound simple."

"It is simple," Crimson said. "Go."

It was not simple.

It was, in fact, the opposite of simple, and the reason it was the opposite of simple was currently audible from forty metres away in the form of the client's voice, which had taken on the specific quality of a man sharing information that had not been requested and was not welcome.

Crimson watched through the windscreen with the patience of someone watching weather.

The client gestured. The contact gestured back — less expansively, more purposefully, the gesture of a man recalibrating his assessment of a situation in real time. The other men in the loading bay shifted in the subtle way that men shift when a situation has changed category without anyone formally announcing it.

"There it is," Crimson said, to no one.

He was already out of the vehicle.

What happened next took approximately ninety seconds and would have been entirely avoidable if the client had not, apparently, mentioned the east approach.

Crimson didn't have the full picture and didn't need it. The full picture was the client's problem. His problem was the geometry — six visible plus two he'd clocked as soon as he was out of the vehicle, the exit options he'd already mapped, the client who was currently backing toward a wall with his hands up while three of the exchange party advanced with the purposeful energy of men who'd decided that the thing in the cases was staying with them and the client was a problem to be resolved.

He moved.

Not running. Running attracted attention and spent energy and committed you to a direction. He moved the way water moves — the path of least resistance toward the specific point where he needed to be, which was between the client and the three men, which he reached at the same moment the nearest one reached for the client's collar.

The next forty seconds were administrative.

He was aware, distantly, that this was probably the part of the job that justified his rates. He was also aware, with the mild internal editorial tone he'd developed for moments like this, that he was going to have to re-examine his rates because the current rates had been set before he'd fully understood what percentage of clients were on Klaxons and what percentage of those would mention the east approach.

Something that had once been a man's plan to grab the client connected instead with the arm, which was not the experience the man had intended.

The arm worked fine.

The vehicle was airborne for a period of time that Crimson's internal narrator logged as briefly but memorably while the actual operational part of his attention was occupied with the exchange of fire coming from the loading bay, the client in the passenger seat making sounds that weren't words, and the geometry of landing options available on a service road at this speed with this vehicle weight and this particular configuration of pursuing vehicles behind them.

I should have charged more, he thought, with genuine calm, while executing a manoeuvre that used all available road and some that wasn't road.

The vehicle came down. Found traction. Continued.

I said east.

Behind them the pursuing vehicle made a navigational decision that the service road didn't accommodate and became a different shape.

I said east specifically. I said east and I said let them talk first and I said come back to the vehicle.

The client said something that might have been a question.

The east approach was clear. I checked at seven this morning. I drove it. It was clear.

"—are we—" the client managed.

"Fine," Crimson said.

"That was—"

"Fine," he said again. "We're fine."

He drove. Behind them the service road was quiet. Ahead of them the city resumed its usual appearance of managed deterioration, indifferent to the last ninety seconds, which had not been documented and would not be.

The client's hands were moving again. Both of them. Independent.

Crimson looked at the road and said nothing and drove east, which had been clear, which he had checked, which he had said.

He dropped the client at the address. Waited while the client counted out payment with hands that still weren't quite steady, which took longer than it should have because Klaxons did something to fine motor control in the comedown phase that turned simple tasks into exercises in determination.

The payment was correct. He'd half expected it not to be.

"You're good at that," the client said, which was the closest thing to a debrief Crimson was going to get.

"East," Crimson said. "Next time."

He drove away before the client could respond.

He walked the last six blocks home because the vehicle needed to cool and because he'd learned, somewhere in the last few years, that six blocks of walking after a job was the closest thing he had to a ritual. Not processing exactly. More like the space between the job and whatever came after it. The city at this hour doing its evening shift change — the day people heading in, the night people heading out, the permanent residents of corners and doorways who didn't observe the distinction.

He was thinking about nothing in particular, which was the goal.

He almost walked past the alley.

Almost.

The sound was — specific. The kind of specific that his nervous system filed and responded to before the cognitive part caught up. He stopped. Looked.

Two men. One against the wall. The arithmetic was straightforward.

He watched for one second. Two. The people passing on the street maintained the careful peripheral blindness of people who had learned that involvement had costs and the calculation rarely worked out.

He stepped into the alley.

What happened after that was brief and quieter than the loading bay and considerably more final for one of the two men, who had not, in fairness, been given much warning.

The other one — young, scared, bleeding from the lip, currently processing the last thirty seconds with the expression of someone whose nervous system hadn't caught up yet — looked at him.

Crimson looked back.

"You're alright," he said. Simply. The way you state a fact.

He almost smiled. Not warmth exactly. Something adjacent to it. The specific expression of a man who had just done a thing his nervous system required and was at peace with the requirement even if he'd stopped examining the reasons.

"Be careful," he said. "Go home."

He walked out of the alley and back onto the street and continued the six blocks without breaking stride.

She'd been watching since the loading bay.

Not following — she hadn't been following him, that wasn't what this was. She'd been in the area, which was a thing that happened, which was different. And then she'd seen the vehicle and the plates and something in her chest had done the thing it did sometimes in this city when the grid was small enough that the same faces kept appearing in the same places and it didn't mean anything.

Except.

The arm.

She'd clocked it in the loading bay from a distance — custom work, unmistakeable, the kind of thing you didn't see unless someone had gone significantly out of their way to make it happen. And the way he moved. The geometry of it. The calculation that was already finished before other people had started theirs.

She'd told herself it was a coincidence of qualities. Lots of people moved like that. Lots of people had that specific economy of motion that came from having been in enough situations where waste was expensive.

Then the alley.

She stood at the mouth of it after he'd gone and looked at the shape of what had just happened — the speed of it, the lack of drama, the smile — and felt the thing in her chest do it again. Louder this time.

Not recognition.

Not yet.

Just a quality she'd filed away a long time ago in the place where things go that don't have a category yet.

Coming back.

She looked at the direction he'd gone.

Then she went home.

End of Chapter Two.

Chapter Three: Before the Meter Broke

The last normal day she could remember clearly was a Tuesday.

Not because anything significant happened on it. That was the point. It was ordinary in the specific way that days are ordinary when you don't know yet that ordinary is the thing worth keeping.

Her brother had eaten the last of the good rations without asking. She'd been furious about it for the better part of an afternoon — genuinely, completely furious, the kind that felt total and justified and world-ending in the small warm kitchen of their unit while he leaned in the doorway doing the almost-smile he did when he knew he'd gotten to her and was enjoying it more than he should.

She'd thrown a book at him.

He'd caught it. Of course he had. He had their father's reflexes and their mother's smile and he knew exactly how to deploy both when he'd done something he wasn't sorry for.

"You love me," he'd said.

"I really don't," she'd said.

She really had.

That was before. Before the word brother got complicated in her mouth. Before she had to decide every morning whether to keep looking or to finally let the looking mean something different than hope.

She didn't let it mean something different.

Not yet.

Present day Lyra moved through the market the way she moved through everything — efficiently, without waste, her attention distributed across the space in a way that looked casual and wasn't. She'd stopped being able to explain when that started. The raid was the obvious answer but she suspected it had always been there, just untriggered. Some people are built with wider peripheral vision and don't know it until something makes the knowing matter.

She stopped at a stall. Picked up a component she didn't need. Put it down.

"You buying or just keeping it warm?" The vendor. Older woman, not unkind.

Lyra smiled. The full one — bright, slightly exaggerated, the one that made people feel like they'd won something.

"Just looking," she said. "Love what you've done with the display though. Very~ professional."

The vendor laughed. Didn't know why exactly but didn't need to.

Lyra moved on.

The smile dissolved the moment she turned away. Not because it had been dishonest — that was the thing people never quite understood about her. It hadn't been dishonest. It had been real and performed simultaneously and she'd stopped trying to explain the difference because most people didn't have the right architecture to receive the explanation.

The information she'd gotten this morning sat in her chest like the component she hadn't bought.

This guy is probably the best you can get for what you have on you.

She'd asked three people. Three different sources, no connection to each other, which was how she verified anything worth verifying. All three had eventually arrived at the same name through different routes.

She didn't have much. She knew that. What she had was specific and the specificity was the value — information about her brother's crew, their movements, their current arrangement, the window that was closing faster than she wanted to think about directly.

She needed someone who didn't ask what was in the box.

Apparently there was a man for that.

Custom arm. Worked alone. Reasonable rates for unreasonable situations.

She'd been watching the city for three days and hadn't found him yet which meant either her sources were wrong or he was better at not being found than most.

She was starting to think it was the second one.

She saw the commotion before she heard it.

A knot of people at the alley mouth — not gathered, more like briefly interrupted, the specific pause of people who'd clocked something and were deciding whether to keep walking. Most were keeping walking. That was the city's natural response to alleys at this hour.

She didn't keep walking.

Not because it was her business. Because the pause in people's movement created a sightline and she followed it automatically, the way a chess player's eye follows forced lines without deciding to.

It was brief. She caught the tail end of it — the shape of the resolution rather than the thing itself. A man walking out of the alley and back onto the street with the unhurried momentum of someone returning from something administrative rather than something violent, which was a distinction most people couldn't maintain and he wore without apparent effort.

The arm caught the light.

Custom work. Unmistakeable.

Her sources hadn't mentioned that specific detail. Which meant either they didn't know or they'd considered it irrelevant, and she filed the gap either way because gaps were information.

She watched him walk.

The way he moved was — specific. Already done with the calculation. Other people's chaos not registering on whatever meter he was running on. She knew that quality. Recognised the shape of it from the inside, which was different from recognising it from observation.

Behind her someone said something she didn't process.

He was almost at the corner.

She didn't follow him.

She stood at the alley mouth and looked at the direction he'd gone and sat with the specific feeling of something slotting into place that she hadn't known was loose.

Not recognition.

Not yet.

Just a quality.

The same one she'd filed away a long time ago in the place where things go that don't have a category yet.

Coming back.

She looked at the alley. Looked at the corner he'd turned.

Hm.

Then she went to find her sources again and ask the question she hadn't thought to ask the first time.

The arm. Tell me about the arm.

The third person she asked laughed.

Not unkindly. Just the specific laugh of someone who'd been asked a question that struck them as optimistic.

"The arm," he repeated, like he was tasting the words. "You want to know about the arm."

"I want to know where it came from," Lyra said. "Who fitted it. When."

The man — stall vendor, components, mid-fifties, the kind of face that had stopped being surprised by things sometime in the previous decade — looked at her with the patient expression of someone about to explain something that should be obvious.

"Girl," he said, not unkindly, "this is Thresh."

He gestured at the market around them. The stalls. The factory-line everything. The grey functional sameness of a town that received what the allocation gave it and made do.

"Nobody here asks where custom work comes from," he said. "Because the answer's always the same."

"Which is?"

"None of my business."

He turned back to his stall.

Lyra stood there for a moment. Looked at the market. At the careful peripheral blindness of people who'd learned that curiosity had costs and the calculation rarely worked out.

"Right," she said. "Thanks."

She walked away and didn't let it show on her face.

Fourth person. A woman who ran what passed for a medical exchange two streets back from the main market. Lyra had picked her specifically — medical people noticed things about bodies that other people didn't, and they remembered what they noticed even when they pretended not to.

The woman listened to the question. Wiped her hands on a cloth. Didn't answer immediately which was itself an answer of a kind.

"Custom neural-linked arm," she said finally. "Full sensory integration. Military-grade articulation."

"You've seen it."

"I've seen him. Not the arm specifically. But you don't miss it."

"Who fitted it?"

The woman looked at her. The look of someone running a quiet calculation.

"Why?"

"I need to hire him," Lyra said. "I want to know what I'm hiring."

"You're hiring someone who gets people where they're going and brings them back," the woman said. "That's what you're hiring. The arm doesn't change that."

"I like complete information."

"Then you're in the wrong town." The woman went back to her work. "Thresh runs on incomplete information. It's the local currency."

She bought something she didn't need from a stall she'd already passed twice.

Stood at the edge of the market and looked at the town the way she looked at everything — the whole picture, what it was saying underneath what it appeared to be saying.

Thresh was not a town that asked questions. That wasn't apathy. That was a considered policy, collectively maintained, that had probably kept a significant number of its residents alive and untroubled by people who didn't want their questions answered.

Which meant the arm wasn't a mystery Thresh was protecting.

It was a mystery Thresh had simply agreed not to notice.

There was a difference.

She filed it.

Her brother's window was closing. She could feel it the way you feel weather changing — not a specific fact, just pressure shifting in a direction that wasn't good. The information she had was specific and it was valuable and it had a shelf life she couldn't calculate precisely but knew was shorter than she'd like.

She needed someone who didn't ask what was in the box.

Thresh had apparently decided collectively that this particular someone existed, was functional, and was none of their business.

That was almost a recommendation.

She finished the thing she'd bought — protein something, warm, unremarkable — and looked at the address she'd been given that morning.

Workshop. South end. You'll know it by the noise.

She started walking.

She heard it before she saw it.

Not loud. Just — specific. The sound of someone who knew exactly what they were doing and was doing it without particular urgency. Metal on metal. Something being tested, adjusted, tested again.

The workshop sat at the end of a service road like it had grown there — accumulated rather than built, the kind of structure that had been added to and subtracted from over years until it had achieved a kind of functional permanence that had nothing to do with planning and everything to do with persistence.

She stopped at the entrance.

Inside: components, equipment, things in various states of becoming or unbecoming. Half-built shapes she didn't have full context for. The organised chaos of someone who knew where everything was and didn't need it to look like they did.

And at the far end, back to her, working on something she couldn't identify from here —

The tattoos.

She recognised them before she recognised anything else about him.

The same ink. Same patterns. The back she'd seen through a gap in a door in the orange dark of a night that had permanently recalibrated her sense of what bad actually meant.

Older now. Different. The arm — the custom arm, military-grade, neural-linked, the mystery Thresh had collectively agreed not to notice — catching the workshop light on its left side.

He hadn't heard her.

She stood at the entrance to the workshop and looked at the back of the man she'd been looking for without knowing she was looking for him.

The stone-in-still-water feeling from a long time ago.

Coming back.

Same, something in her said, quietly and without asking permission. Still the same.

She didn't move.

Didn't announce herself.

Just stood there for a moment with the complete information she'd spent all day trying to find sitting in her chest doing something she didn't have a clean word for.

Then —

"Workshop's not a market," he said, without turning around. "You need something, say so."

"I need a courier," she said.

A pause. Metal on metal stopped.

"Couriers advertise in the market."

"I tried the market. They sent me here."

"Then your sources are misinformed."

"They weren't." She shifted her weight. Didn't step inside yet. Didn't step back either. "You're the one who doesn't ask what's in the box."

The silence that followed had a different quality than the silences in the stone room. Less survival. More — assessment.

He set down whatever he'd been holding. Turned around.

Lyra had prepared for this moment in the abstract, which was how she prepared for most things that mattered. She had a version of herself ready — the smile, the tilt, the tone that made people feel chosen and therefore cooperative. She'd used it on three sources and a medical exchange and a stall vendor who didn't need it but got it anyway because the habit was easier than deciding each time.

She didn't use it.

She was tired. She was holding information about her brother like a component she couldn't put down. Her hair had come half out of whatever she'd done with it this morning and she hadn't fixed it because fixing it would have been a kind of performance and she was too far past the market for that.

He looked at her.

And something happened to his face that she had not expected.

It wasn't recognition — or it wasn't only recognition. His mouth opened. Closed. The custom arm twitched at his side in a small involuntary adjustment, like a man reaching for a tool that wasn't there.

"You're—" he said.

"I'm hiring you," she said, because the pause was getting long and long pauses in Thresh were how people decided you were worth noticing for the wrong reasons.

He blinked. Once. The way you blink when your brain has queued three responses and none of them have reached the front yet.

"I don't—" He stopped. Started again, with visible effort toward the version of himself that handled situations like this. "I don't take walk-ins. You need to state the job. Destination. Timeline. Payment."

"Thresh south junction. My brother. Window's closing." She held his eyes because looking away would have been performance too and she was apparently committed to the disaster of being real in front of a stranger who might be the man from the orange night or might not be and she couldn't afford to ask directly yet. "Payment is everything I have, which isn't much, and information you probably want, which is more."

The arm twitched again.

He looked at her like she had rearranged the room while he wasn't watching.

"That's not a job brief," he said. "That's a feeling."

"It's a Tuesday," she said, and then realised what she'd said and almost laughed at herself, which would have been the wrong move, so she didn't. "I mean — it's specific. I can give you names. Movements. What crew he's running with. What happens if we don't move in the next forty-eight hours."

"And what happens if we do?"

She hadn't expected that question. Most people in Thresh didn't ask the second part.

"I don't know," she said. Honest. Flat. No smile to soften it. "Find him. Get him out if he's—" She made a small gesture that contained several bad outcomes without naming them. "I don't have a plan for after. I just know I can't stop looking."

Something crossed his face that might have been sympathy and might have been horror at his own sympathy and might have been both, badly filed.

"You should have a plan for after," he said.

"You sound like my brother."

The words left her mouth before she could decide whether they were too much. They hung in the workshop air between a half-built something and a rack of components and the organised chaos of a man who knew where everything was.

He stared at her.

"Right," he said. "Okay."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's—" He rubbed the back of his neck with his real hand, which was a thing she hadn't seen him do in any of the versions of him she'd been carrying — the kneeling man in the gravel, the courier in the loading bay, the shape in the alley. This was new. Unguarded. Slightly annoyed at being unguarded. "It's a preliminary assessment."

Lyra felt something loosen in her chest that she immediately distrusted.

"People usually lead with the preliminary assessment when they're about to say no."

"People usually lead with a plan."

"I told you. I don't have one."

He looked at her again — longer this time, which was a problem, because she'd spent years telling herself that long looks were a technique. Inventory. Appraisal. The prelude to someone deciding what they wanted from you and how cheaply they could get it.

This didn't feel like that.

It felt like he was trying to read a document written in a language he almost spoke.

"You're not—" he said, and stopped.

"I'm not what?"

"Nothing." Too fast. "You're not from the south end."

"North block. Before the east—" She cut herself off. The raid sat between that sentence and its end like a door she wasn't opening yet. "Does it matter?"

"No." Yes. He cleared his throat. "Payment first. Then brief. Then I decide."

"Reasonable."

"East," he said, apparently to himself.

"What?"

"Nothing. Habit." He moved to a bench, found a cloth, wiped his hands with the focused precision of a man who needed something to do with his body while his brain caught up. "You're sure you want me."

"I asked three people."

"You followed me from the loading bay."

She went still.

He didn't look up from the cloth. "I didn't turn around. I didn't need to. You were at the alley mouth after. Same quality of attention. Same—" He paused. "You don't follow quietly. You follow completely."

"I wasn't—"

"You were." He folded the cloth. Set it down. Looked at her finally with something like professional irritation aimed at himself. "Which is either very good or very bad and I haven't decided which."

Lyra's heart was doing something she classified as inconvenient.

"Is that a yes?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. The muscle in his jaw did a thing she filed under *this man is not having the day he planned*.

"I said reasonable rates," he said. "Not stupid ones."

"So that's a yes."

"That's a—" He exhaled through his nose. "That's a conversation. Sit down. Start with names."

She sat on the edge of the nearest surface that looked like it could hold a person without collapsing into a project. Didn't cross her legs. Didn't perform posture. Just sat, with the component-information about her brother still sitting in her chest like a weight she was used to carrying.

He stayed standing. Which was probably better. Distance seemed important to whatever was happening to him.

She gave him names.

He listened the way he'd listened to the client on Klaxons — adaptive, apparently passive, actually processing — except his hands were still and his eyes kept doing the thing where they tried to stick to her face and then remembered they had a job and left and then came back.

She watched him watch her and thought: *oh.*

Not *oh, he's interested* — she'd had that thought before, hundreds of times, and had trained herself to translate it into *oh, he wants something* because that translation kept you alive in a town that ran on incomplete information.

This was different.

This was *oh, he looks like I feel when the market smile won't go on right.*

Confused. Exposed. A little angry about it.

"You've done this before," he said when she'd finished. "The asking. The verifying. The three sources."

"I'm thorough."

"You're—" He stopped again. She was beginning to recognise the pattern. "You're good at this."

The compliment landed wrong — not because it was false but because she didn't know what to do with it when it wasn't wrapped in flirtation or leverage. She opened her mouth. Closed it.

"Thanks?" she said, which was the wrong word and they both knew it.

He looked away first. Which was interesting. She'd made men look away on purpose for years. She'd never made one look away by accident.

"Forty-eight hours," he said. "We move at first light. You don't lie to me about what's in the box. I don't ask questions I don't need answered."

"And after?"

The pause was shorter this time. Not because he had an answer. Because he'd already accepted he didn't.

"We'll figure it out," he said, in the tone of a man who hated that sentence and was saying it anyway.

Lyra stood. Extended her hand before she could decide whether that was performance.

He looked at it.

Looked at her.

Shook it — firm, brief, the custom arm careful with pressure in a way that suggested he was thinking very hard about not breaking her fingers or not thinking about her fingers at all, she couldn't tell which.

"Lyra," she said, because she'd realised she hadn't given her name and names mattered when you were asking someone to walk into a closing window with you.

"Crimson," he said.

Her hand was still in his for half a second longer than the handshake required.

She knew that name.

Not from the market. Not from the sources. From the orange dark. From the gap in the door. From a man who'd turned away from a leader's question and found her eye and said *nah* like saving her was the easiest decision he'd ever made and the wink like it was nothing.

Her face must have done something.

His did too — a flicker, quickly managed, the expression of a man who'd just watched someone connect dots he hadn't meant to lay out yet.

"Right," he said. "First light."

"First light," she agreed.

She left before the silence could become a question neither of them was ready to ask.

Behind her, the workshop resumed its specific sounds — metal on metal, test and adjust, test again — but she noticed, without turning back, that the rhythm was off for a long time after.

Outside, the sky was doing its evening thing. Thresh grey. Factory-line everything. The ordinary managed deterioration of a town that didn't ask questions.

Lyra walked home with her hands in her pockets and the stone-in-still-water feeling back in her chest, louder now.

Not recognition.

Not yet.

But the quality she'd filed away — the one that had no category — had a name now.

Crimson.

And the way he'd looked at her when she wasn't performing had done something no market smile had ever managed.

It had made her wonder if she was wrong about herself.

Which was terrifying.

Which was, she suspected as she turned the corner toward the north block, possibly the beginning of something else entirely.

End of Chapter Three.
