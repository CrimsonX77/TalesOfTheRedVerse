# RED SHIFT: Basilisk's Anomaly
## Chapter Two — *Interference Pattern*

---

The rooftop was flat and tar-sealed and smelled of old rain and the particular mineral sharpness that preceded a rift bloom. Sable had been standing on it for twenty-three minutes. She knew the exact duration because she tracked time the way she tracked everything — automatically, precisely, without any recourse to feeling.

Below, two streets over, a light was on in the ruined garage.

She wasn't watching it. She had assessed the sight lines from this position, catalogued all relevant visual information, and was now looking at the horizon where the red shelf of cloud sat unmoving above the flatlands. She was looking at the horizon. Not at the amber glow through the corrugated gap that hadn't gone dark once in twenty-three minutes.

The post-contract report composed itself in the part of her mind set aside for administrative functions.

*Contract 7-Thresh-Anomalous. Target: Voss, Kael. Classification: Persistent Thermal Anomaly, Grade Three. Status: —*

She stopped composing.

The status field required a value. She had four available to her. *Eliminated. Pending. Compromised. Withdrawn.* The first was inapplicable for reasons she was still in the process of filing correctly. The second implied a tactical delay with a clear forward path. The third was for contracts affected by external interference. The fourth was for contracts she had chosen not to complete, which in thirty-one years of service had a frequency of exactly zero.

She left the status field blank.

The wind came in from the flatlands and moved her hair — the living strands corrected automatically, resettling. She registered the cold as data. Temperature: viable. Conditions: unchanged. Her scales, which had been running at an elevated temperature since she'd left the garage, were still not back to their baseline. She ran a diagnostic, the same one she'd already run four times since the rooftop edge.

Result: no physiological cause identified.

Which was its own kind of result.

She replayed the sequence with the clinical detachment she had spent three decades cultivating. The entry had been clean. The strike placement had been correct. The target had responded with improvised technique — untrained but instinctive, the kind of body-knowledge that accrued from surviving a long sequence of situations he should not have survived. She'd recalibrated in real time. Pursued. Cornered.

And then the tattoos had ignited.

She'd seen thermal anomaly expressions before. Had eliminated seven Grade Threes in the past two years alone. The heat was simply a variable — manageable, factored in, irrelevant to outcome. She had operated in rift zones hot enough to boil the moisture from the air. She had completed a contract in the Vael Basin during a storm event that melted the soles of lesser operatives' boots.

She was not susceptible to heat.

She ran the diagnostic again.

Result: no physiological cause identified.

The flush had been —

She stopped that thought before it completed and restarted it with better vocabulary.

The thermal radiation emitted by the target had produced a *response* in her scale pigmentation. Involuntary vasodilation of the chromatophore layer. A known sensitivity to sustained high-temperature proximity, documented in her intake file at the Conclave, never before triggered in field conditions. A biological quirk. An edge case. Entirely consistent with her physiology and entirely irrelevant to her function.

The fact that it had happened while she was on top of him with a blade at his throat was simply a matter of unfortunate timing.

She had disengaged. Correctly. Re-establishing distance had been the tactically sound choice — the chromatophore response created a visibility issue, a loss of operational composure, a risk of compromised target assessment. She had identified the risk and mitigated it.

*This changes nothing,* she had said.

Accurate. Factually correct. The contract remained. The target remained. Her directive remained unchanged.

She looked at the horizon.

She was not looking at the amber light.

---

In thirty-one years she had eliminated four hundred and twelve anomalies.

The Conclave did not use the word *killed.* Killed implied an equivalence between the operative and the target that the doctrine did not support. Anomalies were disruptions — tears in the membrane of a world already coming apart at the seams, wearing human shapes and breathing borrowed air and accelerating the decay simply by existing. Eliminating them was maintenance. It was the thing that had kept Thresh and the hundred towns like it from following the eastern settlements into the red nothing for as long as they had.

She believed this.

She had believed it for thirty-one years with a completeness that had never required examination.

She believed it now.

Below, the amber light moved — a shift in position, the shadow of him crossing in front of it. She tracked the movement automatically and then stopped tracking it because she was looking at the horizon.

He'd known the blade was coming before she struck. She'd noted that. Not a lucky read — he'd settled into a different quality of stillness when she arrived, the kind that came from whatever the combat-experienced part of a human brain did when it received a threat signal and chose to wait rather than run. He'd been calm. Not artificially calm, not the blank performance of someone suppressing fear. Actually calm. Assessing.

He'd asked her what he'd done wrong.

She'd declined to answer, which had been correct operational practice, but the question itself sat in a part of her filing system that didn't seem to want to process it. She didn't have a clean answer. The contract had been issued with the standard documentation — anomaly presence in Thresh, Grade Three thermal classification, potential rift-catalysing effect on the local membrane if left unaddressed. She had read it on the journey south and found it sufficient. She had found all four hundred and twelve contracts sufficient at the time of reading.

*Most days,* he'd said, when she said his name.

She'd noted the answer as evasion. She was revising that assessment now, with the benefit of twenty-three minutes of horizon examination. It hadn't been evasion. It had been the answer. Something in it that was self-aware without being self-pitying, honest without asking for anything in return.

She became aware that she was constructing a psychological profile of a target she should have eliminated forty minutes ago and stopped.

The contract field still read: *Status: —*

She would return tomorrow. She would account for the thermal variable. She would complete the contract and file the report and move to the next assignment, which was a Grade Two anomaly in a transit camp three days east and posed no particular complications.

The amber light went out.

For a moment she tracked the darkened gap in the corrugated wall with the same automatic attention she'd been not-paying it all along. Then: the glow returned, lower, more orange. He'd moved the light source. A different lamp, or the same one repositioned. The shadow of him settled back into stillness.

Still working.

She ran the diagnostic one more time.

*Result: no physiological cause identified.*

She held her hand up in the dark and looked at the faint iridescent shimmer of her palm, the scale pattern that most people never saw because she never let them close enough to see it. It was dim now. Baseline. The flush had gone.

The absence of it felt strange in a way she had no entry for.

She filed the feeling under *anomaly interference — residual* and closed the field and looked back at the horizon, where the clouds were doing what they always did at the edge of the world: stacking, slowly, in shades of red that had no names yet because the world was still working out what it was becoming.

She would return tomorrow.

She descended the far side of the building into the dark and didn't look back.

---

The diagnostic was wrong.

She knew this. Some sub-process that ran below her administrative function and had no official standing had known it since the fourth time she'd run the scan. The chromatophore response wasn't a documented sensitivity. She had been in hotter conditions a hundred times, in closer contact with thermal anomalies twice, without so much as a flicker.

It was him. Specifically. The combination of his particular heat signature and his particular face at that particular proximity doing something to her particular physiology that she had no prior data on and no approved framework for analysing.

She filed this under *anomaly interference — residual* as well, and walked south into Thresh's crooked dark, and told herself it was sufficient.

It wasn't sufficient.

She kept walking anyway.

---

*End of Chapter Two*
