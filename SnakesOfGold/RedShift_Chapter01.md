# RED SHIFT: Basilisk's Anomaly
## Chapter One — *Cold Scales, Hot Metal*

---

The scrambler was lying again.

Kael knew the sound by now — that wet stutter between the third and fourth pulse, like a man trying to remember his own name after a rift crossing. He pulled the casing off with two fingers and held the device up to the light leaking through the workshop's single window. Outside, the sky above Thresh was the colour it always was these days: a deep arterial red, the clouds stacked like bruises over the flatlands, barely moving. Wind came through the gap in the corrugated wall and smelled of ozone and burnt copper, same as yesterday, same as the week before.

He set the scrambler on the workbench and picked up the smallest probe.

The workshop was barely a workshop. Back half of a collapsed transit garage, shored up with reclaimed struts and two walls of stacked salvage that had slowly become load-bearing. A folding cot in the corner he slept on maybe four nights in seven. A fire-blackened kettle on a portable coil. The workbench itself was the one good thing — deep-grained hardwood from somewhere north that had given up existing, bolted to the wall with the kind of stubbornness that outlasted the owner. Tools hung above it in rows, each one in its place. The only clean thing in the room.

He worked shirtless. Had for years. The heat came whether he wanted it or not — a low, constant radiation that put sweat on anyone else in the room within minutes and made lighter fabrics a genuine fire risk after the third incident. These days he kept the cot blanket for the two-hour windows before dawn when Thresh's edge-of-the-world cold pushed through the gaps in the walls and even his skin couldn't argue with it.

The tattoos moved in the low light. Not much. A lazy drift of the glyphs along his ribs, the slow circulation of something like a tide through the larger sigils across his shoulder blades. When he was calm they were dim. Amber, almost. The colour of a coal banked for the night.

He'd been calm for six weeks now. That was the thing about Thresh — it was easy to be calm at the end of everything. Nothing came here except people running out of road.

The scrambler's fourth pulse node had partially fused. He exhaled through his nose and reached for the separation tool.

He didn't hear her come in.

He knew she was there the same way he always knew — the way the air changed, a specific drop in ambient temperature that had nothing to do with the gaps in the corrugated wall. The hairs on the back of his neck. Something in the base of his skull that had been punched in and rewired by too many years doing work that carried a price, and had learned, stubbornly, to be useful.

He didn't move.

"Back counter," he said. "Second shelf. Got a tin of decent tea, two cups that aren't cracked, and whatever's in the flask — don't touch the flask, it's medical. I don't have credits on the premises and anything worth stealing I've already stripped out."

Silence.

Not the silence of hesitation. The silence of someone who had never intended to be heard and was recalibrating.

*Ah,* he thought. *That kind.*

He was already moving when the blade came — dropping off the stool, spinning left, getting his forearm up between his throat and the edge. It clipped him. Cold metal, and her grip was something else, precise as a machine, no wasted force in the strike. He caught her wrist on the redirect and she was already pulling free, light as water, repositioning.

He got his first look at her.

Tall. He clocked that first because it mattered for distance — she had reach on him. Midnight-black hair, dead straight, and even in the half-dark of the workshop he could see the way certain strands moved wrong, slow and independent, like they were thinking. Pale. Pale in a way that wasn't from cold or hunger but from something deeper and stranger — an undertone to her skin that shimmered faintly when she shifted, almost iridescent. Her eyes caught the light and he couldn't read the colour, only that they were bright and entirely without warmth.

She was wearing a huntress rig — close-cut, functional, not from anywhere near Thresh. Too well-made. Too deliberate.

She looked at him the way he looked at the scrambler. Assessing. Identifying the faulty node.

"You're Kael Voss," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Most days." He was still holding the probe. He didn't put it down. "You want to tell me what I've done, or is this one of those professional discretion situations?"

She struck again.

He'd been hoping for the talking option. He went over the workbench backwards, caught the edge with one hand, used the momentum to bring his feet up and kick the whole thing into her path. Tools scattered. The scrambler hit the floor and the fourth pulse node finally gave up entirely. She vaulted it, blade first, and he was already at the far wall with his back to the salvage stack, running the numbers.

Fast. Trained. The precision was divine-order precision — he'd seen it twice before and both times it had cost him something he didn't get back.

He grabbed the nearest thing off the stack — a length of copper pipe, heavy, two feet — and got it between them as she closed the distance.

The impact rang up his arms. She was *strong.* The next strike he barely got under, and the one after that opened a line across his ribs, shallow but immediate, and the tattoos on his chest flared without his permission — a sudden bloom of hot amber-red that lit the whole workshop like a lamp had been struck.

She stopped.

Just for a half-second. Her gaze went to his chest, to the burning glyphs, and something crossed her face that wasn't part of the operational expression she'd come in wearing.

He used the half-second. Shoved sideways, hooked his foot behind her heel, went for the throw —

It almost worked.

She caught her balance, grabbed his arm on the way down, and they went into the shelving together. The impact brought down the second shelf — tools, tin cups, the not-cracked kettle, and the flask, which bounced once and survived, because some things were made to be difficult. He was on his back. She was on top of him, knee to his sternum, blade tip at the hollow of his throat.

The tattoos were fully lit now. Every glyph, the whole sprawl of them across his chest and ribs and up over his shoulders, blazing molten amber-gold in the dark. The heat radiated off him in a visible shimmer.

She was very close.

Whatever precision she'd come in with was still there, technically — the blade was steady, her weight was correctly distributed, she'd done everything right. But her face was doing something she clearly had no policy for. The faint iridescent shimmer of her skin had deepened. Along her collarbones, along the line of her jaw, the faint pattern that he'd barely clocked in the fight had darkened to a vivid, unmistakable crimson. Like a blush taken to its logical extreme. Like her own body had voted against her.

The scales.

He'd seen scale-flush before. Once. He knew what it meant.

He said nothing. He was pinned and bleeding and genuinely had nothing useful to contribute.

She became aware of it approximately half a second after he did. He could tell — watched the moment arrive on her face with the same expression a calculating machine might wear if it produced an output that contradicted its own axioms. Her eyes dropped to his chest, to the blazing tattoos not two inches from her sternum, and then back to his face.

The blade didn't waver. Everything else about her did.

"You're radiating," she said. The tone was flat and clinical but there was something working hard to keep it that way.

"I know. I can't help it. My apologies."

"It's—" She stopped. Started again, clipped. "It interferes."

"With what?"

The look she gave him for that could have stripped paint.

He became aware that her free hand had found the floor next to his head, braced there, and that she was very pointedly not moving. The crimson flush along her jaw was deepening. In the light of his tattoos he could see the texture of the scale pattern now — fine and precise and not quite human and genuinely, surprisingly beautiful in the way that a lot of dangerous things were. She smelled like something cool. Like deep water and metal and something he couldn't name that was neither pleasant nor unpleasant and entirely unforgettable.

"I should finish this," she said.

"Technically yes," he agreed. "Though it does seem like you've had a couple of opportunities."

The look again. Sharper this time.

"I'm just observing," he said. "Not complaining."

She stood up. Fast, clean, no wasted motion — all the precision back in place, blade already gone to wherever it had come from. She didn't look at him while she did it. She looked at a fixed point on the far wall with the focused intensity of someone performing a complicated calculation that kept not resolving.

Kael sat up slowly. His ribs were letting him know about the gash. He didn't put his hand to it. He picked up the probe from where it had landed by his knee and turned it over in his fingers, something to do with his hands.

She walked to the gap in the corrugated wall where the door had been before the door gave up. Stopped there. Didn't turn around.

"This changes nothing," she said.

He looked at the probe. Looked at the mess. Looked at the scrambler on the floor with its fourth pulse node finally, definitively dead.

"Didn't say it did," he said.

She left.

The cold came back into the room in her wake, that specific cold that had nothing to do with Thresh's edge-of-the-world wind, and then it faded too, and it was just the workshop — wrecked, dim, smelling of ozone and copper and his own blood — and the red sky outside doing what it always did, bleeding slowly from one shade of arterial to the next.

He looked at the scrambler for a long moment.

Then he picked it up and got back to work.

---

*End of Chapter One*
