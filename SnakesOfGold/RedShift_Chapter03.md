# RED SHIFT: Basilisk's Anomaly
## Chapter Three — *Collateral*

---

The Thresh market ran every Seventhday in the square behind the transit depot, which had stopped being a transit depot three years ago when the last functioning crawler came through and didn't come back. Now the depot building was a cold storage facility for the Murano family, who grew root vegetables in hydroponic racks in their basement and charged accordingly, and the square behind it was twenty-odd stalls of everything Thresh needed to keep not-dying for another week.

It wasn't much of a market. It was exactly enough of one.

Kael moved through it the way he moved through most things — at the pace of someone with nowhere urgent to be who had also never in his life been surprised by anything enjoyable. He had a canvas carry-bag over one shoulder. He stopped at the Derev stall and examined a row of hand-sealed preserves without touching them, decided on the third jar of something dark red, and paid with a folded note that Derev took without counting.

"Scrambler parts," Kael said.

Derev, who was not primarily a scrambler-parts vendor, pulled a small wrapped package from under the counter without comment and added it to the transaction. He also didn't count that.

The square smelled of frying oil, damp canvas, the particular scorched-metal undertone that Thresh's air had carried since the Halveth rift opened two years back. The red shelf of cloud sat on the horizon same as always. In the square, thirty or forty people moved between stalls in ones and twos — not hurrying, not lingering, the specific unhurried efficiency of people who had edited everything non-essential out of their routines. A pair of children chased each other around the base of the old depot sign, making the noise children made when they'd temporarily forgotten about everything.

Kael bought a paper-wrapped parcel of fried flatbread from the next stall, tore a piece off, and ate it while he walked.

She appeared on his left somewhere between the Murano vegetable stand and the woman who sold reclaimed cable by the metre.

She was wearing different clothes — still the close, functional cut, but darker, a hood up, something approximating an attempt at not standing out. She stood out. He didn't say that.

"You're going to want to keep walking," she said quietly. "Don't stop."

"I'm at the market," he said. He didn't stop. He also didn't accelerate.

"I'm aware."

"Then you know I'm going to stop at the Yeva stall because she gets a spice delivery on Seventhday and I'm out of everything."

Silence for two strides. She kept pace with him with the effortless, unhurried precision of someone who had followed targets through worse terrain than a provincial farmers market.

"I came to tell you," she said, "that yesterday was a postponement. Not a withdrawal."

"I know."

"You don't seem concerned."

"I'm getting spices." He angled toward the Yeva stall, which was a folding table covered in small labelled jars tended by a woman in her sixties who nodded at him and began sorting through her stock with the familiarity of a standing arrangement. He picked up a jar, checked the label. Set it down. Picked up another.

Sable stopped beside him. He was aware that she was performing the mental calculus — the angle, the proximity, the crowd, the thirty-odd people who were not paying attention to either of them and would continue not paying attention until something changed the ambient texture of the situation. He let her run it.

"You're using them," she said. Her voice had dropped further. Not anger. Something more precise than anger.

"I'm at the market," he said again.

"As a deterrent."

He put the second jar in the bag. Picked up a third. "I come here every Seventhday. That's just what Seventhday looks like."

"You knew I'd come."

He paid Yeva, who tucked the notes away without making eye contact with either of them, which was the correct Thresh response to standing near something that felt like it might escalate.

He moved away from the stall. She moved with him. He ate another piece of flatbread.

"Here's the thing," he said, in the tone he used to explain a mechanical problem to a client who didn't want to hear it. "A threat's got a particular function. It's supposed to make someone afraid of what you could do. If you wanted to make me afraid, there were better places. My workshop. Any of the four routes I take between my workshop and here. The underpass at the depot end — nobody goes through there since Halveth, good sight lines, three exits." He glanced at her sideways. "You know all of that. You cased the town before you came to the garage."

Her jaw was doing something. He filed it away.

"So you didn't come here to use them," he said. "Which means you came here knowing you wouldn't. Which means you picked the one location where your hands are tied and then came anyway." He stopped at the edge of the square, where a low wall separated the market from the old crawler road. He leaned against it. "That's not a threat. That's a visit."

The look she gave him was extraordinary. It contained several things he could identify and at least two he couldn't, all of them carefully not touching the surface.

"You're suggesting," she said, "that I have some kind of — consideration. For bystanders."

"I'm not suggesting anything. I'm reading the location you picked."

"I could simply be avoiding the administrative complications of a mass event."

"You could be."

She held his gaze. He held hers. The market went on around them — the children were still doing their circuit of the depot sign, someone's cart wheel needed attention, the smell of frying oil had shifted direction with the wind.

"You're very calm for someone who should be afraid of me," she said.

"I was afraid of you," he said. "Last night. In the workshop, when you had the blade at my throat — yeah, that registered." He turned the canvas bag in his hands. "You didn't finish it. So today I'm at the market."

Something went across her face. Too quick. He caught the edge of it and not the centre.

She looked at the crowd. He watched her look at it — actually look at it, not the way she'd arrived looking like she was calculating exit vectors, but something slower. The children were laughing about something, one of them had picked up a scrap of canvas and was wearing it as a cape. An older man was arguing cheerfully with the cable vendor about a spool that was clearly shorter than advertised. A young woman was balancing three bags and a sleeping infant and making it look like she'd been doing it since birth.

Thresh. Just Thresh. The end of the road, keeping itself going by communal stubbornness and a farmers market every Seventhday.

"They'd have no warning," she said quietly. Not a threat. Not exactly.

"No," he agreed. "They wouldn't."

"That doesn't concern you? Using them this way?"

He looked at her. "I'm not using them as a shield. If you wanted to kill thirty civilians you'd have done it before you walked up to me, and you wouldn't have walked up to me first. You picked a market because you wanted to see me and needed a reason you could file away somewhere." He shrugged. "I'm not judging it."

Her expression did the thing again — that rapid internal movement that her face was not supposed to permit. He was starting to read the shape of it, if not the content.

She looked at the flatbread in his hand. 

He held it out.

The pause lasted long enough that he thought she'd simply leave. Then she took it — two fingers, precise, like handling evidence — and stood there holding it.

"This doesn't mean anything," she said.

"It's flatbread."

She took a small, careful bite. He looked back at the square.

They stood at the wall in something that wasn't quite silence because the market was still making its market sounds all around them. After a moment he ate the last of his piece. She finished hers with the same careful precision she appeared to apply to everything and did not comment on it.

"You'll want to know," she said finally, "that I have a reporting window. I'm expected to file by Tenthday."

He looked at her.

"I'm telling you," she said, in a voice that had been drained of inflection so completely it had become its own kind of inflection, "so that you can make whatever arrangements you feel are relevant."

He was quiet for a moment. "That's an interesting thing to tell me."

"I'm aware."

"Why are you telling me?"

Another pause. She looked at the horizon — the red shelf of cloud, the flatlands, the colour the sky made at the edge of the world when it thought no one was watching.

"Administrative courtesy," she said.

He nodded slowly. "Right."

"Tenthday," she said.

"I heard you."

She pulled the hood forward and stepped back into the flow of the market crowd, and the people around her made room without knowing why, the way people always made instinctive space for something their hindbrain recognised as a different order of thing. He watched her go until she was through the gap between the Derev stall and the cable woman and gone.

He stood at the wall for another moment.

*Tenthday.* Two days. She'd given him two days and called it administrative courtesy.

He picked up his bag, adjusted the strap, and headed back through the square toward the workshop. He stopped at the Murano stand on the way and bought another jar of the dark red preserve without looking at it, because Yeva's spices were good but he'd also run out of that, and he had between now and Tenthday to decide what to do about a divine assassin who was apparently going to give him a running count of her own deadlines.

The children were still running around the depot sign.

He didn't look at them on the way past. He kept walking.

*End of Chapter Three*
